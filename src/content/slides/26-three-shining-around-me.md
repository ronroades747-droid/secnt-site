Most of us were handed an order without noticing it was an order. First you secure that God is one — that is the foundation. Then you bring in the persons, and you explain, as carefully as you can, how there can be three of them without the one coming apart. **On that arrangement the persons arrive as a complication**, and the three-in-one question is a question about a join you built yourself. **Now hear the sentence John Calvin reaches for at John 1:1.** Calvin is explaining what the early church meant by the word *persons*, and to say what that is like he quotes Gregory Nazianzen: **"I cannot think of the One (God) without having the Three (Persons) shining around me."** *(The parentheses are the translator's, in the 1847 English; Calvin cites the sentence in this one-directional form only at this place in his commentary.)* **Read the order in it.** Nazianzen is not holding the one God and then remembering there are three. He is saying **there is no moment when the One is standing there by himself, waiting** — think of God at all, and they are already shining around him. **And the verse itself works that way.** *In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God:* it never names God without the Word who is with him. You are not handed a bare One and then given a second thing to fit in beside it. Our research concludes that the order the hearer keeps is the thing this sentence overturns, and that nothing he believes has to be given up to let it go. **The persons were never a complication laid on top of the oneness. They are how the one God is reached at all.**

*For the preacher, the shape of it in three steps.* First, **name the order out loud** — secure the one, then add the three — because a congregation cannot put down a habit it has not noticed it has. Second, **hand over the sentence**, and let it be someone else's: it is a fourth-century bishop's, quoted by a sixteenth-century commentator, and its age is part of what it does. Third, **say what changes** — not the doctrine, the sequence — and stop there.

*What this page does not do, said plainly.* It does not argue the two errors the doctrine guards, or name them. It does not distinguish the communion God is from a communion God enters, or ground the sending in the distinction inside God. It does not develop what makes the persons distinct, or take up the vocabulary the early church coined for them. It does not go to the councils or the creed, and it does not exegete the preposition in *with God*. **It hands over one sentence and the order it reverses, and stops there.**

## Pedagogical task

This slide's job is to **let a preacher deny an order from the front without appearing to deny the doctrine**. The restraint is that you must not turn it into a lecture on the Trinity — the slide is not the doctrine in miniature, and a congregation that leaves having learned three technical words has been given the wrong thing. Project it and read it in its own order: the denial, then the reason, then stop on the gold line and stay there. The reason line is the one to point at, because it is the one your hearers can check in the verse in front of them. From the pulpit the whole move is one sentence: *you were taught to secure the one God and then fit the persons in, and the sentence Calvin quotes has no such moment in it.* **What not to do beyond that:** do not use it to make anyone feel foolish for having kept the order — nearly everyone does, and the sentence lands as a gift only if it is not handed over as a correction. And do not let the quotation become a curiosity: it is not on the screen precisely so that you say it aloud, and it should be the slowest thing you say. **Two downloads, two jobs.** The **wide slide carries the whole move** — the denial, the reason and the replacement — and it is the one to project while you teach from it. The **tall slide carries the denial and the replacement without the reason**, which makes it the one for a bulletin, a newsletter column or an invitation: it says the surprising thing and sends the reader to find out why.
