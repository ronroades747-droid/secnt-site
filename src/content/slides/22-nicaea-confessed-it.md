You have probably heard the objection, and you may have made it: Jesus never claimed to be God, and the church made him God centuries later — at Nicaea, under Constantine, by a vote. It is a serious objection, and it expects an answer from church history. **The Gospel of John answers it from inside itself, and from both ends at once.** Its first sentence, before a single miracle or a single sermon: *In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God* — in John's Greek, *θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος*. And its last scene, in the twentieth chapter: the risen Jesus standing in front of Thomas, and Thomas, with the wounds in front of him, saying to him, *my Lord and my God*. The book opens with the claim and closes with a man saying it to Jesus's face, and both were written centuries before any council met. **Nicaea confessed that. It did not concoct it.** Our commentary reads the Gospel as an arch: the first verse and Thomas's cry are the two stones it springs from, everything in between is held up by them, and take either one away and the arch comes down.

*What this page does not do, said plainly.* It does not read Thomas's words — what they claim, how they are put, or what Jesus says next. It records that they are said, in that book, to that face, which is all the argument needs; the confession itself belongs to the commentary on the twentieth chapter, which lies ahead. And it does not argue *how* the first verse says the Word was God — which readings John's grammar refuses, and how the Word can be God and yet be *with* God. Those are the work of the commentary on the clause itself, and they are settled there; this page takes the first verse as it stands and puts it beside the last.

## Pedagogical task

This slide's job is to **move the argument off the ground the objection chose** — and the restraint is that you should not follow the objection back onto it. The hearer who says *Nicaea invented it* is expecting a conversation about the fourth century, and the worst thing you can do is have one: the moment you are defending Constantine, or explaining what the council decided, you have conceded that the question is about the council. Put the slide up and read the two lines off it — the first verse of the Gospel, and Thomas's words at the end of it — and say the one sentence: **the book had already said it, front and back, before any council met.** From the pulpit that is the Lecture's own shape: begin at the end, with Thomas, and walk it back to the beginning. In a class, or across a coffee table, it is a book handed over rather than a case made. **What not to do:** do not explain Thomas's words. The moment you start on what *my God* means in his mouth you are interpreting a verse this slide only cites, and a skeptic will rightly ask you to justify the reading; what you need is only that the words are there. And do not climb into the grammar of verse one — what the Greek rules out, how the Word can be God and yet be with God. Those are true and they are elsewhere; here they are a distraction from the one fact the frame is built on. Two verses, one book, no council: that is the whole move.
