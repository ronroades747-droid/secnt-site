You have read John 1:1 more times than you can count, and the odds are you have never counted the one word in it that repeats. *In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God. This one was in the beginning with God.* Four times, in the space of two verses — and in the Greek every one of them is the same word, ἦν, the tense that marks no beginning. The reason nobody counts it is that repetition in an old text reads as style: the way a sentence happened to come out, the rhythm a writer falls into, and *was* is the dullest verb in the sentence, so it slides past unread. The count is what breaks that. Notice what is *not* in those two verses: nothing happens in them. There is no event, no act, no arrival — one small verb for being, struck four times over, before the evangelist has said a single thing about what this Word did. Our research reads that density as designed rather than incidental, and it is not a novel reading: the fourfold ἦν of verses 1–2 has been registered in the reference grammars for decades as deliberate. What the count gives a congregation is the evidence in their own hands, in the most familiar sentence in the Bible, in a word so plain it disappears — which is exactly why it is worth stopping on. The Prologue is not built the way most openings are built, and the first thing John does is establish, four times over, that the Word simply was.

## Pedagogical task

This slide's job is to make a count possible, not to teach a tense. Project it at verse 1 and do the one thing it licenses: read the two verses aloud, slowly, and have your people count the word with you — out loud, on their fingers. Four. The line breaks on the slide do the work for you: one clause per line, one *was* per line, four lines. Then say what the count is doing rather than what the tense means: John spends his opening on one small verb before he tells you anything that happens. The restraint is the whole discipline here. Do not explain the imperfect from the front, do not put the Greek on the screen, and do not reach for the contrast with the verbs that come later — the count is complete in itself and stops being a discovery the moment it becomes a lesson. Your congregation will feel it before you finish counting; let that be the end of the move. In a classroom the same two verses open the question the lecture takes up at length, and the link is on this page.
