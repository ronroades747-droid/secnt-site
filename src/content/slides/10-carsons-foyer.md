Most readers treat John 1:1–18 as an introduction — eighteen lofty verses the Gospel moves past on its way to the story. D. A. Carson has a better picture. The Prologue, he writes, is "a foyer to the rest of the Fourth Gospel" — an entrance hall that draws the reader in and sounds the Gospel's great themes before the rooms open. Our research takes the image at full architectural force, because a foyer is not a preface. A preface comes before, and is left behind; nobody rereads the preface. A foyer frames — you enter the house through it, and the house answers back to it. That is what John built: the Word, the life, the light are sounded in the Prologue, and the whole Gospel answers them. To introduce the Prologue as a preface, then, is to read it flatter than it is — as though the story starts at verse 19 and the opening is done with. Introduce it instead as the entrance hall: the room the whole Gospel is entered through, and the room the whole Gospel answers back to.

## Pedagogical task

This slide's job is to change how you introduce a text, not to add a point inside one. Project it at the start of any sermon or series on John 1 — before the exegesis — and let the gold line make the correction: a preface is left behind; a foyer frames. Then say what the frame means for the weeks ahead: the themes your people are about to meet are sounded here first, and the whole book answers back to this opening. The restraint: don't map the architecture from the front — no diagrams, no lists of parallels — and don't over-explain the image; everyone has stood in an entrance hall, and the picture does its own work. One sentence is the whole move: *stop preaching the Prologue as a preface; introduce it as the entrance hall the whole Gospel answers back to.*
