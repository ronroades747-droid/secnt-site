Most hearers meet the word *exegesis* as a specialist's term — what scholars do to texts, carefully and at some distance. **John 1:18 turns that around.** At the close of the Prologue John says of the Son ἐκεῖνος ἐξηγήσατο — "he has made him known" — and the verb he chooses is the one our word *exegesis* descends from. **The Son is the Father's exegete**: the one who narrates the unseen God, who draws him out into the open where he can be known. Our research holds the line here at the plain sense of the verb — the fuller treatment of verse 18 belongs to the commentary's work on the Prologue's closing verses — and the plain sense is enough to carry the turn. **It also reframes the work of anyone who teaches from it.** The name we give the careful, word-by-word reading of Scripture comes from the verb John uses for the Son making the Father known. **Careful reading is not a technique for handling a text; it is an act of knowing God.**

*For the teacher, the shape of it in three steps.* First, **the word as your hearers know it** — ask what *exegesis* means to them, and let the answer be the dry one: a method, a seminary word. Second, **the verse**: read the last clause of John 1:18, give them *he has made him known*, and then show them the verb — the gold word and its transliteration on the slide make the resemblance visible before you name it. Third, **the reframe**: if the name of careful reading comes from the Son's own work, then reading Scripture closely is not a cold discipline beside faith but a way of knowing the God the Son has made known. **The order matters.** Let the resemblance land before you explain it; a hearer who sees *exēgēsato* and hears *exegesis* for himself owns the point in a way a definition from the front cannot give.

*What this page does not do, said plainly.* It does not open the rest of verse 18 — the Son's title there, the image of the Father's side, or the verse's first clause — each of which the commentary treats in its own place. It does not argue how the Prologue is built, or pair this closing verb with the creation of verse 3; that structure is its own lesson and it has its own slide. It does not claim that John coined the word, or that every later use of it carries his meaning; **it claims something narrower, that the verb behind our word stands here, at the Prologue's close, naming what the Son does.** And it does not turn the slide into a lesson on method: the point is not how to do exegesis but whose work the word names here.

## Pedagogical task

This slide's job is to **let a familiar word surprise the people who use it**. Project it when you reach John 1:18, or whenever the question of why we read Scripture closely comes up. Let the headline land first — most hearers have never heard the Son called an exegete — and **let the gold word and its transliteration show the resemblance** before you name it; on the wide slide the two grey lines beneath make it explicit. The restraint is most of the discipline here. **Stay on the plain sense of the verb.** **Do not open the rest of verse 18 from the front** — it is rich, and it is not this point. **Do not turn the slide into an argument about how the Prologue is built** — the frame of the Prologue is a real lesson and it is not this one. One sentence is the whole move: *careful reading of Scripture takes its name from the Son's work of making the Father known — so it is an act of knowing God.* **Two downloads, two jobs.** The **wide slide carries the whole move** — the claim, the verb, and underneath them what the verb means and what our word inherits from it — and it is the one to project while you teach. The **tall slide carries the claim and the verb without the explanation**, which makes it the one for a bulletin, a newsletter column or an invitation: it says the surprising thing and sends the reader to find out why.
