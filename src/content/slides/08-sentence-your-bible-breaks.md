In your English Bible, John 1:3 ends with the making of all things and a full stop, and verse 4 opens a new sentence: "In him was life." But John did not write the verse numbers — no one did, for more than a thousand years. The chapter divisions arrived in the thirteenth century and the verse numbers in the sixteenth, added by an editor so that readers could find their place. They are good tools; they are not inspired. And at this line, the tool cuts through the middle of a sentence. Our research concludes — a settled position in our treatment of the text — that the relative clause printed at the end of verse 3, ὃ γέγονεν (*ho gegonen*), does not close verse 3: it opens verse 4, as the subject of its sentence — ὃ γέγονεν ἐν αὐτῷ ζωὴ ἦν, "What has come into being, in him was life." Nothing is wrong with your Bible. Not a word is missing; not a word has moved; the division is editorial, and the words on either side of it stand exactly as John wrote them. Reading the sentence whole is not a correction of Scripture — it is a recovery of it: the sentence John wrote, heard at its own length. And a congregation that hears it learns two things at once: what the verse says, and what verse numbers are for.

## Pedagogical task

This slide's job is to hold the sentence whole in front of your people while you read it across the line. Project it as you come to verses 3 and 4. Read the sentence aloud the way John wrote it — across the boundary — and let the slide be the congregation's warrant that what they heard is what the text says: the clause in gold, the sentence beneath it, and the reference that spans two verse numbers because the sentence does. Then make the one clarification that keeps trust intact: nothing is missing and nothing has moved — the numbers came centuries later, to help readers find their place — and let that be the whole excursus. The restraint: leave the textual scholarship out of the pulpit. Don't explain the Greek tense; don't compare translations; never say "your Bible is wrong," because it isn't — say "the verse numbers came later," because they did. One sentence is the whole move: *read the sentence across the verse line, and tell them the numbers serve the text — not the other way round.*
