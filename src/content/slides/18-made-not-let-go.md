Most readers of John's opening carry a picture of creation they have never put into words: God made the world — back at the beginning, complete — and the world has been running on its own power ever since. Made, and then let go. The Prologue refuses that picture in one small phrase. Having said that all things came to be through the Word, it says of what has come into being: in him was life — ἐν αὐτῷ, *in him*. Our research reads the phrase as the denial of creation's autonomy. The creation that came to be through the Word does not, having come to be, stand on its own; its life is held in him. The dependence established at the moment of origin was not discharged once the thing existed — it abides, as the creature's very life. To be a creature is not only to have been made, once; it is to have one's life, now and continually, in him. That is a different picture altogether: not made-and-released but made-and-held — a grip that has never opened.

*One thing worth knowing before you teach it: the English above — what has come into being, in him was life — is this commentary's own rendering of the Greek text and its division; some printed Bibles attach the opening words to the previous sentence instead. Nothing in this slide's claim depends on where that line is drawn: the phrase ἐν αὐτῷ stands in verse 4 either way, and the slide pins the phrase, not the sentence break.*

*The Greek on the slide is the critical text, the phrase set alone, without the words around it. The gloss line is the commentary's own statement of the clause's content; the slide carries no other English beyond its headline.*

## Pedagogical task

This slide's job is proclamation, not proof: it states the refusal and hands you the phrase it stands on. The hearer's picture — made, then let go — is almost never spoken aloud, so name it first and let the headline contradict it; the surprise is that a verse they have read a hundred times was refusing their picture all along. From the pulpit that is a ten-second move and it asks no Greek of anyone: put the slide up, say what the old picture was, read the gloss line, and land the application — the Word did not make you and let you go; your life, this morning, is held in him. In a classroom, put the slide up after reading the passage and ask what the gold phrase rules out; the discussion will surface the deist picture on its own, and the correction lands better for having been discovered. The restraint is the threshold: this slide establishes *that* the life of what has come to be is in the Word — what that life *is*, and what it becomes, belongs to the verses ahead, and reaching for them spends the next passage's capital early. And resist the urge to compare prepositions from the front — the companion slide *Through him, then in him* does that work; this one asserts what follows from it. One phrase, one refusal, one application. Leave it there.
