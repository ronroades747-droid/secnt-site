Read John 1:13 in the Authorized Version: *Which were born, not of blood, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of man, but of God.* Most of us hear a list: three things that do not make a Christian, and then the one thing that does. Blood, flesh, man — three separate exclusions, each with its own reason.

**It is not a list. It is one thing, refused three times.** Each denial names part of the ordinary way a human being comes into the world. "Bloods" — John writes the word in the plural — is the union of two parents; Barrett: "Probably the blood of father and mother is meant." The will of the flesh is the impulse toward that union. And the will of a husband is the last and most personal step: McHugh calls him "the one who initiates the move towards physical union." Ridderbos puts the three together: "In all three the reference is to the natural process of procreation".

**And that one thing stands for everything the world can do.** Barth: "Sexual propagation is a type of other kinds of establishment that are proper to the being of the cosmos and to being in the cosmos." Birth is the world's way of making a person, and the verse refuses it — and with it every other way the world makes, grants or passes on what we are. Harris: "Divine procreation is totally unrelated to human procreation; spiritual generation has nothing to do with physical generation."

**Then God stands where the husband stood.** The three denials all stand on the father's side of a birth, and John's last word puts God in that place. Morris notices it in the verb: "his word 'are born' is the verb commonly used of the male parent in begetting children". That is why the commentary says *begotten*, not "born." Not of the world — of God.

**Why it matters.** Two verses earlier, his own people did not receive him — a household defined by descent from Abraham. John's first denial is "bloods." Carson draws the line: "heritage and race, even the Jewish race, are irrelevant to spiritual birth." No family, no heritage, no decision of ours, makes anyone a child of God. The one who does is God.

**Our research concludes that the three denials are one refusal of the world's way of making a person, and that God is set in its place.** The figure is held with high confidence.

*For the teacher, the shape of it in three steps.* First, **read the verse and ask the room what the three denials rule out.** Let them name three things. Second, **walk the three as one birth** — the two parents, the impulse, the husband who decides — **and then put the headline on the screen.** Third, **read the last words, "but of God," and then the gold line.** Not something the world can produce. God stands where the husband stood.

*What this page does not do, said plainly.* It does not take up how the begetting works, or how God's act meets our believing; John does not say, and the commentary does not say either. It does not set out what the church confesses about the new birth, or how the Father, the Word and the Spirit stand in this gift; that is a later page's. It does not argue whether "flesh" here means sin (it does not), or whether the verse speaks of baptism or of the birth of Jesus (it does not); the commentary page takes each of those up in turn.

## Pedagogical task

This slide's job is to **turn a list into a single refusal**. Most rooms hear three exclusions. **Let them hear the three as one birth** — two parents, the impulse toward their union, the husband who decides — before anyone generalizes. **Then widen it with Barth**: the ordinary way a person is made stands for everything the world can do. **Land on the last three words.** "But of God" is not a fourth item on the list; it is a different kind of answer, set exactly where the husband stood. **Stop at the verse.** How the begetting meets faith, and what the church confesses about regeneration, belong to later lessons; do not let this one swallow them. **Two downloads, two jobs.** The **16:9 carries the whole move** — the headline, the gold line and the verse beneath it — and it is the one to teach from. The **9:16 carries the title and the reference alone**, for a newsletter or a bulletin column with your own writing beneath it.
