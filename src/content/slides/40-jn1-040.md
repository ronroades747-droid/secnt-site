Read John 1:11 slowly. *He came to what was his own, and his own people did not welcome him.* Most of us hear it in one of two ways. Either it closes a door on Israel: they refused, and the story moves on without them. Or "first to Israel" becomes the whole of it, and the light narrows to one people.

**One word, twice.** "His own" comes twice in the verse, and not in the same form. The first is neuter: what was his own, the world he made, his own domain. The second is masculine: his own people, Israel, the covenant household inside that estate. He came to what was his own, the world he made, and within it to his own people. One line narrows from the estate to the household within it.

**First.** The order in salvation's history is real: he came first to Israel, the people taught by the law and the prophets. Their refusal was not a stranger turned away but a belonging repudiated. And the refusal is real, but it is not final. The very next verse speaks of those who received him.

**Never only.** The same line that grieves the household's refusal calls the whole world his. The universal scope of the light *of men* is not diminished by the covenant refusal. Israel-first is not Israel-only, and that is not a caveat imposed on the verse from outside. It is what the wordplay says. The guard is written into the verse's own grammar.

**Our research concludes that "his own people" names Israel, held with high confidence, verging on very high**, and that "his own" place is the world he made, held with high confidence, as is the reading of the one line as a narrowing from the estate to the household within it.

*For the teacher, the shape of it in three steps.* First, **read verse 11 aloud and ask the room whom "his own" means.** Most will give one answer. Second, **show them that the word comes twice**: his own place, his own people. Third, **put the slide up.** Israel first, never Israel only. The same line calls the whole world his.

*What this page does not do, said plainly.* It does not argue the clauses of verse 11 again; the commentary on verse 11, and the slide "A welcome refused", take those up. It does not set out the guilt of the refusal at length, or the Gospel's hinge at 13:1, which the commentary holds provisional. It does not take up verse 12's welcome, which is the next page's. And it does not adopt the severer reading, that when Christ was not received, the grace passed to the Gentiles; the commentary records that reading and does not adopt it.

## Pedagogical task

This slide's job is to **keep the room from hearing verse 11 as either a door shut on Israel or a light narrowed to Israel.** Both errors miss what the verse says about itself. **Begin with the question of whom "his own" means**, so that the room finds the word twice for itself. **Then set the two forms side by side**, the world he made and his own people within it, and let the order stand: first through Israel. **Land on the gold line.** The same line that grieves the household's refusal calls the whole world his; first, and never only. **Two downloads, two jobs.** The **16:9 carries the whole move** — the headline, the gold line and the two meanings of "his own" — and it is the one to teach from. The **9:16 carries the title and the reference alone**, for a newsletter or a bulletin column with your own writing beneath it.
