John's first verse is usually heard as three grand statements laid side by side — the Word was, the Word was with God, the Word was God — and heard that way it is lofty and it is inert, a list of high things said about Christ. **It is not a list.** The last two clauses are load-bearing on each other, and the quickest way to see it is to take one away. Remove *the Word was with God*, and *the Word was God* has nothing to land on: with no personal distinction already set down, the deity has nowhere to go but into the Father, and the Word becomes the same person under a second name — the error the ancient church called **modalism**. Now remove *the Word was God*, and the communion of the clause before it sinks: with God, near to God, in fellowship with God, but **less than God** — a great being beside the Creator rather than the Creator himself, which is **subordinationism**. Our research concludes, with the field's major commentators reading the verse as a single coherent whole, that this is the verse's own structure and not a doctrinal grid laid over it: **the personal distinction is set down before the deity is ever spoken**, so that the deity, when it comes, has nowhere to collapse to; and **the divine nature is predicated** so that the communion cannot be read downward into something less. What is worth teaching is that the two work **at the same time**. They are not one safeguard and then a second — they are two stones in an arch, each leaning on the other so that neither can fall. **And the arch was built long before anyone had a name for either way of falling.**

*For the teacher, the shape of it in three steps.* First, **the two clauses**, read aloud in John's order and in no other. Second, **the removals**: read them again with one of them missing, and then again with the other missing, and let the room hear what breaks each time — that is the whole lesson and it takes about forty seconds. Third, **the mutuality**, which is the step most likely to be lost: say plainly that the guarding runs *both* ways *at once*, so that the verse is stable in both directions rather than merely high in one. **The order matters.** Removing a clause is a demonstration your hearers can watch; asserting that the verse is precise is a claim they have to take on trust. Do the demonstration first and the claim will already be made by the time you say it.

*What this page does not do, said plainly.* It does not teach the Greek article, and it does not explain why the second clause says *the God* and the third says simply *God* — that mechanism is its own lesson and it has its own slide. It does not name Sabellius or Arius, or take the argument to the councils and what the church later built on this clause; the names and the conciliar history belong to their own treatments. It does not develop the doctrine of the Trinity, which this verse presupposes rather than argues. **And it does not claim that the verse stopped anyone** — readers with this sentence in front of them have gone wrong in both directions, and the point here is not about the sentence's historical effect but about how it is built: **it is built so that neither error can take hold.** It hands over one structure and the two removals that show it, and stops there.

## Pedagogical task

This slide's job is to **turn a list into a structure**. Your hearers already know the three clauses; what the slide changes is the relation between the last two — from *and, and* to *because of each other*. Project it, read the two gold lines aloud in order, then read them again with one of them gone. **The gold lines hand your hearers the words**, so the claim rests on the text rather than on you; the two grey lines beneath say what each removal costs. The restraint is most of the discipline here. **Do not explain the article from the front** — the small word that carries the guard in the Greek is a real point and it is not this one. **Do not name the heresiarchs**: the plain names of the two errors do the work in a room, and a fourth-century surname invites a history lesson you did not come to give. **Do not step into the doctrine of the Trinity** — the point here is smaller and much harder to argue with, that verse 1 is one structure rather than three separate claims. And **do not let *forecloses* creep in**: the verse did not stop anyone, and you do not need it to have. One sentence is the whole move: *remove either clause, and the other falls.* **Two downloads, two jobs.** The **wide slide carries the whole move** — the claim, the two clauses, and underneath them what each removal costs — and it is the one to project while you teach. The **tall slide carries the claim and the two clauses without the consequences**, which makes it the one for a bulletin, a newsletter column or an invitation: it says the surprising thing and sends the reader to find out why.
