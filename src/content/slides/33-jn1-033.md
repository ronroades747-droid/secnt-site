Read John 1:9, and then keep reading. *The true light, which gives light to every human being, was coming into the world.* Every human being. And two verses later: *his own did not receive him.* Most people who have taught this passage have felt the question coming before anyone asks it. If the light is given to everyone, why did so few see it? Was the light not enough?

**It was not a failure of the light. It was a refusal of sight.** The verse's universal is exact. The light is offered to each, and it is sufficient for each. In Ridderbos's words, it is for every human being "that by which alone he or she can live." Köstenberger draws the line between purpose and outcome: giving light to every person "was the purpose of Jesus's coming though sadly not the actual outcome due to many people's unbelief." Nothing in the refusal shows that the light failed to reach anyone. The sun does not stop shining on the man who shuts his eyes.

**Chrysostom saw the mechanics of it, and his sentence is the whole answer.** "If some, willfully closing the eyes of their mind, would not receive the rays of that light, their darkness arises not from the nature of the light but from their own wickedness as they willfully deprive themselves of the gift. For the grace is shed forth upon all, turning its back on no one." The light turns its back on no one. When backs are turned, they are ours.

**Our research concludes that the illumination this verse asserts is real, and given to every human being** — the possibility of sight, not sight forced on anyone. The illumination is held with high confidence, and the universal reach it carries with very high confidence. What the unbelief of verses 10 and 11 refuses is not the light's arrival. It is perception.

**There is more, and it is the hope in it.** The Gospel does not leave the light standing at the door. It keeps pressing toward sight through the whole story, and it shows perception arriving in its hardest case: Thomas, who would not believe unless he saw, is shown and says, "My Lord and my God." Refusal was never the light's deficiency; the same light that shone all along meets his demand. And sight is not made the requirement, because the next words are "Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have believed."

*For the teacher, the shape of it in three steps.* First, **read verse 9 and then verse 11 aloud, and let the question arise on its own.** Someone will ask it, and it is the right question. Second, **put the two headline lines on the screen and say them slowly** — offered to each, sufficient for each — before saying anything about refusal. Third, **read Chrysostom's last sentence and then the gold line.** The grace turns its back on no one. Not failing to see — turning our backs.

*What this page does not do, said plainly.* It does not argue how the verse is construed, or what "true" means; the commentary takes both up in full. It does not take up the reading that narrows "every human being" to those who are enlightened. It does not teach that everyone is saved: the Prologue's own guard against that reading comes at verse 12, *to all who received him*. And it does not diagnose anyone in your room. The slide describes what refusal is; it does not say who has refused.

## Pedagogical task

This slide's job is to **answer the question verse 9 raises before anyone has a chance to answer it badly**: that a light given to everyone must have been too weak, or not really given, since most did not see it. **Lead with the grace.** The headline says it twice for a reason. If you start from the gold line, "turning our backs" becomes a scolding and the gift becomes an afterthought; spoken after the headline, the gold line takes away the excuse the hearer is already reaching for. **Do not end at the refusal.** The hope in this verse is that the light keeps coming — all the way to Thomas — so close with 20:28–29, not with verse 11. Do not take up the grammar of the verse from the front; it is settled in the commentary and it will swallow the time. **Two downloads, two jobs.** The **16:9 carries the whole move** — the gift and the refusal — and it is the one to preach from. The **9:16 carries the title and the reference alone**, for a newsletter or a bulletin column with your own writing beneath it.
