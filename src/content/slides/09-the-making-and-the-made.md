At the hinge of John 1:3–4, two forms of the same verb sit side by side. Verse 3 states the creation with ἐγένετο (*egeneto*) — the aorist, the tense of the act: all things came to be through him, the coming-to-be of what had not been. The clause that opens verse 4 changes the form: γέγονεν (*gegonen*) — the perfect, the tense of the standing result: what has come into being, and now stands. English can barely show the difference — your Bible gives "was made" and then "has been made," and nearly every reader takes the variation for style. It is not style. Our research concludes that the change of tense is doing doctrine: creation is not only a past act; it is a standing result. The aorist looks at the making; the perfect looks at the made. The world God made is not a project he finished and walked away from — it stands, now, and John lodges that standing in the Word. A congregation that hears the two forms learns something the English never quite said aloud: creation happened, and creation stands.

## Pedagogical task

This slide's job is to let your people watch one verb change its form while you say what the change carries. Project it as you reach verses 3 and 4, and say the two Greek forms aloud — the transliterations under the gold pair are on the frame for exactly this, so the congregation hears the difference it is seeing. Then make the one move: creation is not only what God did — it is what stands, now, held in the Word. The line under the pair hands you the whole sentence: the aorist looks at the making; the perfect looks at the made. The restraint: this is one clarifying beat inside a creation sermon, not a grammar lesson — give the words *aorist* and *perfect* one sentence each and no more, don't parse, don't diagram, and let the standing itself preach: God did not finish the world and file it away; he made it, and it stands. One sentence is the whole move: *what God made stands, held, right now — and your Bible's own tenses say so.*
