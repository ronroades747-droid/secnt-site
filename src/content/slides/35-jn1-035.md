Read John 1:11 slowly. *He came to what was his own, and his own people did not welcome him.* Many English Bibles give the last clause as "his own received him not," or "his own did not receive him," and most of us hear it as something that happens inside a person: an idea not accepted, a decision not made.

**It was a welcome refused. The owner stood at his own door.** John's verb pictures someone arriving at a house. Schnackenburg says it is "probably from the metaphor of welcoming to a house." Ridderbos: his own were "unwilling to receive him as a welcome guest." Harris speaks of "the welcome he deserved." Barth: "They ought to receive him willingly and joyfully as one who is at home with them."

**And the one at the door was no guest.** The line uses one word twice. He came to what was *his own* — the world he had made — and *his own people*, Israel, did not welcome him. The household turned away the one whose household it was.

**Why "welcome," and not "know"?** The verse before says that the world did not know him. Cyril of Alexandria saw why John does not say that here. Of Israel, he wrote, "it would not have been true to say 'knew him not,' when the older law had preached about him and the prophets who came after led them by the hand." The door was shut by a household that had been told who was coming.

**The force is in the picture, not in the dictionary.** The Greek verb carries no special meaning of its own that the preacher needs to unpack; what makes the clause weigh so much is who came, to whom, and where. And it says no more than it says. In McHugh's words, the phrase signifies "the absence of a true welcome; it does not necessarily imply a fully deliberate rejection."

**Our research concludes that verse 11 tells of a personal welcome refused** — the owner come to his own, and his own people not taking him in. The force of "did not welcome" is held with very high confidence, and the reading of "his own people" as Israel with high confidence.

**And it is not the last word.** John does not explain why they refused, and neither should we. The refusal is real, and it is not final. The very next verse turns to those who did take him in, and in this Gospel Israel's name stays an honoured one: Nathanael confesses him King of Israel (1:49). And the Gospel's next word over the whole world, at 1:29, is the Lamb of God who takes away its sin.

*For the teacher, the shape of it in three steps.* First, **read the verse in the version your room uses, and ask what "did not receive him" means.** Let the room say that they did not believe in him; it is the answer most people carry. Second, **read Schnackenburg and Ridderbos — a house, a welcome guest — and then put the headline on the screen.** Third, **read Cyril, and then the gold line.** A welcome refused. The owner at his own door.

*What this page does not do, said plainly.* It does not argue the two senses of "his own" clause by clause, or what "he came" asserts; the commentary takes both up in full. It does not take up what verse 11 means for Israel's election, or for Israel's place in God's purpose; that is a later page's. It does not take up verse 12's "received," which is the next page's. It does not say why his own people refused, because the text does not. And it does not make the verse a charge against Jewish neighbours today: the same line that grieves the refusal calls the whole world his.

## Pedagogical task

This slide's job is to **give the verse back its picture**: a door, a household and its owner, where most hearers have only an inward "receive." **Lead with the picture, not the charge.** The headline lands once the room has heard Schnackenburg's house and Ridderbos's guest; spoken before them it is only a stronger word. **Let Cyril carry the weight**, not the Greek: the clause is heavy because the refusers had been taught, not because the verb is rare, so do not build the sermon on a word study. **Do not end at the door.** The refusal is real and it is not final, so close with those who did take him in and with 1:29, not with verse 11. Do not take up Israel's election from the front; it deserves its own lesson and will swallow this one. **Two downloads, two jobs.** The **16:9 carries the whole move** — the headline, the gold line and the verse beneath it — and it is the one to preach from. The **9:16 carries the title and the reference alone**, for a newsletter or a bulletin column with your own writing beneath it.
