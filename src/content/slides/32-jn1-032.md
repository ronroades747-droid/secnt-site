Read John 1:8 on its own and listen to how it lands. *He was not the light.* It sounds like a correction. As though the evangelist, having introduced a man sent from God and given him the whole business of testimony, now remembers to put him in his place before anyone gets the wrong idea. Generations of readers have heard it that way, and it is why the verse is so often preached apologetically — as damage control about a man who was getting too much attention.

**It is not a demotion. It is the definition.** A witness is constituted by not being the thing he attests. The moment he becomes what is testified to, he has stopped being a witness — he is now the evidence, or the defendant, or the prize, but he is no longer the one pointing. Verse 8 is not subtracting from John's office. It is what makes there be an office at all.

**The evangelist performs it in the grammar, and the detail is worth showing.** Through verse 7 the Baptist is οὗτος, *this man* — the near demonstrative, the man right here in front of us. At the denial the pronoun changes: οὐκ ἦν ἐκεῖνος τὸ φῶς, *that one* was not the light. The far demonstrative. He is pushed back a step in the very clause that separates him from the light. And then ἀλλ᾽, the strong adversative — *but* that he might testify about the light. The sentence does not end on the negation. It turns, hard, and comes back to his purpose. He is not the light; he is *for* the light.

**Our research concludes that the denial is the office's own definition rather than a qualification appended to it**, and the ground is the structure of these three verses: a commission traced to its sender, an office stated forensically, and a guard — and the guard is what holds the office to its object. That is held at very high confidence, and it rests on the text's witness-logic rather than on any reconstruction of the situation behind it.

**There is a refinement, and it belongs to the teaching rather than qualifying it.** A great deal has been written about a rival Baptist movement that thought too much of John, and on that reading verse 8 is polemical — aimed at somebody. The occasion may well be real, and this commentary grants it at exactly the bound Schnackenburg set: what may legitimately be deduced is a Messiahship-claim circulating in Baptist circles, and no more. But **a plausible historical occasion is not the ground of a reading.** The slide does not carry this and a teacher does not need it. It is here because the distinction transfers: the question of why a text may have been written is not the question of what it says.

*For the teacher, the shape of it in three steps.* First, **read verse 8 aloud and ask what it sounds like.** Most rooms will say it sounds like a put-down, and saying so out loud is most of the work. Second, **ask what a witness is** — not what John was, but what any witness is, in any courtroom. The answer arrives on its own: someone who is not the thing he is testifying about. Third, **read the whole verse again and stop at the word *but*.** The sentence does not end on what he is not. It turns back to what he is for. That is the part they will still have on Tuesday.

*What this page does not do, said plainly.* It does not exegete the commission of verse 6 or the purpose-clause of verse 7 — each has its own treatment in the commentary. It does not take up what it means that the testimony was *that all might believe*, which is a separate claim about purpose and result. It does not adjudicate the rival-sect hypothesis beyond the bound stated above. And **it does not claim that John is diminished here.** The thrust of the office toward him is positive from beginning to end — Calvin's herald, the dawn that goes before the sun; Augustine's lamp; Chrysostom's observation that the greater witness is borne to the lesser. The claim is the opposite of a diminishment: his greatness is precisely what he is not.

## Pedagogical task

This slide's job is to **turn a sentence that sounds defensive into a sentence that defines**, not to teach a doctrine of John the Baptist. Your people are not confused about who John was; they have simply never been shown that verse 8 is doing constructive work rather than damage control. So project it and do the one thing it licenses: read the verse, let them hear the demotion they expect, and then ask what a witness actually is. **Do not turn this into a lesson about humility.** It is available — John will say it himself at 3:30 — but the moment you go there the observation is spent, and what is lost is the structural point that the denial is the office. Do not take up the rival-sect question from the front; it is interesting, it is bounded, and it will swallow the time. And **do not oversell it**: this is not a recovered reading. It is what the verse says, heard properly. **Two downloads, two jobs.** The **16:9 carries the whole move** — the claim, the phrase from the verse, and the reason — and it is the one to project while you teach from it. The **9:16 carries the title and the reference alone**, which makes it the one for a newsletter or a bulletin column with your own writing beneath it.
