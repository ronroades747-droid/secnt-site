You have read John 1:3 as a verse about Christ, and you have read it right: the whole of creation is the Word's doing, and nothing at all stands outside it. **But the second half of the verse is doing something else as well, and it is about the world rather than about him.** To hear it, look at what the alternatives needed. Herman Bavinck sets out the systems the early church had to answer — **Gnosticism**, which to explain sin posited an inferior god alongside the supreme deity, or an eternal matter nobody made, and later **Manichaeism**, which explained sin by an original evil being beside the true God. **Every one of those systems needed a second principle to carry the blame for the world's darkness** — and every one of them, by needing it, impugned the world. If matter is the problem, then the material order is a mistake, and the person hearing you is living inside the mistake. **Now set the negative of verse 3 against that.** If not even one thing came to be apart from the Word, there is no second principle at all: no province of being that some demiurge botched, **nothing in the creation traceable to a rival hand**. **And that is the conclusion, which is a stronger thing than an assertion:** the goodness of the world is not argued for here — it is *secured*, in the same act and by the same words that secure the Son. Our research concludes that this is the second doctrine the negative clause gives, and it is the one a congregation is least likely to have been told about. **The world is good because it is wholly his.**

*For the preacher, the shape of it in three steps.* First, **the alternatives all needed somebody to blame** — that is the historical fact, and it is what makes the rest an inference rather than a claim. Second, **the clause leaves nobody available** — that is the exegesis, already settled, and you do not have to argue it from the front. Third, **therefore the world is good** — and that is the sentence to land on and stop.

*What this page does not do, said plainly.* It does not argue that the anti-Gnostic reading of this clause fails on dating, or that what it forecloses is wider than the demiurge — that is its own slide, and this page takes its conclusion as given. It does not develop creation out of nothing, or the distinction the tradition drew between two Greek words for it. It does not exegete the force of the negation, or read the two halves of verse 3 as two distinct claims, or take up whether *through him* makes the Word an agent or an instrument. It does not go to the Fathers or to the creed. **This page draws one conclusion from a settled reading and stops there.**

## Pedagogical task

This slide's job is to **hand a preacher a doctrine he already believes and show him it was in a verse he thought he had finished with**. The restraint is that you must not turn it into a lecture on Gnosticism. The systems are on the screen for one reason — to show what the alternatives *needed* — and a congregation that leaves knowing about Valentinus has been given the wrong thing. Project it and read it in its own order: the claim, then the reason, then stop on the gold line and stay there. From the pulpit the whole move is one sentence: *do not use the second half of verse 3 only to say how great the Son is — use it to say that the world your people live in is good, and that there is nobody else to blame for it.* **What not to do beyond that:** do not soften it into a general cheerfulness about creation. The claim is precise and it is negative in form — *nothing traceable to a rival hand* — and its force comes from what it excludes. And do not pit it against the Christology: the verse says both things with the same words, which is the whole reason this is worth preaching rather than merely noting. **Two downloads, two jobs.** The **wide slide carries the whole move** — the claim, the reason and the consequence — and it is the one to project while you teach from it. The **tall slide carries the claim and the consequence without the reason**, which makes it the one for a bulletin, a newsletter column or an invitation: it says the surprising thing and sends the reader to find out why.
