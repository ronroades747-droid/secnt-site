*In the beginning was the Word.* You know what that line is doing: before anything was made, the Word already was. **That reading is true, and the Lecture's own verdict on it is that it is too small.** The reason is that John is not using one verb in these opening lines — he is using two, and they are not two speeds of the same thing. At verse 1 the verb is **ἦν**, the imperfect of *to be*, the tense of continuous existence in the past: it states existence, with no beginning marked. At verse 3, of all things that came to be through him, the verb is **ἐγένετο**, the aorist of *to become*: it states coming-to-be, entry into existence at a point. The picture nearly every hearer carries is a single line, with creation somewhere along it and the Word standing further back on the same line — earlier, but on the line. **Our research concludes that the two verbs will not allow that picture.** They are not two points in time; they name two different orders of existence. The Word does not become; the Word is. The creation does not eternally be; it comes to be. Between those two verbs lies the entire distinction the Prologue is built upon — which is why this is a grammatical observation that turns out to be a doctrinal one, and why the move fits in a single sentence: *everything else comes to be, and the Word simply is.*

*For the teacher, the parsing in full.* **ἦν** is the third person singular imperfect of **εἰμί**, *to be*. **ἐγένετο** is the third person singular aorist of **γίνομαι**, *to become, to come to be*. The short does not put either parsing on the screen and does not speak the two lexical forms; they are here because a teacher will want them and a hearer will not.

*What this page does not do, said plainly.* It does not count the occurrences of the imperfect across verses 1 and 2, or read anything from their number. It does not draw the two refusals the tense supports — that there is no moment at which the Word was not, and that he is not the first and oldest of the things that were made; those follow from this contrast but they are a further step. It does not read the *through him* of verse 3, which is a claim about how creation came about and belongs to the commentary on that clause; the slide records that the verse says it. And it does not go to the councils, or to the tradition's own name for this distinction, or to what eternity is. **This page establishes the two orders, and stops there** — which is precisely what the Lecture's own instruction for this slide says to do.

## Pedagogical task

This slide's job is to **take away the timeline, not to move the Word further back along it** — and the restraint is that you must not answer the question the hearer is actually asking. Someone who says *the Word existed before creation* is asking a question about *when*, and if you answer it — however generously — you have agreed that the two belong on one line. Put the slide up and read the two verbs off it, and ask the class or the congregation the question the frame is built to provoke: not *what does each verb mean*, but **what does each verb state?** One states existence with no beginning marked. One states a coming-to-be at a point. Those are not two answers to *when*; they are two answers to *how a thing exists*. From the pulpit the whole move is one sentence at the end of the reading: *everything else comes to be — the Word simply is.* **What not to do:** do not translate the verbs from the front. The moment you say *ἦν means "was"* you have handed back the very word the hearer is mishearing — which is why the slide sets the Greek bare, with no English rendering under either verb, and why the English above each verb states what the verse *says* rather than what the verb *means*. And do not turn it into a grammar lesson: the parse is in the notes above for you, not for the screen. Two verbs, two orders, no timeline: that is the whole move.
