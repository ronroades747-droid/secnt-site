You have almost certainly heard it, and you may well have said it: John wrote verse three to shut the door on the Gnostics. It is one of the most repeated things said about this verse — and **it will not bear the dates.** The fully developed Gnostic systems, with their named teachers and their elaborate hierarchies of aeons, are second-century. The Prologue is late first. So whatever the clause was answering in its own moment was a tendency, a drift in that direction, and not Valentinus or Basilides by name; to read it as a point-by-point rebuttal of systems that did not yet exist is to date the polemic wrong. **Something in the claim is right, and it should be kept.** Read the clause back into the world it was written into and there is an edge in it: the verse does stand against any reading that would set a lesser maker between God and the world. **But our research concludes that the clause's force is not locked to that polemic**, and the correction is a gain rather than a loss. The clause is not narrower than the heresy; it is wider than it. It does not rule out only the demiurge — it rules out every mediating instrument and every competing agency whatsoever, any second hand of any kind, in any century. **The anti-Gnostic reading is the most prominent historical instance of what the clause forecloses. It is not the boundary of it.** Which is why the correction is worth making out loud rather than quietly dropping the old line: the narrow claim ties the verse to a controversy your hearer has never heard of and cannot check, and the wider one hands them something they can use on any Monday.

*For the teacher, the two reasons in order.* The first is **dating**: the developed systems postdate the Gospel, so a point-by-point rebuttal is chronologically impossible and only a tendency was there to answer. The second is **scope**, and it is the larger of the two: the foreclosure is stated absolutely, so it excludes every rival agency as a class rather than one rival by name. The first reason limits what the clause *could* have been doing; the second describes what it *is* doing. **A hearer who only gets the first will think something has been taken away from the verse; it is the second that gives it back with interest.**

*What this page does not do, said plainly.* It does not argue the goodness of creation, which is where this same foreclosure goes next and which is its own slide. It does not exegete the emphatic form of the negation, or read the two halves of verse 3 as two distinct claims. It does not take up whether *through him* makes the Word an agent or an instrument — that is a question about the Word's own standing and belongs to the commentary on that clause; this page's phrase asks only whether anything *else* could be either. It does not name the early readers whose reception history the Lecture traces, and it does not go to the councils. **This page corrects one claim and widens it, and stops there.**

## Pedagogical task

This slide's job is to **take a claim away and hand back a larger one in the same breath** — and the restraint is that you must not spend the whole time on the taking away. The temptation with a corrected misreading is to enjoy the correction; do that and your hearer leaves with a hole where a familiar line used to be. Project the slide and read it in its own order: the refusal at the top, the two dates in the middle, and then stop on the gold line and stay there, because that is the part they are meant to carry out of the room. From the pulpit the whole move is one sentence: *do not say John wrote this against the Gnostics — say he wrote it against every rival, and the Gnostics were only the most famous.* In a class, put the two dates side by side first and let them see the claim fail on its own before you say anything about what replaces it; the correction lands better when the hearer has already made it. **What not to do:** do not turn it into a lecture on Gnosticism. The systems are not the subject — the *class of things the verse rules out* is the subject, and the Gnostics are on the screen only as the most famous member of it. And do not overcorrect: the polemic is real and the edge is genuinely there, so a teacher who says John had nothing to do with any of this has swapped one wrong claim for another. **Two downloads, two jobs.** The **wide slide carries the whole move** — the claim, the dates and the replacement — and it is the one to project while you teach from it; the talking points are all on it. The **tall slide carries the question and the replacement without the dates**, which makes it the one for a bulletin, a newsletter column or an invitation: it raises the problem and sends the reader to find out why, rather than settling it in a box they will read once and put down.
