Read John 1:10 slowly. *He was in the world, and the world was made through him, and the world did not know him.* Most of us hear the last clause as a sad misunderstanding. A stranger arrived, nobody recognized him, and the story moved on. That is how the verse is usually preached: the world did not know who he was.

**It was not a stranger unrecognized. It was the Maker refused.** The middle clause will not let the verse be read as a case of mistaken identity. The world was made through him. John says of *the world* what the tradition says of *all things*, and he says it at exactly the point where the world fails to know him. McHugh observes that, apart from this verse and Acts 17:24, the New Testament nowhere says that the world was made by God. The world that refused him owed its existence to him.

**Ridderbos puts the whole of it in three sentences.** "The world did not know him, not because he was a stranger but because it was estranged from him, from its origin. The world *should* have known him." Barth says the same from the other side: the Word came into the world from above, "yet not, of course, as a foreign body, but as the truth … which the world really ought to know." The world is estranged, not alien.

**Knowing, here, is a relation, and not knowing is that relation refused.** In Ridderbos's words, "*not* to know the Word is to reject a relationship with him." Readers of very different traditions come to the same place: the knowing the verse has in mind engages intellect, heart and will together. What the world refused was not information. It was the one from whom it had its being.

**Our research concludes that verse 10 tells of a refusal, not a misunderstanding** — the Word present in the world he made, and then the turn against him. That reading of the verse, the echo of verse 3 that grounds it, and the sense of "did not know" are each held with very high confidence.

**And it is not the last word.** The Gospel does not explain the refusal. In Ridderbos's words, "The inner contradiction of this not knowing is not explained." But it does not end there either. The next time John names the world, at 1:29, it is the world whose sin the Lamb of God takes away.

*For the teacher, the shape of it in three steps.* First, **read the verse aloud and ask what went wrong.** Let the room say that nobody recognized him; it is the answer most people carry. Second, **read the middle clause again, slowly — the world was made through him — and then put the headline on the screen.** Third, **read Ridderbos's three sentences, and then the gold line.** Not a stranger unrecognized. The Maker refused.

*What this page does not do, said plainly.* It does not argue what "the world" means clause by clause, or what "he was in the world" asserts; the commentary takes both up in full. It does not explain why the world refused, because the text does not. It does not take up verse 11's "his own," which is the next page's. And it does not diagnose anyone in your room. The slide describes what the refusal was; it does not say who has refused.

## Pedagogical task

This slide's job is to **take away the excuse the verse is usually heard to offer**: that the world simply failed to recognize a stranger. **Lead with the middle clause.** The headline lands only once the room has heard that the world was made through him; start from the refusal and it becomes an accusation without a ground, but spoken after the middle clause it is what the verse is, an indictment. **Do not end at the indictment.** The refusal is real and it is not final, so close with 1:29, not with verse 10. Do not take up the three senses of "world" from the front; the commentary settles them, and they will swallow the time. **Two downloads, two jobs.** The **16:9 carries the whole move** — the headline, the gold line and the sentence beneath it — and it is the one to preach from. The **9:16 carries the title and the reference alone**, for a newsletter or a bulletin column with your own writing beneath it.
