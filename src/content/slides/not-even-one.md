Almost every English Bible gives you John 1:3 the same way: *and without him was nothing made*. Nothing. It is the flattest word available, and it has been repeated so often that the ear no longer stops on it. John did not write it. The colorless one-word negative was there for him to use, and he passed it over for a two-word form — οὐδὲ ἕν — which pulls the negation apart into its pieces and puts the weight on the last: *not even one*. Not even one thing. The two forms are true of exactly the same facts; they do not weigh the same. It is the difference, in English, between *nothing was left out* and *not a single thing was left out*. Our research concludes that this emphatic form is the reading of the verse — it is what the earliest surviving copy of the Prologue carries and what the modern critical editions print — and that the emphasis is not ornamental. A flat *nothing* closes the question loosely. *Not even one thing* closes it absolutely: it goes item by item through the whole of creation and finds no exception — nothing overlooked, nothing delegated, nothing that arrived by another road. That is why the second half of the verse is worth its own sentence in your sermon rather than a nod on the way past.

## Pedagogical task

This slide's job is to replace one word in your hearers' ears, not to teach them Greek. Project it and read the gold line aloud before you explain anything — the point lands on the words themselves, and the headline gives the hearer permission to be surprised by a verse they thought they had finished with. Then use the rendering: say *not even one thing*, and let it shut the door item by item — the smallest thing, the strangest thing, the thing someone told them slipped in some other way. The restraint: don't parse the Greek from the front, don't put it on the screen, and don't let this become a lesson about translation committees — the familiar rendering isn't wrong, it is only quiet. One sentence is the whole move: *say what John said, and let the weight fall where he put it.*
