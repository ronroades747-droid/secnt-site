There is a sentence millions of Christians say out loud, in unison, most Sundays — and it is a verse of the Bible. The Nicene Creed confesses the Son as the one *through whom all things were made*. That clause is not a summary of John's third verse and not an allusion to it. It is that verse. The evangelist writes that *all things came to be through him*, and when the church came to fix her faith in a symbol she reached for his preposition — δι' οὗ, *di' hou*, *through whom* — and set it in the middle of what the whole church says together. Our research reads that clause as John 1:3 made confession: the preposition of the Prologue carried up into the rule of faith. And the recognition opens a second thing, because the word *through* is easy to hear wrong. The ear takes it as *used* — the Father did the making, the Son was what the making was done with — so that a line about the Son's dignity is heard as a line about his usefulness. The church has never read it that way. The Greek and Latin Fathers alike received the verse as the church's charter of creation through the Word, and Theodore of Mopsuestia, in the fourth century, put it with a precision worth hearing: the evangelist, he says, shows clearly that the words *all things were made through him* were used not to denote service, but cooperation. Not service. Cooperation. That reading is not a modern construal laid over the verse; it is the tradition's own — and your congregation is reciting it, in the apostle's own preposition, every time it says the Creed.

*Theodore's line reaches English through Rudolf Schnackenburg,* The Gospel according to St John, *vol. 1, translated by Kevin Smyth (New York: Herder and Herder, 1968), page 238, where he renders it in the course of his own treatment of John 1:3; Schnackenburg cites Theodore's* Commentary on John *at 1, 3. The creed's clause is δι' οὗ τὰ πάντα ἐγένετο, from the symbol of 381; the English above is this commentary's own rendering of it.*

## Pedagogical task

This slide's job is to make a recognition possible, not to teach a doctrine. Your people already have everything it gives them — they have said it, many of them hundreds of times. What they have never been told is what they were saying. So project it in the ten seconds after the Creed, while the words are still in the room, and do the one thing it licenses: read the clause off the screen, then say where it comes from. *What you just said is John, chapter one, verse three, in the apostle's own words.* Then hand them the second half, and keep it to a sentence: the church has always heard in that little word a Son at work with his Father, not a Son being used. The restraint is the whole discipline here. Do not explain the Greek from the front, do not walk them through the grammar of the preposition, and do not turn the moment into a history of the councils — the recognition is complete in itself and stops being a discovery the moment it becomes a lesson. **And do not use this one cold.** It is a pulpit move and it needs a congregation with the words still in its mouth; in a classroom, where nobody has just recited anything, it is an interesting fact rather than a recognition, and the lecture this page links is the better door.
