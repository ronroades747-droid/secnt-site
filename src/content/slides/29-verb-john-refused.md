Every preacher has been warned off the argument from silence, and the warning is a good one. **What a text does not say usually proves nothing**, because a writer's reasons for leaving a word out are invisible to us — he may have weighed it and declined it, or he may never have thought of it at all, and from the outside those two look identical. John's opening is the exception, and it is worth knowing exactly why it is one. Read John 1:1–2 and look at the verbs. **There is one, and there is nothing else**: ἦν, *was* — and no verb of any other kind stands in those two verses. Then read one sentence further. At verse 3 John reaches for a second verb and uses it three times over — ἐγένετο, *came to be* — and spends every one of them on the created order: *all things came to be through him; apart from him not one thing came to be; that which has come to be.* **So the verb of origination was not missing from his vocabulary, and it was not far away.** It is one clause later, in the same breath, and it goes to everything that exists. He does not put it on the Word. **That is what lifts this out of the ordinary argument from silence.** The usual objection is that you cannot show the writer had the word and passed it over — and here you can, because the word is in his hand three times in the very next sentence, and it goes to the whole creation and not to the one through whom the creation came. Our research reads that distribution as designed rather than incidental. What the slide hands a preacher is not a claim about eternity so much as the evidence for one: **John has not written a sentence anywhere in these verses saying the Word is eternal, and he has done something harder to argue with — he held the verb that would have said otherwise, and he never once used it.**

*For the teacher, the shape of it in two steps.* First, **the absence** — read verses 1 and 2 and say what is not there. One verb, four clauses, and nothing else: no making, no becoming, no origin of any kind. Second — and this is the step that does the work — **the presence.** One sentence later the same writer uses the other verb, three times, of everything that exists. **The order matters more than it looks.** A silence offered on its own is a claim your hearers have to take on trust; a silence with the sound beside it is evidence they can check while you are still speaking. If you have room for only one of the two, it is the second you cannot drop — the absence means nothing until they have heard what would have filled it.

*What this page does not do, said plainly.* It does not count the occurrences of *was* across verses 1 and 2 or read anything from their number; that is its own lesson and it has its own slide. It does not set *was* against *came to be* as two orders of existence, which is a further step again. It does not teach the imperfect. **And it does not claim that John never uses that verb of the Word anywhere** — he does, once, at verse 14, where *the Word became flesh*. That verse carries its own argument and belongs to its own treatment; the claim here is about the opening, where the question is what the Word is, and where the verb of coming-to-be never touches him. It does not go to the councils, or to what the church later built on this clause. **It hands over one silence and the reason that silence counts, and stops there.**

## Pedagogical task

This slide's job is to **license a move most preachers have been taught to avoid, and to fence it in the same breath**. Someone in your congregation has been told, rightly, that you cannot argue from what a text does not say — so the slide's work is not to assert a silence but to show why this one is different. Project it and make the argument in two steps, in that order: first the absence, then the presence one clause later. **Do not reverse them, and do not skip the second.** The restraint is most of the discipline here. **Do not count the *was*es** or build anything on their number — that is another slide's move, and the moment you start counting you have changed the subject from what is missing to what is repeated. **Do not set *was* against *came to be* as two kinds of existence**; that is a further step and it belongs elsewhere. **Do not explain the Greek from the front** — the gold word on the screen is there to be pointed at, not parsed, and the English in your mouth does all the work in the room. And **do not let *came to be* slacken into *came***: the Word does come, later in the Prologue, and that is a different verb doing a different job. One sentence is the whole move: *he had the word for it, he used it on everything else, and he never once used it on the Word.* **Two downloads, two jobs.** The **wide slide carries the whole move** — the claim, the Greek, and the reason underneath it — and it is the one to project while you teach. The **tall slide carries the claim and the Greek without the reason**, which makes it the one for a bulletin, a newsletter column or an invitation: it says the surprising thing and sends the reader to find out why.
