Read John 1:6–13 once more and watch nothing but the verbs. A man came. He came to bear witness. The light was in the world. The world did not know him. He came to his own, and his own did not welcome him. As many as received him were given a right. They were begotten of God. **Every verb in that list is past.** Together they tell an advent already complete, told from its far side.

**Except one phrase.** At the end of the turn, where the passage says the right is given *to those who believe in his name*, the telling reaches past itself. τοῖς πιστεύουσιν (*to those who believe*) is a present participle, and the time it names is not the story's time but the reader's. It is the one verb in the movement whose time reaches the reader's present, and the only place in these eight verses where the passage leaves off narrating and faces the reader.

**Two other verbs here are present in form, and it is worth knowing why neither does the same work.** At verse 9 the light was *coming* into the world. That participle gives the manner of the advent inside the long *was* of the verse, and it does not reach forward to the reader. At the same verse the light *enlightens* every human being. The commentary reads that verb as naming what the light does as light, not as fixing a time. That reading is contested: Bultmann and Michaels both take its present as reaching the evangelist's own day, and the commentary sets the disagreement out. So what to look for is not a present form. It is a time that reaches you.

Look at where it sits, because the placement is the teaching. It is not at the light's shining. It is not at the refusal, which is where a preacher's instinct would put it and where the passage's weight seems to fall. **It is at the reception, and nowhere else.** The evangelist tells you about an advent that is already complete, leaves exactly one thing open, and the one thing he leaves open is the door you may walk through.

**Our research concludes that this placement is composed rather than incidental**, and the ground is the shape of the passage at that point. The phrase stands in the sentence where the asyndeton breaks: from the Prologue's opening each new sentence begins without a connecting particle, except the one that opens verse 12, *but as many as*, as McHugh counts them. It stands just past the point where, as Carson observes, the words handed from line to line give out and the pace rises. It falls inside the two verses Barth hears as the Prologue's first climax. And it is the phrase the Gospel's last sentence answers: *to those who believe in his name* here, *that believing you may have life in his name* at the book's close. None of these was noticed in order to find a door. Together they show that the passage's one opening onto the reader's present was set where the movement turns and where the book's ending looks back. That is held with very high confidence, and it rests on the shape of the passage rather than on any one reader's judgement about it.

**There is a refinement, and it belongs to the teaching rather than qualifying it.** This movement is told from the far side of a completed advent, which is what makes its one opening onto the reader visible at all. The commentary observes that the Prologue's first movement may run the other way, with the light that *shines* in the present and the summary as the completed thing, but it offers that comparison as its own observation, and it turns on the contested reading of *enlightens*. **The slide does not carry this and a teacher does not need it.** It is here because it is why the reading holds: the open phrase is not an accident of Greek but the one opening in a passage told as a finished advent.

*For the teacher, the shape of it in three steps.* First, **read the passage aloud and have them listen for when each thing happens**: came, was, knew not, received, were begotten. Doing it out loud is most of the work, because the pattern is obvious once heard and invisible on the page: this is a story told as done. Second, **stop at "to those who believe" and name what changed.** Not the meaning; the time. Everything before it happened then, and this reaches whoever is listening now. Third, **ask where it sits**: not at the shining, not at the refusal, but at the receiving. That is the part they will still have on Tuesday.

*What this page does not do, said plainly.* It does not exegete verse 12, or take up what the right amounts to, or what it means to become a child of God; each has its own treatment in the commentary. It does not argue what the begetting of verse 13 is. It does not settle the time of *enlightens* at verse 9; the commentary sets that out with the disagreement. It does not claim that the believing is the passage's achievement; the passage keeps God's act for its last word, which is a different point and belongs with it. And **it does not claim the open phrase is hidden.** It is in every translation. The claim is that its placement is deliberate, and that a reader who notices where it falls has understood something about how the passage was built.

## Pedagogical task

This slide's job is to **move one phrase from the middle of a verse to the centre of attention**, not to teach a doctrine of faith. Your people are not confused about "to those who believe"; they have simply never been shown that it is the only thing in the passage still open. So project it and do the one thing it licenses: read the verbs, let them hear the story told as done, and then stop at the one phrase that reaches them. **Do not preach the believing from the front.** The moment this becomes an appeal, the observation is spent and the passage's own restraint is lost — and the restraint is the point. Do not take up what being born of God means; that is a study of its own and it will swallow the time. And **do not oversell it**: this is not a recovered reading. It is the ordinary shape of the passage, seen. **Two downloads, two jobs.** The **16:9 carries the whole move** — the claim, the phrase from the verse, and the reason — and it is the one to project while you teach from it. The **9:16 carries the title and the reference alone**, which makes it the one for a newsletter or a bulletin column with your own writing beneath it.
