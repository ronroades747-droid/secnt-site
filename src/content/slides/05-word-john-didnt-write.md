*All things came to be through him.* Most of us read *all things* as a stock phrase — the large, round word a hymn reaches for when it means "everything" — and read straight past it. But at that point in the sentence John had a choice, and the interesting thing is the word he didn't choose. Greek gave him a perfectly good word for *the world*: ὁ κόσμος, creation taken as one whole, a single bounded thing. John knew the word well; he uses it seven verses later, at verse 10 — *he was in the world, and the world came to be through him*. So *the world* was right there in his hand. At verse 3 he reached past it. What he wrote instead was πάντα: *all things*, with no article in front of it — not τὰ πάντα, "the all," the universe gathered into one closed totality, and not ὁ κόσμος, but the bare plural, which in Greek reckons the total not as one object but thing by thing. Our research concludes that the choice is deliberate and the reading is absolute and universal: every single thing that came to be, each one owing its being to the Word, with no category exempted and no realm of creation left out. *The world came to be through him* lets a hearer picture a globe — one object, made once. *All things came to be through him* will not let the hearer stop there: it is the great and the small, the seen and the unseen, every item in the sum of what exists.

## Pedagogical task

This slide's job is to make your hearers see a choice where they have always seen a cliché — and the choice is an absence, a word that is not in the verse, which is why the headline names it in English and the gold line shows what John wrote in its place. Use it from the pulpit in that order. Read the verse, let *all things* go past as it always does, then stop and say: John had another word, and he didn't use it. Tell them the word — *the world* — and prove it from verse 10, where he does use it. Then point at the gold word and give them the one he chose, and let them feel how much wider it is. The restraint: do not teach the grammar from the front — "no article, so it counts thing by thing" is one sentence, said once, and the rest belongs here for you, not on the screen for them; do not go on to *not even one* in the next clause, which is its own lesson; and do not reach for what the verse grounds — creation from nothing, the Word's lordship over all — which this clause founds but does not yet say. One sentence is the whole move: *show them the word John didn't write, then the one he did.*
