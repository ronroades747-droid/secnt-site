There are two small words in the opening of John that a reader passes over a hundred times, and they are not the same word. In verse 3 the Greek reads δι' αὐτοῦ — *through* him. In verse 4 it reads ἐν αὐτῷ — *in* him. Three lines of English apart, the preposition changes, and most readers hear the two as one pious gesture: the Word had something to do with it.

Our research reads the change as deliberate, and as the hinge on which the passage turns from one register of claim to another. δι' αὐτοῦ is the preposition of agency, and the reach it describes runs outward: all things came to be through him. ἐν αὐτῷ turns the reach inward, to a place rather than a channel — the one in whom what has come into being has its life. Same person in both, and the two are not interchangeable: *through him* answers **how** the creation happened, and *in him* answers **where** it now stands. Made through him, alive in him. What makes the turn worth a slide is its size. It is not a new subject, not a new sentence, not a new argument — it is one word, changed once, at the crossing from verse 3 to verse 4, and the whole reach of the passage turns with it.

*One thing worth knowing before you teach it, because it saves an argument: the two phrases on this slide sit on either side of the place where the Greek sentence's division is discussed, and neither of them is in the disputed part. Wherever a reader puts the break, δι' αὐτοῦ is in verse 3 and ἐν αὐτῷ is in verse 4. So the comparison holds however your hearers' Bibles print the verse, and the slide asks nothing of them on that question.*

*The Greek on the slide is the critical text, each phrase set alone, without the words around it, and the elision mark on* δι' *follows this commentary's own printing. The English renderings above are this commentary's own; the slide itself carries no English beyond its headline.*

## Pedagogical task

This slide's job is to stop the eye on two phrases it has always taken as one. The difference here is not hidden — it is right there in English too — and that is precisely why it gets skipped, so the slide's whole work is arrest rather than revelation: two short phrases, one above the other, each with its verse number under it, and the second one in gold because the second one is the turn. From the pulpit that is a ten-second move and it asks no Greek of anyone: put it up, read the two lines back to back leaning on the preposition each time, and say what each answers — *through him* is how, *in him* is where. In a classroom, use the same frame differently and let the room do the work: put it up, say nothing, and ask what changed between the lines. Somebody will say *through* became *in*, and the discovery is worth more than the telling. The restraint is the discipline. Do not parse the two forms from the front, do not teach Greek prepositions as a topic, and do not push on to what follows from the second phrase — the question of what it means that creation's life is *in him*, rather than merely that it is, is a bigger claim with more behind it, and reaching for it here spends a thing your people can see on an argument they cannot yet weigh. Two phrases, one word apart, doing two different jobs. Leave it there and it will stay with them.
