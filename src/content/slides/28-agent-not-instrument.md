There is a word in John's third verse that quietly costs the Son something every time it is read too quickly. *All things were made through him.* **The ear takes *through* as *by means of*** — God did the making, and the Word was the thing the making was done with. The Father is the craftsman and the Son is the chisel. A clause placed there to say how high the Word stands has become a clause about what he was useful for, and the hearer has done nothing wrong except understand an English preposition the way English usually means it. **What corrects it is not a bigger claim but a more exact one.** The word we reach for — *agent* — is not a gentler word for *tool*. It is the opposite word. Daniel Wallace makes the distinction at the very point where he is refusing a definition that had collapsed the two, and he makes it in a sentence worth keeping: "When agency is indicated, the agent so named is not used by another, but is the one who uses an instrument." **A tool is held. An agent holds.** Wallace's image for what that means at John 1:3 is exactly right — the Logos, he writes, is "represented as the Creator in a 'hands-on' sort of way". Not the Creator at arm's length working through something, but the Creator with his hands in the work. **D. A. Carson puts the same thing in a phrase and then presses on the adjective:** the Word "was God's Agent in the creation of all that exists" and he is "a personal agent" — not a force, not a principle, but someone who acts. Murray Harris, reading the same clause, refuses the picture the misreading depends on outright: "he himself was the agent who caused all things to exist." Our research concludes that *through him* names the Word's agency and not his usefulness, and that a hearer who has been given the difference between an agent and an instrument has been given the whole of what this slide is for.

*For the teacher, the shape of it in three steps.* First, **put the misreading into words before you correct it** — say out loud the thing your people are half-thinking, that *through* sounds like the Father made and the Son was what he made with. Naming it is most of the work, because nobody defends a reading they have heard said aloud. Second, **give the definition, not an argument.** An agent is not the one who is used; he is the one who uses. It is seven words on the wide slide and it does not need a second sentence. Third, **hand them Wallace's picture and stop** — hands in the work, not at arm's length. It is the part they will still have on Tuesday.

*What this page does not do, said plainly.* It does not parse the preposition, and it deliberately does not claim that Greek formally marks an agent off from an instrument — Wallace's own lexical entry lists *διά* with the genitive under both agency and means, he calls his table of agency "a general guideline, not an absolute rule" and he says of John 1:3 that its pattern is "typical (though not exclusive)" rather than a rule. **The weight here rests on what the word *agent* means, not on what a case ending proves.** It does not take up the scope of *all things*, or the negative clause that seals the verse, or the tense of *came to be*. It does not set out the kinds of agency the New Testament distinguishes. And it does not reach for the creed, or for what the church later built on this verse. **It hands over one distinction and stops there.**

## Pedagogical task

This slide's job is to **change what one word sounds like**, not to teach a doctrine of agency. Your people are not confused about John 1:3; they are hearing *through* the way English uses it every day, and what fixes that is a definition rather than an argument. So project it and do the one thing it licenses: read the denial off the screen — *the Word was not what God used* — and then give them the reason in the seven words underneath it. *A tool is held. An agent holds.* Then stop. **The restraint is most of the discipline here.** Do not parse the preposition from the front: the moment you start explaining a Greek case you have made this a lesson about grammar and lost the people the slide was for. Do not lay out the kinds of agency — that belongs in a classroom and it is not what this slide does. And **do not reach for the tidy version**, however well it scans: *means is impersonal, agency is personal* is a real grammarian's sentence, and Wallace prints it in order to correct it as "a little too general." A teacher who repeats it has taught the wrong thing with confidence. **Two downloads, two jobs.** The **wide slide carries the whole move** — the denial, the Greek, and the reason — and it is the one to project while you teach from it. The **tall slide carries the denial and the Greek without the reason**, which makes it the one for a bulletin, a newsletter column or an invitation: it says the surprising thing and sends the reader to find out why.
