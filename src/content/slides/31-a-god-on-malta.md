Sooner or later someone tells you that John's opening line should read *and the Word was a god*, and the instinct is to answer that their Greek is wrong. **The instinct is the weaker move**, and it is worth knowing that before you need it rather than after. At the level of grammar alone the rendering is available — and there is a place in the New Testament where it is the right one. In Acts 28 Paul is shipwrecked on Malta; a viper fastens on his hand as he lays brushwood on the fire, he shakes it off, and the islanders — who had been waiting for him to swell up and fall down dead — conclude that he is a god. Murray Harris says that "a god" is there **"a proper translation,"** and it is: those islanders had a pantheon, and their world had room on it for one more. Our research concludes, with the field's commentary and grammar at near-unanimity behind the reading of John's clause, that what rules the same rendering out of the Prologue is **not the words but the frame they stand in**. John writes inside Israel's confession that God is one, and for a monotheist the singular can name one being and no other — there is no shelf of lesser deities to set the Word on. **So the rendering that fits Malta perfectly has nowhere to stand in John 1:1.** It fails, and it fails on the world the sentence is spoken in.

*For the teacher, the shape of it in three steps.* First, **the concession** — say out loud that the rendering is not bad Greek, and that there is a verse where it is the right translation. Almost nobody expects that, and it is what makes everything after it audible. Second, **Malta**: tell Acts 28 as a story, because the pantheon has to be felt rather than asserted — those men had a shelf, and a man who shakes off a viper goes on it. Third, **the frame**: John writes where God is one, and a shelf is exactly what he does not have. **The order matters.** Grant first, and the person in front of you stops defending and starts listening; correct first, and you have spent the conversation on ground you did not need to fight for.

*What this page does not do, said plainly.* It does not teach the Greek article, and it does not explain why the second clause of John 1:1 says *the God* and the third says simply *God* — that mechanism is its own lesson and it has its own slide. It does not run the grammatical half of the case: there is a further argument, from the shape of the construction itself, that the clause is positively saying something about the Word's nature, and it belongs to the commentary rather than to a two-minute short. It does not name a translation, or the group best known for the rendering — **the moment a group is named the conversation stops being about the sentence.** And it does not claim that grammar is beside the point; it claims something narrower and more useful, that grammar alone does not settle this one, and that the frame does.

## Pedagogical task

This slide's job is to **spend a concession in order to buy a hearing**. Project it when the "a god" rendering comes up, and open by agreeing — the rendering is good Greek, and there is a verse where it is right. **The gold word hands your hearers the thing actually under discussion**, so the claim rests on the word rather than on you; the two grey lines beneath it on the wide slide say what makes Malta different. The restraint is most of the discipline here. **Do not explain the article from the front** — the small word that carries the guard in the Greek is a real point and it is not this one. **Do not bring in the grammatical survey** that carries the rest of the case; it is on the commentary page, and reaching for it here turns a concession into a lecture. **Do not name a translation or a group**, however tempting the shorthand — you will have changed the subject from a sentence to a movement, and you will not get it back. One sentence is the whole move: *grant the grammar, then ask what frame the sentence is standing in.* **Two downloads, two jobs.** The **wide slide carries the whole move** — the concession, the word, and underneath them what makes the two frames different — and it is the one to project while you teach. The **tall slide carries the concession and the word without the reason**, which makes it the one for a bulletin, a newsletter column or an invitation: it says the surprising thing and sends the reader to find out why.
