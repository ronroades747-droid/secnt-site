Read John 1:10–12 slowly, and listen only for the verbs. *He was in the world, and the world came to be through him, and the world did not know him. He came to his own, and his own did not receive him. But as many as received him, to them he gave the right to become children of God.* Most of us hear two different kinds of event: a long refusal, and then a moment of faith. And a sermon on the refusal will often lean on the tense. The past tense, we are told, makes the rejection decisive, once for all.

**Five verbs, told the same way.** Did not know. Came. Did not receive. Received. Gave. All five stand in the same Greek tense, and all five do the same thing with it: each takes an action whole and sets it down as one thing, however long it took. Carson states the principle in his note on verse 5: the tense "merely establishes that the writer looks on the action holistically." Schnackenburg, of *came* and *did not know*: "These aorists merely reflect the fact that the encounter between the Logos and the world took place in the reality of history." The refusal of verse 11 and the reception of verse 12 are composed as a matching pair.

**So the crisis is not in the tense. It is in the story.** Some careful readers hear more in these verbs. Morris, at verse 11: "It fastens our attention on the crisis rather than on the continuing result." The crisis is real. But it is what the verbs point to once the rest of the Gospel is read into them: the refusal reaching its crisis at the close of Jesus' public ministry (12:37), and the reception reaching its fullness in the community he loved to the end (13:1) and in those who have not seen and yet believe (20:29). Brown sees the shape from the other side: verses 11 and 12 "are really short summaries of the two parts of the Gospel: the Book of Signs and the Book of Glory."

**And the last of the five is his.** Read the verbs with their subjects. The world did not know him. He came. His own did not receive him. As many as received him — and then, he gave. The five run from the world's refusal to the Word's gift, and they do not end on anything done with him. They end on what he gave: the right to become children of God.

**Our research concludes that the five verbs are told alike, and that the decisiveness some readers hear in them is the Gospel's, not the grammar's.** That is held with very high confidence.

*For the teacher, the shape of it in three steps.* First, **read verses 10 to 12 aloud and have the room call out the five verbs** as they hear them. Second, **ask which of them was the crisis**, and then show them that John tells all five the same way; send them to 12:37 and 13:1, where the Gospel tells the crisis and the fullness at length. Third, **put the slide up and read the last verb.** From refusal to gift. The last of the five is his.

*What this page does not do, said plainly.* It does not take up what the right is, or what it means to become a child of God; the commentary on verse 12 does. It does not take up the begetting of verse 13, or the phrase "to those who believe"; each has its own page in the commentary. It does not say the refusal was not a crisis. It was; the claim is about where the crisis is told. And it does not set out what the church confesses about the world that did not know its Maker; that is where the commentary goes next.

## Pedagogical task

This slide's job is to **let five verbs be heard as one movement.** Most rooms hear a refusal and then a conversion, two different kinds of thing. **Let them hear the five told alike** — each a whole, each finished — before anyone draws a lesson from the tense. **Then move the crisis to where John tells it**, in the Gospel's story rather than in the grammar. **Land on the last verb.** "Gave" is not one more event in a list; it is the Word's act, and the movement stops there. **Leave the Greek terms out of the room.** The point is the movement and where it ends, not the name of the tense. **Two downloads, two jobs.** The **16:9 carries the whole move** — the headline, the gold line and the five verbs beneath it — and it is the one to teach from. The **9:16 carries the title and the reference alone**, for a newsletter or a bulletin column with your own writing beneath it.
