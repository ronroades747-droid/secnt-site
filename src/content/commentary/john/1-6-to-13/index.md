The unit is John 1:6–13, read on the working text this commentary has settled — the critical editions' common text, adopted without emendation:[^text]

> ⁶ Ἐγένετο ἄνθρωπος, ἀπεσταλμένος παρὰ θεοῦ, ὄνομα αὐτῷ Ἰωάννης· ⁷ οὗτος ἦλθεν εἰς μαρτυρίαν ἵνα μαρτυρήσῃ περὶ τοῦ φωτός, ἵνα πάντες πιστεύσωσιν δι᾽ αὐτοῦ. ⁸ οὐκ ἦν ἐκεῖνος τὸ φῶς, ἀλλ᾽ ἵνα μαρτυρήσῃ περὶ τοῦ φωτός. ⁹ Ἦν τὸ φῶς τὸ ἀληθινόν, ὃ φωτίζει πάντα ἄνθρωπον, ἐρχόμενον εἰς τὸν κόσμον. ¹⁰ ἐν τῷ κόσμῳ ἦν, καὶ ὁ κόσμος δι᾽ αὐτοῦ ἐγένετο, καὶ ὁ κόσμος αὐτὸν οὐκ ἔγνω. ¹¹ εἰς τὰ ἴδια ἦλθεν, καὶ οἱ ἴδιοι αὐτὸν οὐ παρέλαβον. ¹² ὅσοι δὲ ἔλαβον αὐτόν, ἔδωκεν αὐτοῖς ἐξουσίαν τέκνα θεοῦ γενέσθαι, τοῖς πιστεύουσιν εἰς τὸ ὄνομα αὐτοῦ, ¹³ οἳ οὐκ ἐξ αἱμάτων οὐδὲ ἐκ θελήματος σαρκὸς οὐδὲ ἐκ θελήματος ἀνδρὸς ἀλλ᾽ ἐκ θεοῦ ἐγεννήθησαν.
>
> ⁶ There came a man, sent from God; his name was John. ⁷ He came for witness — to bear witness to the light, so that all might believe through him. ⁸ He was not the light; he came to bear witness to the light. ⁹ He — the Word — was the true light, the light that illumines every human being, coming into the world. ¹⁰ He was in the world, and the world came to be through him — and yet the world did not know him. ¹¹ He came to what was his own, and his own people did not welcome him. ¹² But as many as received him — to them he gave the right to become children of God; that is, to those who believe in his name, ¹³ who were begotten — not of bloods, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of a husband — but of God.

The rendering presupposes what the exegesis argues, and each presupposition is placed here so the reader knows where it is carried. The text is the editions' common text; the unit's one crux — the plural οἳ … ἐγεννήθησαν of verse 13 against the singular carried in a thin Latin and patristic tradition — is resolved for the plural in the treatment of the text. The Word, not every human being, is the subject of Ἦν … ἐρχόμενον in verse 9 — *was … coming* — and the English makes the subject explicit where the Greek carries it by continuity (see the treatment of verse 9). "Did not welcome" renders οὐ παρέλαβον at the contextual force the exegesis adopts, the compound verb's lexicon left unburdened (verse 11). "Right" renders ἐξουσία, not the Authorized Version's "power" (verse 12). "Begotten," not "born," renders ἐγεννήθησαν under the convention this volume keeps, and "bloods" keeps the plural the Greek has rather than smoothing it to "natural descent" (verse 13). The dashes in verses 10 and 12 carry relations the exegesis argues — the concessive turn of verse 10's first two clauses toward its third, and the suspended construction of verse 12 that foregrounds the receivers — and not a syntax the Greek lacks; the comma at the join of verses 12 and 13 records that the two verses are one sentence.

The unit opens where a person's aorist opens it and closes where the begetting closes it, and the evangelist built both edges. Ἐγένετο ἄνθρωπος is narrative style set against the ἦν that has governed verses 1–5 — the first time the Prologue's verb of *becoming* is applied to a person and, on [the spine of tenses the Prologue runs](/commentary/john/1-1-to-3/03-macro-tense-spine/), the first person-application of its ἦν / ἐγένετο discrimination. At the other edge, verses 12–13 close as one sentence, and after them the Prologue's only καί-connective since verse 12 opens something new: ὁ λόγος returns, in Barth's observation, "for the first time since v. 1," and with verse 14, in Ridderbos's, "the third and last segment of the three-part cycle begins."[^edges] Eight traditions set a seam after verse 13 and nine set the movement's opening at verse 6 — Ridderbos and Schnackenburg from opposite sides of the hymn question, Barth hearing at verses 12–13 "a first climax in the prologue" and saying that were he dividing the Prologue he would break it after verse 13 — and the seams are the evangelist's.[^seams] That the movement begins by taking up the first movement's last word is not a blurring of the edge but the shape of a cycle in which, as Ridderbos puts it, the categories of the first come back: the darkness that in verse 5 neither knew nor overcame the light becomes, here, the world that did not know him and his own who did not welcome him.[^cycle] This is the backward face of the hinge at verse 9, subordinate to its forward face, and received at the threshold as the same face.

Inside its edges the unit is one movement in three beats, with one turn and one open door. The first beat is the parenthesis, verses 6–8: a man came, sent, to bear witness to a light he was not. The second is the light's coming to its own, verses 9–11, a single crescendo in three verbs — ἐρχόμενον → ἦν → ἦλθεν, *coming*, *was in the world*, *came to his own* — a crescendo Westcott hears in the verbs, *was, was in the world, came to His own*, which this commentary reads from ἐρχόμενον forward, and Ridderbos in the argument, verse 11 being the place where "the prologue has reached its low point."[^crescendo] The third is the reception's turn, verses 12–13, and the turn is marked in the text's own grammar: ὅσοι δέ is the unit's only interior connective, every other sentence from verse 1 to verse 11 having opened by asyndeton, and it is where the Prologue's catchword chain ends and, in Carson's phrase, the rise in pace adds forward movement.[^turn] At the movement's three joints stand three figures the exegesis establishes one by one before the unit is seen whole: one refusal thrice told, across verses 9–11 and reaching back to verse 5 (verse 11); the verb-pair across the verse boundary, οὐ παρέλαβον answered by ἔλαβον (verse 12); and the οὐκ … οὐδὲ … οὐδὲ … ἀλλά triad at the close, answering the refusal-series member for member (verse 13). Read for its content, the three-beat movement is the spine of this commentary's exegesis and the spine of its lectures — *witness → coming and refusal → reception and begetting* — as the four treads were for [John 1:4–5](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/). One discrimination governs throughout and is stated here so it need not be re-argued at each verse: the unit is **deliberate composition, not deliberate verse**. Here that discrimination has a sequel the first movement did not need. These verses are prose set between hymnic registers, and the change of register at verse 6 — hymnic, then narrative, then hymnic again — is received as composed contrast, the alternation of cadence that the evangelist writes with, not the seam of a source he interrupted; the interpolation and displacement readings are declined under the discipline this commentary keeps of receiving an observation while declining the explanation offered for it.[^register]

## Why This Passage Matters

This is where the Prologue turns. The first movement said what the light is and that the darkness had not overcome it; it deliberately withheld the drama of reception — who received the light, who did not, and in what mode. These eight verses tell it. The darkness of verse 5 is shown refusing the light in the concrete: the world that came to be through him did not know him; his own people, the covenant household inside the estate that was his, did not welcome him. And then, at a single δέ, the refusal is answered — not extended into a fourth negation but *answered* — by those who received him and were given the right to become God's children, begotten of God. The passage matters, first, because the Gospel's whole story of belief and unbelief is composed here in miniature: the turn at verses 11 | 12 is the Gospel's own turn, "his own did not welcome him" over the Book of Signs and "as many as received him" over the Book of Glory, a correspondence six traditions register and the Gospel confirms in the two summaries it sets at its own seams (12:37–41; 20:30–31) — and the unit's last phrase, τοῖς πιστεύουσιν εἰς τὸ ὄνομα αὐτοῦ, is answered by the Gospel's last sentence, πιστεύοντες ζωὴν ἔχητε ἐν τῷ ὀνόματι αὐτοῦ (20:31). It matters, second, because of the one thing in it that is not past. The unit narrates a completed advent from this side of it — a man came, the light was coming, came, was not known, was not welcomed, was received; children were begotten — and leaves one door open, at the believing and nowhere else: the present participle of verse 12c, in Harris's words, "open to those who live after John's time."[^harris] For the reader and the teacher alike the unit sets before both a single composed movement: a witness sent to a light he was not; the true light coming to what was its own and refused there; and, at the turn, the reception that God's own begetting makes children of — with the door held open for whoever believes. What the passage does not do is also part of what it is. It does not say the Word became flesh; that is verse 14's, the summit the Prologue's frame reaches at its far pole, and the next unit's. And it does not say *how* God's begetting meets the believing it is coincident with; verse 13 names the source and withholds the mode, and the Gospel supplies the mode at 3:5–8. Both are held where the text holds them.

## How to Read This Commentary

The commentary is organized in seven sections. The first — this one — establishes the passage: its text, its edges and its shape, and why it matters. The second records the framework inheritances under which the unit has been read: what the settled Prologue architecture and the treatment of [John 1:4–5](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/) hand forward, and the one calibration, settled inside this unit, under which the unit's imperfects are read. The third names the primary sources and the discipline under which they were engaged at every register, including the text-critical judgment that governs the patristic evidence. The fourth articulates the unit's compositional architecture at six layers — the bounded movement, its articulation, its temporal payload, its frame, its Gospel-facing register, and its aperture — as the foundation on which the verse-level exegesis operates; it is placed before the exegesis because the exegesis leans on it at every seam and at every verb. The fifth is the exegesis: the text-critical notes, then each verse in turn under the three beats it composes, and a closing sub-section that gathers the unit's tenses back onto the spine once the reader has met each verb. The sixth draws out the theological implications as discrete loci. The seventh closes with the unit's pastoral hearing and its forward arc into the Prologue's third movement and the Gospel. A record of the scholarship engaged, with the works consulted and an inventory of the register at which each source was brought to bear, follows at the close. Throughout, the apparatus is carried in the footnotes, and the readings on which the exposition stands are the project's settled conclusions, set out here rather than re-argued. Where a section rests on one of those conclusions, the page carries that conclusion's own apparatus — the rival readings, the voices engaged against — at the depth the section needs to read as argued rather than asserted, and no further: it introduces no engagement the underlying work did not raise, routes no claim past a recorded silence, and where it would conflict with that work in substance, the settled conclusion carries the presumption.

## Framework Inheritances

This commentary inherits the project's standing framework for the Johannine Prologue, the settled readings of John 1:1–5 that hand directly into these eight verses, and one calibration settled inside this unit under which half the unit's verbs are read. Naming the inheritance is part of the commentary's scholarly disclosure: the reader is given the framework under which the unit has been read, the points at which 1:6–13 stands on work already done, and the one point at which the unit's own conclusions are held at a cap that later work will move.

### Linear architecture, and the unit's place

The Prologue is read under the Ridderbos/Morris three-movement linear architecture: 1:1–5 (the Word in the beginning), 1:6–13 (the Word coming as light), 1:14–18 (the Word's glory in the flesh). Where John 1:4–5 completed the first movement, John 1:6–13 *is* the second — one composed movement bounded at Ἐγένετο ἄνθρωπος and ἐκ θεοῦ ἐγεννήθησαν, its edges set by eight traditions at the seam after verse 13 and nine at the opening at verse 6 (see the treatment of the bounded movement). The chiastic alternative (Culpepper, Boismard, Staley, with centres variously at 1:12–13 or 1:14) is no longer only acknowledged. Culpepper's scheme places the Prologue's centre at 12b, and this unit has engaged it at the unit register: the weight is there — verses 12–13 are, in Barth's word, the Prologue's "first climax," and the Gospel's own turn is composed at verses 11 | 12 — but the shape is not.[^climax] The evidence is a turn in a linear movement, marked by the unit's only interior connective and by the verb-pair across the verse boundary, not a pivot in a concentric one (see the treatment of the Gospel-facing register). The 1:14-centred schemes still await the next unit, where verse 14 is the natural pivot; until then the linear framework stands as the working architecture, now with two of its three movements seated by the commentary's own adjudication.

### The Foyer frame

The Prologue is read under Carson's *foyer* image — the entrance hall through which the reader passes before stepping into the Gospel proper — and the strong form of the claim, that the Prologue originates rather than summarizes the Gospel's themes, is held provisionally, as it was for John 1:4–5. The unit's placement in that frame has changed with its position. John 1:4–5 stood at the foyer's far pole; John 1:6–13 stands *enclosed*: outside the verse 1 / verse 18 and verse 3 / verse 18 brackets of [the foyer-bracket architecture](/commentary/john/1-1-to-3/02-foyer-bracket/), and held between the poles of the verse 4 / verse 14 pair — the life-and-light on one side, the glory beheld on the other — so that the second movement is what the foyer's inner pair holds between its poles (see the treatment of the frame). Barth says the same from the other side, in the descending ladder he sees the Prologue's framework in — "The Word with God, the Word and the world, the Word among men, the Word itself flesh" — with these verses on its third rung, the Word among men, by this commentary's own placement, and adding that the descent "forms the framework of consideration" and not the whole: which is the reason the frame is a frame and not a coupling.[^ladder] That the verse 4 / verse 14 pair is a deliberately composed inclusio remains at high but provisional confidence, its confirmation reserved for the treatment of 1:14, and the movement's Prologue-wide seating is provisional with it. That provisionality is stated here once and is not repeated at each layer of the architecture.

### What John 1:4–5 hands forward

The first movement is not only architecture inherited but substance deposited, and three deposits are taken up. First, the darkness of verse 5, which neither knew nor overcame the light, is here shown refusing it in the concrete — and the evangelist binds the two movements by their vocabulary: κατέλαβεν → παρέλαβον → ἔλαβον, *did not grasp* → *did not welcome* → *received*, the -λαβ- chain that Boyarin reads across the Prologue and that the earlier treatment received as evidence of integrated composition; the οὐκ ἔγνω of verse 10 and the οὐ παρέλαβον of verse 11 are the first movement's οὐ κατέλαβεν told again, in the concrete and in the covenant.[^chain] Second, the universal scope of [the light *of men*](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/07-anthropology-light-of-men/) — the guard the earlier treatment set and expressly declined to narrow toward Israel — is confirmed from below twice in this unit: at verse 11, where the verse's own syntax carries the discrimination that *Israel-first is not Israel-only*, and at verse 12, where ὅσοι bounds the receivers by reception and not by lineage. Third, the two structural parallels the unit draws: its temporal reference stands to [the run of tenses in John 1:4–5](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/03-macro-tense-run/) as that unit's did to its own, and its unit-synthesis to the earlier unit's — and the spine those two syntheses instantiate, ἦν / ἐγένετο / ὃ γέγονεν, runs here on its first two terms only. No ὃ γέγονεν stands in 1:6–13; the second movement runs on being and coming alone, a datum about the Prologue's design recorded for the volume's introduction and not settled here.

### The working calibration — presence-as-advent

One calibration, settled at verse 9 and carried through the unit, governs how the unit's imperfects are read, and it is stated here once so that the exegesis can invoke it by name. The working calibration reads the light's presence as *presence-as-advent*: the light was always present to the world it made, and its coming is one continuous advent whose salvation-historical sharings — through creation, through the covenant career, through the prophets — are the advent's body, with fullness of perception reached at the glorification. The terminus is perceptual and confessional, never ontological: the hour of 12:32, and Thomas's confession at 20:28 closing the arc 1:1 and 1:18 open, with 20:29 as the guard (see the treatment of verse 9). Under it, ἦν at verse 10 is presence-as-advent (verse 10); ἦλθεν at verse 11 is one advent, its covenant career through the prophets a quiet anticipation of verse 14 as the trajectory's pointing (verse 11); the status of God's children at verse 12 is not confined to the enfleshed arrival (verse 12); the interior imperfects of verses 8–10 reach beneath the unit's aorist frame across the whole history of the light's presence, so that the Word is coming from verse 9 and arrives at verse 11 with no single verse the threshold (the unit's tenses); and the verses 9–11 movement is read as one. Every one of these is held with high confidence *and capped* — the cap being the calibration's own ground, which the treatment of 1:14 reassesses, where the enfleshment the calibration reaches toward is adjudicated. This commentary carries the cap; the count of the calibration's instances belongs to the project's internal record.

### Intra-Johannine restraint, with the permitted forward gestures

The exegesis treats the unit on its own terms, permitting forward gestures only where a settled reading made one. Those the readings made are listed with their locus, and no others are taken. 1:19–51 — the narrative of the Baptist's witness that the parenthesis anticipates, so that, in Bultmann's against-interest observation, the later narrative reads as commentary on 1:6–8 (verses 6–8).[^bultmann-narrative] 5:43 and 13:20 — the Gospel's own authority-sense of *receiving* a person, which fixes what ἔλαβον means at verse 12. 2:23–24 — the guard that believing in the name is not self-certifying, its power from its object (verse 12). 3:5–8 — where the Gospel supplies the *mode* of the begetting verse 13 names the source of and withholds (verse 13; the fourth locus). 12:32 and 20:28–29 — the working calibration's terminus and its guard. 12:37–41 and 20:30–31 — the Gospel's two seam-summaries, in which the turn composed at verses 11 | 12 is realized as the Gospel's own, and 20:31's πιστεύοντες … ἐν τῷ ὀνόματι αὐτοῦ answering 12c (the Gospel-facing register). And 13:1 — where οἱ ἴδιοι returns and the household that refused him is replaced by the household he loves, the reversal of verse 11's wordplay — a claim at the Gospel's register, *held provisional* outside this volume's scope and named as such wherever it appears. The Word's becoming flesh (1:14) and the glory beheld are reserved to the next unit without exception.

### Inter-canonical permission, governed by substantive illumination

Canonical material beyond the Gospel enters only where a settled reading brought it in, and one permission is expressly withheld. The idiom of sacred narrative — Ἐγένετο ἄνθρωπος as the Septuagint's way of introducing a person into a story — is the vehicle of verse 6's register-shift, the composed effect being the spine's discrimination and not the idiom itself (verses 6–8). Torah and Wisdom, the lights Israel called true, are the historically specific instance behind τὸ ἀληθινόν, the archetypal sense being settled at the broader logical register with the instance named and weighted (verse 9). The perfects of 1 John's γεγέννηται supply the *state*-aspect of the begetting where the Gospel's aorist supplies the act, the extension bound at that (the unit's tenses). Second John 1 is admitted at verse 13 only as Barth's stated ground for his construal of the antecedent, and at no wider register (verse 13). And the permission withheld: no Genesis-frame claim is made at verse 13. Where [the Genesis frame](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/08-cosmology-light-in-darkness/) was foundational to 1:4–5, this unit found no warrant to extend it to the begetting — Menken, the corpus's Genesis-in-John authority, has no 1:13 entry and treats none of verses 9–17 against Genesis, and the negative is honoured.[^menken]

### Patristic engagement at primary-text register

The Prologue's Greek reception is load-bearing at the registers this unit engaged it. The **supersession principle** (described under the sources below) operates at two instances here. At the 1:13 crux, Gregory of Nyssa is an adaptation-only witness — he cites the verse's substance in forms adapted to his argument and never its wording — so that the editor's own collation sets the bound on what his text can be said to attest, and the published secondary treatment is superseded in both directions, where it claims too much and where it claims too little (the treatment of the text). At verse 9, Gregory's citation of the true light and the adaptation-chain that runs verse 5 → verse 9 are read as christological reception of the verse — early evidence that the light's coming was heard as the Word's — with the narrower published net carrying no claim (verse 9).[^nyssen] The **singular reading** of verse 13 — ὃς … ἐγεννήθη, the birth predicated of the Word — has a patristic career that the text-critical decision does not erase: it enters as a reception datum, the early christological hearing of the verse and its resonance with the virginal conception, and its disposition is made where the verse is expounded — no birth-allusion asserted, the correspondence real and explaining why the church heard one, and Galot's internal pressure answered on the plural text without emending it (verse 13).[^galot] Cyril of Alexandria holds both bounds of the begetting — a real change of kind, and no participation in the divine being — and gives the christological reason for both. Chrysostom supplies the mechanics by which a universal light and actual unbelief coexist. Augustine and the Fathers, with Westcott and Calvin after them, read the unit's verbs as *phases* of the Word's activity, and that reading is placed as the historically specific instance of the temporal reference this commentary settles at the broader register. Origen, engaged through Heine's edition, carries the parenthesis's earliest sustained reading and is a load-bearing negative at verse 9; the *Ancient Christian Commentary* enters at the clause-reception register, as before.[^origen]

### Held open to revision

This commentary is a permanent scholarly artifact, held open to retroactive refinement under the project's standing procedure for it. It composes settled readings, each under its own governance, and the discipline needs no running discussion. What it does need is the naming of this unit's live pressure points, so the reader knows where refinement is expected and in which direction. The first is the working calibration's cap: every conclusion downstream of it moves with the reassessment at 1:14, in either direction, and the expected direction of the unit's Prologue-wide seating is upward as the third movement is seated. The second and third are assessments owed on the record and not changes: Barth's four pages on the 1:13 crux stand against the settled text-critical decision as an item held for later assessment, and Brown's frame for the Spirit's begetting — "there was as yet no Spirit" until the glorification, 7:39 — presses on the capped finding that the status of God's children is not confined to the enfleshed arrival; neither is resolved here, and the treatment of 1:14, which owns the calibration's terminus, is where both are assessed.[^brown-spirit] The fourth is apparatus, and it is already applied: this commentary carries Barth's break-after-verse-13 sentence at p. 69, where the page-marker places it (the verses 12–13 lemma opening at the foot of p. 68), and carries Schnackenburg's three-section statement as the architecture's second load-bearing modern carrier, a final-form positive found inside a source-critical section — both findings made at the unit-architecture stage.[^apparatus]

## Sources and Methodological Discipline

The apparatus is layered, and at every conclusion the layer at which each source is brought to bear is disclosed. Five layers operate. Full substantive engagement with the source read directly at the load-bearing locus is the highest register. A source may instead be engaged directly at a supporting register — a critical apparatus, a reference grammar, a standard reference work; through its own summary articulation, which carries the author's authority but not the underlying analysis; through another scholar's citation rather than the source read directly; or substantively on an adjacent locus while remaining at apparatus level on the present question. The reader can therefore see, at every conclusion, both which sources bear on the result and what kind of evidence each supplies. A full inventory of the unit's sources by layer accompanies the engagement with prior scholarship at the close.

### The commentators read directly

Across the unit a fixed core carried the adjudication at the highest register, and one commentary entered the corpus for the first time. Schnackenburg remains the load-bearing modern source: he holds the Baptist verses integral to the Prologue as the evangelist's deliberate placement, against interest, since his own source theory would have let him say otherwise; his aspect-objection controls the construal of verse 9; he declines the composed shift of sense in κόσμος; he presses the real-capacity objection at ἐξουσία, which the commentary answered from the figure rather than outvoted, and which is now known to rest on his developed account of faith as grace in his excursus on the notion of faith, read whole before the verse 12 reading was settled; and inside his source-critical treatment of the Prologue and the Logos-hymn he states, on the final form, that one should rather distinguish three sections — verses 1–5, verses 6–13, verses 14–16 or 18 — a positive found by reading him whole and consumed as the architecture's second load-bearing carrier.[^schnack] McHugh supplies the unit's fullest dossiers: the referent of δι᾽ αὐτοῦ; the distance-datum that keeps verse 9's construal loose rather than strictly periphrastic; the τὰ ἴδια / οἱ ἴδιοι dossier, engaged convergently at four registers and against at one; the *Great Trial* reading of the witness-office; the asyndeton datum that marks the turn; the Curetonian Syriac's incoherence at the crux; and, at the third movement's threshold, the perduring witness of verse 15.[^mchugh] Barth stands at the highest register in seven of the unit's nine settled readings, more often engaged-against than adopted, and at full force either way: against, at the restrictive πάντα of verse 9 and at σάρξ as hostile in verse 13; adopted, at the witness-office defined by its own negation (contra Bauer), at *right* for ἐξουσία, where he and Calvin concur across four centuries, and at the Word's giving as concentration rather than exclusion; ### Methodological discipline

Seven disciplines govern the composition, four carried from John 1:4–5 and three this unit added or matured. First, **final-form composition**: the unit is read as the evangelist's deliberate final-form work; the hymn-*Vorlage* and interpolation readings (Bultmann, Schnackenburg, Brown, Lincoln; Bauer's displacement) are surveyed at the diachronic register and not adopted, and the register-break at verse 6 is received as composed contrast. Second, the **receive-the-observation / decline-the-explanation** move, at its fifth, sixth and seventh instances in this unit: the source-critics' seams at 6 | 9 and 12b | 12c are received as seams and their insertion-explanation declined; Bultmann's gloss at verse 9 received and his hymn layer declined; the phases reading's observation received and its frame declined. Third, the **co-articulation of the synchronic and the diachronic**, matured from a candidate to a settled pattern at this unit's close on its second instance, with its definition refined: two dimensions of one composition tracing the same treads on distinct witnesses, an offset of one clause admitted at a hinge, co-location of the present-aperture not required (see the treatment of the co-articulation). Fourth, **the source record as the consuming interface**: each source's routing and its recorded silences are drawn from the project's per-volume record of what each source treats, no claim is routed to a source recorded as silent at the locus, and two corrections to that record made at the unit-architecture stage are applied here — Barth's break-after-verse-13 sentence at p. 69, and Schnackenburg's three-section statement routed to structure where the record had routed the span to hymn-theory only. Fifth, the **predicate rule**, first settled at verse 10: where one noun stands under several aspects, the aspect is carried by the clause's predicate and never by the noun, so that ὁ κόσμος keeps one referent across three clauses and the exegesis reads the verbs for the sense. Sixth, the rule that **the word does not carry what the passage carries**, settled as a pattern at verse 13 after the reception reading first reached for it: a lexeme is not made to bear a sense the surrounding composition supplies — σάρξ is not hostile because the Gospel elsewhere sets flesh against Spirit, αἵματα is not "descent" because descent is what the context is about, and the decisiveness some hear in the reception aorists is the Gospel's story and not the grammar. Seventh, the **dual-posture engagement**: a reading received at one register and declined at another, in the same breath and with the register named — Carson's prolepsis at verse 10 received as compositional and declined as sense, Barth's restrictive πάντα at verse 9 engaged-against while his ground is admitted. Above all, the governing discrimination stated at the outset holds throughout — the unit is *deliberate composition, not deliberate verse*, and here prose composed between hymnic registers.
