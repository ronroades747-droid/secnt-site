Here the Prologue turns. After three refusals — [the darkness that did not grasp](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/09-soteriology-not-overcome/), [the world that did not know](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/12-world-did-not-know/), [his own who did not welcome](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/13-his-own-welcome-refused/) — a single particle, δέ, and a single repeated verb turn the movement: ὅσοι δὲ ἔλαβον αὐτόν, *but as many as received him*. The verse then says what they were given, what they became, who gave it, and who they are. Seven things are fixed on this page: the verb-pair across the verse boundary and what "received" means; who the receivers are; what ἐξουσία is; what τέκνα says and what "adoption" is doing; who gives; what the irregular opening construction does, and whether it says anything about number; and what believing in his name is.

## The pair is composed, and receiving is believing

Two independent lines show the evangelist composing verse 12 to answer verse 11.

The connective is marked. Köstenberger observes that John continues "now not with an adversative καί but with the adversative δέ."[^eec-de] Harris, compiling for another purpose an inventory of the Prologue's adversative καί, lists "1:5b, 10c, 11" — a list he gives as illustrative, "not exhaustive."[^harris-kai] And at verse 12 the adversative καί is not used.

And the verb is repeated. There is a classical idiom, noted by Moulton, in which the preposition of a compound verb is dropped without weakening the sense *when the verb is repeated*. McHugh, Harris and Barrett all cite it here, Harris with a double hedge, and Zerwick states the carry-over without the condition.[^moulton] So ἔλαβον and παρέλαβον mean the same thing, not despite the composition but because of it: the simple verb is what a repeated compound becomes. Michaels says the same from the other side — the latter "echoes the former and reinforces the contrast between the two clauses. The 'contradiction' is deliberate".[^michaels-deliberate] Schnackenburg says it from a third side, twice: "This dialectical procedure and the expression λαμβάνειν τινά show clearly the hand of the evangelist," and, in his excursus on faith, that 1:11 "is taken up by the evangelist in 12" with "ἔλαβον for the compound παρέλαβον."[^schnackenburg-hand] Bultmann dissents on the mechanism — for him the interchange is a Semitism rather than Greek usage — and reaches the same conclusion, that the verbs are interchangeable.[^bultmann-semitism]

What ἔλαβον asserts is then a finding about usage, not a lexical gloss. Three independent sources land on one verse, and a fourth concurs on another's authority. For McHugh, to receive him is to accept his authority, "as in Jn 5:43; 13:20, where the verb is equated with 'to believe'."[^mchugh-believe] For Bultmann, receiving him at 5:43 is believing.[^bultmann-receive] Köstenberger writes that "Receiving is believing," restating a sentence whose note cites Schnackenburg.[^eec-receiving] And Schnackenburg twice: the expression is peculiar to John and recurs in the same sense at 5:43 and 13:20, and to receive Jesus's words and accept them inwardly is to accept the revealer himself along with his revelation "and so to 'believe' as John understands it."[^schnackenburg-believe]

McHugh takes a further step: that the identity with παραλαμβάνω imports the reception of authoritative teaching handed down by living tradition.[^mchugh-tradition] That step is declined at its premise, since the compound carried no such freight at [verse 11](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/13-his-own-welcome-refused/). Barth states the discrimination in its sharpest form: παραλαμβάνειν is a technical term in Paul, and occurs elsewhere in John only twice, and not with the same meaning as here.[^barth-technical] What John's verb carries is John's usage.

The welcome-register of verse 11 carries over, attenuated. The figure at verse 12 is a grant, not a homecoming, so what persists is the *relational* register — a person received, not a proposition assented to. That the pair is composed is held with very high confidence, and so is its identification with believing.

## The refusal answered, not extended

Ἔλαβον stands as the positive term over against all three negations. Its principal carrier is the chief dissenter from the reading of the refusals as an advance. Bultmann reads 3:11's "you do not receive our testimony" as carrying the same comprehensive meaning as οὐ κατέλαβεν, οὐκ ἔγνω and οὐ παρέλαβον, together with 3:32's "no one receives" — five expressions under one meaning, two of them the simple verb.[^bultmann-five] Barth ties the far end forward from verse 5 and the near end back to verse 10.[^barth-ties]

But the series acquired at verse 11 — [one refusal told three times](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/03-articulation-three-figures/), advancing in the definiteness of the one who refuses and the personalization of the one refused — is here *answered*, not continued. At verse 12 the object stays αὐτόν, already at its maximum, and the subject does not narrow further. Ὅσοι is an indefinite relative that, in Harris's words, "has no implication of numbers."[^harris-numbers] What happens is not a fourth step along the trajectory but a re-sorting within the third: from a body defined by belonging to a body defined by response. That is the more accurate claim and the more interesting one, because it is what the whole verse turns out to be doing.

## The receivers — bounded by reception, not by lineage

The δέ qualifies a statement about Israel, so the verse's *immediate reference* is the believing part of the household verse 11 named. That is why the vocabulary of the remnant sits so naturally here — in Carson, Dodd and Keener, and in Harris's "majority of Jews" at verse 11, which presupposes a minority who did not fail.[^remnant]

But the term John chose does not bound them by descent, and three of the verse's most careful readers say so. Westcott: the national rejection was qualified by the personal belief of some, who "believed as men, so to say, and not as Jews," and "The privilege of Israel (Ex. 4:22) was extended to all the faithful."[^westcott-men] Barth: "The only point of the hosoi is that it marks a transition from consideration of all anthrōpoi, of the cosmos, of the humanity that v. 11 claims as the idioi of the Logos, to a consideration of specific people, of concrete individuals."[^barth-hosoi] Ridderbos refuses both poles in one sentence: the privilege of being children of God "is not a natural quality that every human being has as a creature of God; nor is it the inalienable right of Israel as 'his own' … It is, rather, the gift that is given only to those who believe in the Word."[^ridderbos-gift]

Behind them stand Dodd's κρίσις, a sifting of men by their varying responses,[^dodd-krisis] and Harris's σχίσμα, the division of 1:10–12 between those who believed and those who did not.[^harris-schism] The Gospel's own text about the children of God enters here too. Schnackenburg identifies the flock given to the Son with "the 'children of God' scattered throughout the world whom Jesus will gather together (11:52)"[^schnackenburg-scattered] — a reach explicitly not bounded by descent. And Köstenberger states the transfer of the covenant title in so many words: "yet here 'children of God' are said to be all those who believe in his name."[^eec-transfer]

So [the universal scope of the light *of men*](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/07-anthropology-light-of-men/) is carried a second time by the grammar rather than by a caveat. At verse 11 the universal possession stood in the neuter of the line that named the covenant household. At verse 12 the universal admission stands in the relative pronoun of the line that answers it. Israel-first, never Israel-only — and now the sentence that grieves the refusal is followed by a sentence whose subject the refusal's own boundary does not determine. This reading of the receivers is held with high confidence. It stops short of very high because the δέ's qualifying force is a real argument for a single field, answered rather than dissolved.

## Ἐξουσία — a right, and a right that carries its effect

The Authorized Version's "power to become the sons of God" has taught generations to hear a capacity infused. The Greek says something else.

The lexicon is plain: ἐξουσία comes from ἔξεστιν, *it is permissible*.[^zerwick-exesti] The survey is Bultmann's: "Ἐξουσία in John is always used in the normal Greek sense of 'right', 'authority' (1.12; 5.27; 10.18; 17.2; 19.10f. …), and not in the sense developed in Gnosticism and magic, where the emphasis is placed not on the element of right but of power".[^bultmann-survey] The substitution that names it is attested twice, four centuries apart and independently, and the concurrence is this commentary's own finding at this verse. Calvin: ἐξουσία "is sometimes put for ἀξίωσις, (*a claim*,) a meaning which falls in admirably with this passage."[^calvin-claim] Barth: "not possibility in the sense of dynamis, power, might, strength, but in the sense of axiōsis, authority, legitimation, right."[^barth-axiosis] Dodd's footnote fixes the rendering: the Authorized Version's "power," which makes the word indistinguishable from δύναμις, is one of its most misleading mistranslations.[^dodd-mistranslation]

But the right is not a permission held in suspense, and the reading locks that inside the sense rather than beside it. Dodd supplies the figure: "ἐξουσία is given by a competent legislative or judicial pronouncement; in this case, by the authoritative 'word' of the Ruler of the universe"[^dodd-pronouncement] — a grant that constitutes the standing it declares. Westcott, independently and almost identically: the word "does not describe mere ability, but legitimate, rightful authority, derived from a competent source which includes the idea of power."[^westcott-authority] Calvin refuses the mere-possibility reading in terms. The evangelist "does not mean by it any intermediate faculty, or one which does not include the full and complete effect"; the power is "that *fitness*; (ἱκανότης)" for which Paul thanks God.[^calvin-fitness] And the syntax says the same. Γενέσθαι "limits ἐξουσίαν … by defining its nature,"[^harris-limits] so that what is conferred is *the standing to become*.

The most serious opposition to this reading is Schnackenburg's, with McHugh beside him on the same side. For McHugh, ἐξουσία "denotes the possibility, the ability, a capability, and therefore the potential, the power 'to become children of God'."[^mchugh-power] The opposition is answered from the figure rather than outvoted. A second objection comes from Brown, and it is aimed at the figure itself rather than at the word; it is answered in the note.[^brown-figure]

Schnackenburg's ground is this reading's own. His argument against the Gnostics is right in what it denies — no one is a child of God by a pneumatic nature, and human beings cannot of themselves attain to sonship — and this commentary denies it with him.[^schnackenburg-denies] What it declines is his lexical verdict, that "to be entitled to" is "too weak."[^schnackenburg-weak] On a legislative grant the entitlement *is* the enabling: nothing further needs to be infused for a rightful standing to be real.

And his objection is weightier than a footnote, and is declined knowing that. His excursus on faith carries a worked-out account on which "the power to be united to Jesus in faith … must come from God."[^schnackenburg-power] The demand for a real conferred capacity is where his whole theology of faith stands. The lexical ground this reading rests on — Bultmann's survey of every Johannine instance — is untouched by it, since the excursus never treats the word. The lexical sense, *right*, is held with very high confidence; the theological reading, a right that carries its effect, with high confidence.

## Τέκνα — children, and what "adoption" names

John withholds the title υἱὸς θεοῦ from believers. Barth: "The synonymous huioi theou does not occur in John at all, although once we find huioi phōtos (12:36)."[^barth-huioi] Ridderbos: believers "are not called 'sons' (υἱοί) but exclusively '*children* (τέκνα) of God'."[^ridderbos-tekna] And McHugh: the sole New Testament author to use μονογενής of Jesus "declines to extend the title υἱός θεοῦ to any other person."[^mchugh-declines]

Which word, then, carries the claim about nature? Τέκνα. McHugh's word-study fixes it: τέκνον, from τίκτω, "describes the offspring from the viewpoint of the relationship to their progenitors," where υἱοί and θυγατέρες belong more often to a legal context.[^mchugh-progenitors] And υἱοθεσία, the New Testament's actual word for adoption, is Pauline in that sense only.[^mchugh-huiothesia] Westcott: the idea of "child" as against "son" "is that of a community of nature (v. 13) as distinguished from that of a dignity of heirship."[^westcott-nature] So John gives believers the word of *derived nature*, and reserves the word of *unique filial dignity* for the Son.

The tradition's language of "adoption" names the derivative mode. It is not a substitute for nature. The guard shows at every witness. Harris warrants "adoptive" from 3:3, a text about begetting.[^harris-adoptive] Augustine's contrast is with the Son — "not by nature, of course, like the one whom he begot."[^augustine-nature] And Barth states the whole matter: "The right of the hosoi to be children of God is thus enclosed and grounded in the Son. In this light we can understand John's hesitation to say huioi instead of tekna."[^barth-enclosed]

Westcott supplies the sequence, and it has the same shape as the reading of ἐξουσία: "They are adopted — placed, if we may so speak, in the position of sons — that so they may become children actually … The fruit is not given at once, but the seed."[^westcott-seed] A standing conferred that is a real filiation, and a filiation that is *to become*. What τέκνα *says* belongs to this verse. How the begetting *works* belongs to the next verse, and Westcott's own "(v. 13)" points there.

One further finding is held more modestly. Under [the working calibration this commentary adopted at verse 9](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/11-true-light-coming/) — one advent, with a covenant career through Moses and the prophets and its direction toward verse 14 — the reception this verse describes is as wide as the coming it answers. The status is therefore *not confined to the enfleshed arrival*. Dodd writes, "That God did in fact authorize men to claim the status of sonship can be proved from scripture,"[^dodd-scripture] and Barth, "There were and are those who indeed live in the world like all the rest but who receive him".[^barth-were] Those sentences are the calibration's own consequence. Köstenberger's "only by placing their faith in Jesus" is received exactly as it stands — a claim about the condition, not the date.[^eec-condition] How a reception before the incarnation was mediated is not asked here. The finding rests on the working calibration and is held with high confidence and no higher, expressly open to refinement when verse 14 is treated. Brown reads the verse otherwise, and his argument presses on it there: for him, sonship is realized only when the risen Jesus gives the Spirit.[^brown-timing] The claim about nature is held with very high confidence; adoption as its mode, with high confidence.

## The giver — the Word, and concentration, not exclusion

The subject of ἔδωκεν is unexpressed, and it carries over from the construal adopted at [verse 9](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/11-true-light-coming/). McHugh: "The grammatical subject is still the Logos, as Bengel perspicaciously observes: 'ἔδωκεν. dedit: Gloria Christi, Unigeniti'."[^mchugh-bengel] Michaels: "the subject of verse 12 is not the 'many' who 'received' the Light, but rather … the Light himself."[^michaels-light] Barth adds the argument from architecture: the main clause ἔδωκεν αὐτοῖς is "the central pillar which carries the whole."[^barth-pillar]

The weighting is adopted with the limit Barth himself supplies. We are not told that the Father gives the ἐξουσία, nor, as Paul and Calvin might say, that the Spirit gives it, "but rather (not contradicting these statements but giving them an unheard-of concentration) that the Word gave it to them." The Word is "a locum tenens for the one who later in the Gospel will be called especially 'the Son.' He gives this exousia who himself originally has it."[^barth-concentration] Barth's "these statements" are the Father's gift — 1 John 3:1, which he has just quoted — and the Spirit's. Schnackenburg's conferral by divine power revealed by the mediator, and Westcott's right "not inherent in man, but 'given' by God to him," where *him* is the one who receives the right,[^westcott-given] are statements of the same kind: convergent with Barth's, not opposed to it. How the agency is distributed across verses 12 and 13 — the Word giving, and God begetting — belongs to the next verse.

## The construction, and the number

Ὅσοι δὲ ἔλαβον αὐτόν is a pendent, or hanging, nominative — a *casus pendens* — resumed by the dative αὐτοῖς. So Wallace, Zerwick, Barrett, Brown and Morris.[^pendent] McHugh and Barth call it a "nominative absolute," and Harris gives both names; that label Wallace reserves for what does not occur within a sentence.[^nominative-absolute] Bultmann dissents on the classification; the dissent is recorded, and the classification stands.[^bultmann-classification]

What the construction does, three sources agree. Wallace's taxonomy places 1:12 under *emphasis*: the pendent nominative focuses on the main topic of the sentence, whether or not it is the grammatical subject.[^wallace-emphasis] Westcott: "The irregular construction of the original … gives prominence to the act of personal faith," and then, by a change of subject, to the Word and what he did.[^westcott-irregular] Barth: a smooth dative "expunges the emphasis on the event: hosoi elabon, which is what the nominative expression in Greek achieves."[^barth-expunges] And Michaels holds both halves in one sentence: the subject is the Light himself, but "The author, however, has highlighted the recipients instead of the giver by placing them front and center in a relative clause."[^michaels-highlighted]

So the syntax says what the sentence says. The fronting throws the receivers into relief; the change of case then makes the Word the agent. The verse names them first, and then tells you they did not accomplish it — the same structure the readings of ἐξουσία and of τέκνα each reached at their own word: a standing given, not achieved.

As to number, the word says nothing and the context says few. Ὅσοι "has no implication of numbers."[^harris-numbers-2] Barth, naming Bauer, refuses to make smallness necessary.[^barth-small] And the discrimination is made most cleanly by the source the field had counted on the other side. Bultmann: "ὅσοι in itself, however, tells us nothing about how great or small their number is. But it must be small; for the general picture is conveyed by v. 11".[^bultmann-small] Smallness is a true inference from the mass refusal of verses 10 and 11, and from the remnant vocabulary. It is not the pronoun's content. What the construction does is held with high confidence; that the pronoun says nothing of number, with very high confidence.

## Believing in his name

Τοῖς πιστεύουσιν εἰς τὸ ὄνομα αὐτοῦ — who they are, not which of them qualify.

The apposition is *defining*, not restrictive. It says who the receivers are, not which of them qualify. McHugh says so in terms,[^mchugh-defining] and so do Harris's epexegetic apposition, Westcott and Schnackenburg.[^defining]

Nor does it state a *condition* of the grant. Barth: "We must seriously oppose Holtzmann and Bauer, and also Zahn, who agrees with them here, when they find in the last clause the presupposition of the transfer … The text does not say this. Logically the third clause is parallel to the second as well as the first."[^barth-oppose] Köstenberger's "people 'become' God's children (τέκνα θεοῦ) only by placing their faith in Jesus"[^eec-become] is received as a true account of how one comes to be among the ὅσοι, and declined as a reading of the clause's syntactic role. So too Schnackenburg's "Faith is the basic prerequisite for salvation, and in Johannine theology the one condition which contains all others (cf. 6:29)".[^schnackenburg-condition] That is a statement of what is required of us, received with Köstenberger's, while Schnackenburg's own reading of the syntax is the defining one — the participle "takes up the relative clause."

Nor is the participle only the consequence of receiving. Barth allows that reading "with H. Holtzmann and W. Bauer," and prefers his own: "I would rather say that pisteuontes (which does in fact describe a habitus) is one side of the matter that is present in and with the event of elabon."[^barth-rather] Michaels: "'Receiving' and 'believing' are virtually synonymous in this Gospel … each interpreting the other."[^michaels-synonymous]

And its tense is doing work. Belief "is ongoing and is open to those who live after John's time";[^harris-ongoing] it is "present and lasting … and not simply as triumphant in the crisis of trial."[^westcott-lasting] This is [the door the architecture left open](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/07-the-aperture/).

What, then, is believing in his name? Schnackenburg: πιστεύειν εἰς τὸ ὄνομα is typically and exclusively Johannine, and "implies the acceptance of Jesus to the full extent of his self-revelation. Such an act of faith is possible only in the encounter with a historical bringer of salvation".[^schnackenburg-acceptance] With him stand Dodd's entering into allegiance,[^dodd-allegiance] Harris's total committal of the self to the person,[^harris-committal] and the construction's own testimony: in the Gospel, πιστεύω εἰς is used only of a divine object of faith — of God rarely, usually of Christ — and never of a human one.[^personal-object]

Here the reading meets Barth's strongest lexical claim, and answers him in his own terms. He denies that this is *fiducia*, the trust of the Reformers: in John, "among almost a hundred instances there is only one (14:1) in which pisteuein might denote the Reformation fiducia," and believing is regarding as true.[^barth-fiducia] His point is received where it is right. "Regarding it as true is not just an intellectual matter … But the power of faith derives, not from its existential nature, but from its object"[^barth-object] — a guard this reading adopts: committal here is not a psychology of faith. What is declined is only his verdict that John's verb *denotes* acknowledgment, with the personal relation supplied from outside. John's εἰς-construction, used of no other object, carries the relation in the syntax.

Bultmann gives the same answer from the other side. Of receiving testimony at 3:11 and 3:32–33, he writes that "to admit sth. to be true" is the sense "which probably comes closest to the Johannine usage here," yet the admitting "in John is not based on logic, but on the obedience of faith"[^bultmann-obedience] — and receiving him, at 1:12, is for Bultmann believing.

And Schnackenburg makes it a third voice, at length and by name. A survey of the vocabulary makes it certain, he writes, that "in John 'to believe in Jesus' means above all to acknowledge his claims for his own person," the name-formula of this verse his first example; the texts about salvation "are not to be taken in the sense of a *fides fiducialis*";[^schnackenburg-claims] and the revelation John records, he writes, "does not spare man the need for his personal decision, for submitting to God as he reveals himself in Christ, for the obedience of faith"[^schnackenburg-decision] — Bultmann's phrase, reached from the opposite end of the field. Three major voices converge: an assent that is not merely intellectual, an admitting on obedience, an acknowledgment of claims that is neither *fiducia* nor merely rational. That the third is the chief objector to the reading of ἐξουσία is what makes the convergence worth its weight.

And the boundary that keeps this honest is John's own. The identical phrase describes the faith in signs that Jesus declines to trust at 2:23–24. Believing in his name "is not necessarily stronger than 'believing'; it rather depends on whether people continue in the faith,"[^eec-continue] and the faith of 1:12 is not guaranteed genuine.[^carson-genuine] That is not a weakening of the reading but its necessary limit, and the present participle is where John himself puts it. The defining apposition is held with very high confidence; the reading of believing as committal, with high confidence, and with its boundary.

## The name, and the baptismal reading

The ὄνομα is the person — the revealed name that, in Westcott's words, "gathers up and expresses for man just so much as he can apprehend of the divine nature."[^westcott-name] Barth presses further: the name is a divine name, *the* divine name, "coessential with ho theos as himself theos … Materially it is simply a question of affirming v. 2".[^barth-name] This commentary receives that as confirmation from below of [the Word's full deity](/commentary/john/1-1-to-3/08-theos-en-ho-logos/), asserted in the Prologue's first verse — the first time that reading is confirmed from the believer's side rather than from the predication — and it locks nothing new.

The Fathers read the verse through the practice of baptism. Chrysostom speaks of the image of sonship impressed on us at baptism; Basil cites the verse inside a treatise on baptism.[^fathers-baptism] That is a real fact about the verse's reception, and it is received as reception, not as reference. The one claim of reference among the major modern commentators is Schnackenburg's. He reads verse 12's becoming children of God of baptism, and finds its explanation at verse 13.[^schnackenburg-baptism] The claim travels there, where its warrant would have to lie.

## A standing given, not achieved

The verse thus lands as one teachable claim: not everyone turned him away. The same sentence that names Israel's refusal turns, and names those who took him in — and it names them by what they did, not by whose children they were.

To them he gave a right. It is not a permission they might use, and not a power infused into them, but a standing granted by the one who alone had it to grant — and a standing that makes them what it calls them: children of God, born and not merely reckoned, though never as the Son is.

And the verse tells us who they are before it tells us what they were given. They are the ones believing in his name — which in John means taking him for what he is, the divine one whose name is the name of God. That believing is not warmed-up feeling, and it is not bare assent; its whole weight comes from its object. It also does not certify itself. John will show us people who believed in his name and were not trusted with it. What the verse promises is not to the confident but to those who go on receiving.

It is the seed of the Gospel's "he who receives me receives him who sent me" (13:20), and of its last sentence, "that believing you may have life in his name" (20:31). And it leaves one question the verse itself does not answer — what it is to *become* a child of God — which is the work of the sentence's second half.
