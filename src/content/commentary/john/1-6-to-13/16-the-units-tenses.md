The reader has now met every verb in these eight verses, each in its own place: the man who came, the light that was coming, the world that did not know, the home that did not welcome, the ones who received and were begotten, and those who believe. [Before the verses were read one by one](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/04-temporal-payload/), this commentary set out what their tenses do together — an advent told in retrospect. This page does the same work from the other end. With each verb now read where it stands, the tenses are gathered back into one plan; the one dispute about them that the verses left open is closed; and the plan is held against the one serious claim that these verses mark no time at all.

## What the verses carry, counted

Set aside the forms that carry no time of their own — the subjunctives of the purpose clauses (μαρτυρήσῃ twice, πιστεύσωσιν), the infinitive γενέσθαι, and the perfect participle ἀπεσταλμένος — and the movement's tenses are these: nine aorists, three imperfects, one present indicative and two present participles.[^inventory]

The nine aorists are ἐγένετο and ἦλθεν in the frame (verses 6 and 7); οὐκ ἔγνω, ἦλθεν, οὐ παρέλαβον, ἔλαβον and ἔδωκεν in the reception (verses 10 to 12); ἐγεννήθησαν at the close (verse 13); and one more that is easy to pass over — the ἐγένετο of verse 10, *the world came to be through him*. That ninth is the creation-word of verse 3 sounding again inside the movement. Its reference is the world's making, not the light's reception, which is why it stands outside [the run of tenses the movement tells its story in](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/04-temporal-payload/).

The three imperfects are οὐκ ἦν (verse 8), ἦν (verse 9) and ἦν (verse 10). The two present participles are ἐρχόμενον (verse 9) and τοῖς πιστεύουσιν (verse 12).

And the one present indicative is φωτίζει, *illumines*, at verse 9. It needs to be named, because it is the verb this plan most easily leaves out. This commentary reads it as the light's own office, [with Schnackenburg](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/11-true-light-coming/), who finds the hymn insisting that the power to enlighten "essentially belongs to him (φωτίζει)", and for whom the illumination is "active since creation and always remaining (present) active" — rather than as a report of a time.[^office] That reading is contested. Bultmann and Michaels both hear φωτίζει as the evangelist's own present, [as the discussion of the movement's open door sets out](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/07-the-aperture/).[^contest] The contest stands, and it is returned to at the end of this page.

## The frame

The movement's first two verbs are aorists and narrative ones: Ἐγένετο ἄνθρωπος, *there came a man* (verse 6), and οὗτος ἦλθεν, *this one came* (verse 7). They set the movement's time as retrospect. The evangelist narrates from this side of an advent already complete — "from John's vantage point in the past," in the phrase Köstenberger uses of the light's presence at verse 10.[^vantage]

Barrett is right that at verse 6 "for the first time the stage of history is reached."[^barrett-stage] His sentence is received here as a statement about the narrative's *vantage*, and not about how far back what follows reaches. The reason is that the aorists which open the frame are themselves summaries. Verse 6 does not report the first moment the Baptist appeared; it takes his whole appearance into one word. Barth says so of this verse: the coming of the Baptist "refers to his total appearance and not simply to the first moment of his activity."[^barth-total] Chrysostom, as McHugh reports him, speaks to what the verb points at — "not to this man's coming into existence, but to the mission itself."[^chrysostom] Harris's constative class of the Prologue's six uses of ἐγένετο gives the verb its grammatical home.[^harris-class] And at verse 7 Harris labels μαρτυρήσῃ a constative, "encompassing John's whole ministry in a single glance."[^harris-glance]

A frame opened by summarizing aorists is a retrospect, not a threshold.

## The interior

Inside the frame stand the three imperfects: οὐκ ἦν, *he was not* the light, at verse 8; ἦν, *was*, of the true light at verse 9; ἦν, *he was* in the world, at verse 10. They do not narrate the visitation the frame looks back on. They reach beneath it. They belong to the register of the Prologue's opening verses, where what the Word is, he *was*. Of the first of them Barth says it himself: "note the ēn of v. 8a with a reference back to v. 4."[^barth-reference] So they are read on the Prologue's line of tenses rather than inside the Baptist's aorist.

On [the reading adopted at verse 9](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/11-true-light-coming/), the Word is coming from verse 9 and arrives at verse 11, and no single verse is the threshold. That places the two long-standing readings of these verses without defeating either. The older reading, which hears the Word before the flesh in verses 9 and 10 — the Fathers, Augustine, Calvin, Westcott and Abbott, each in his own way — hears a reach that is really there, and then adds a threshold at verse 11 which the tenses do not draw. The modern reading, which hears the incarnation from verse 9 — Carson, McHugh, Ridderbos, Lincoln, Keener, Köstenberger and Harris — is right about where the aorists land, and cannot show that the imperfects narrate that landing.[^two-readings] McHugh, who reads verse 9 as the light entering the world "at a particular moment in time," says as much himself: the two positions are "perhaps not mutually exclusive alternatives, but complementary… the reception of the Word of God under the Old Covenant was repeated at the coming of the Word made Flesh."[^complementary]

This is the one part of the plan held with high rather than very high confidence, because it rests on the reading of verse 9 rather than on the tenses alone. [The full case is set out with the movement's architecture](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/04-temporal-payload/), and is not repeated here.

## The reception aorists, and the dispute closed

The five verbs of the reception — οὐκ ἔγνω at verse 10; ἦλθεν and οὐ παρέλαβον at verse 11; ἔλαβον and ἔδωκεν at verse 12 — summarize the light's reception at its climax. Each views its action whole. All five are constative.

That claim rests on class and principle, and it is worth being exact about which. Harris's class is the Prologue's six uses of ἐγένετο, none of which is among these five, so this commentary carries it to them by analogy. Carson states a general principle in his note on verse 5: the aorist "merely establishes that the writer looks on the action holistically." Schnackenburg, of ἦλθεν and οὐκ ἔγνω, says the aorists "merely reflect the fact that the encounter between the Logos and the world took place in the reality of history." And the refusal of verse 11 and the reception of verse 12 are composed as a matching pair.[^class]

At the individual verbs the labels are few and uneven, and they are reported as they stand. Harris labels οὐ παρέλαβον a constative, "summing up in a comprehensive glance," and οὐκ ἔγνω "more probably" constative after weighing the ingressive, *did not begin to recognize*. Waetjen labels οὐκ ἔγνω a constative that "reduces this ongoing response of denial and rejection to a single fact" — his grammar taken here, his reading of the verse as the history of the Word before Jesus not. Robertson labels ἔλαβον "Effective." No one labels ἦλθεν or ἔδωκεν.[^labels]

Other readers hear more than a summary in these verbs. Morris hears it in the aorists of verses 10 and 11: "The aorist tense here, as in the previous verse, points to the decisive action of rejection. It fastens our attention on the crisis rather than on the continuing result"; at verse 10 he had said the aorist "perhaps indicates a single action." Robertson reads ἔλαβον as "Effective aorist … exceptional action on the part of the disciples and other believers." Barrett hears in the ἦλθεν of verse 11 "a unique coming, the incarnation." And Michaels takes the aorist of ἔγνω to mean that the world did not "come to know" the light.[^more]

This commentary carried Morris's reading and Robertson's forward against Harris's, as a three-way dispute, until this point. It is worth saying plainly what kind of dispute it was. None of the three made his parse against the others: Robertson labels verse 12 and gives verse 11 no aspect, Harris labels verses 10 and 11 and gives verse 12's aorists no aspect label, and Morris hears a crisis at verses 10 and 11 and says nothing of the aspect of verse 12. The contest is this commentary's construction from three silences, and it is closed as such.

It closes on a distinction this commentary has drawn since [verse 12](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/14-the-turn-children-of-god/): the word does not carry what the passage carries. Morris's crisis and Robertson's accomplishment are not parses that compete with Harris's constative. They are what the verbs' *reference* supplies once the rest of the Gospel is read into the summary — the refusal reaching its crisis at the close of the public ministry (12:37), the reception reaching its fullness in the community (13:1; 20:29). Brown states the shape from the other side: verses 11 and 12 "are really short summaries of the two parts of the Gospel: the Book of Signs and the Book of Glory."[^brown-summaries]

So the verbs are symmetric in their grammar, and the asymmetry first sensed at [the reception verse](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/14-the-turn-children-of-god/) is real in what they refer to — and it belongs to the Gospel's story, not to the verb. That is why [the world's refusal](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/12-world-did-not-know/) and [his own people's](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/13-his-own-welcome-refused/) could each be read as a constative summary and still be called a crisis. The decisiveness some readers hear in these verbs is the Gospel's, not the grammar's. This is held with very high confidence.

## The climactic aorist, and what it does not order

The movement's last verb is ἐγεννήθησαν, *were begotten*, at verse 13. It is a constative aorist that locates the begetting as one completed act of God: for McHugh "a once-for-all event in the past," for Schnackenburg "an act (note the aorist) of divine and heavenly origin."[^begetting] And it stands with the reception, not before or after it. Westcott: "The act of reception coincided with the infusion of the divine principle."[^westcott]

What the tense does not do is order believing and being begotten. Barrett: "The aorist is not pluperfect in sense." Michaels: "The text defines no temporal or causal relationship between 'believing' and being 'born of God,' either to the effect that individuals are born of God because they believe, or that they believe because they are already born of God."[^ordering]

Neither is cited as the ground of what this commentary holds, and it matters to see why. Barrett's own order runs the other way. His note opens, "This birth is conditional upon receiving Christ and believing on his name," which makes the reception the condition of the birth; this commentary [argues against that at verse 13](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/15-begotten-of-god/). Michaels's text defines no relation in either direction, though in a note he judges that Bultmann assumes the believing-first order "without argument," and that "the accent in verse 13 on divine sovereignty and on the absence of any human involvement in the birth of 'the children of God' points, if anything, in the opposite direction."[^michaels-note]

That the one act of God is the ground of the reception is therefore this commentary's own reading, carried from verse 13. It is a priority of ground with the mechanism withheld, and this is its exact form in the tenses: the aorist states the act and orders nothing. So the claim that God's begetting grounds the human turning is not a claim about tense, and no reading of this passage should make it one.

The begetting has one further extension, and it is bounded. The perfects of the First Letter of John — "has been begotten of God," at 2:29; 3:9; 4:7; 5:1, 4 and 18 — are the same begetting seen from the other side: the Gospel narrating the act, the letter describing the state it leaves.[^epistle] The extension goes no further than that. And Dodd, for one, keeps the two writers apart on sonship: the author of the letter, he thinks, "does not seem to have understood" that what God calls people, they are.[^dodd] This, too, is held with very high confidence.

## The present inside the aorists

Two present participles stand inside all this past, and they hold two different offices.

Ἐρχόμενον, *coming*, at verse 9, gives the advent's ongoing manner inside the long *was* of the verse. Its time is relative to that imperfect, [as the reading of verse 9 construes it](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/11-true-light-coming/), and it does not reach toward the reader.

Τοῖς πιστεύουσιν, *to those who believe*, at verse 12, does. For Harris the change from the aorist to the present shows that belief "is ongoing and is open to those who live after John's time." Brown, conjecturing that the clause may be an editorial expansion, suggests it may have been added to stress "continued belief in him (present in 12c)." For Michaels the present participle "suggests that the author has in mind Christian believers (or potential believers) in his own day, even as he writes his Gospel." Köstenberger finds that it likely forms an inclusio with the Gospel's stated purpose at 20:30–31. And Wallace, on the New Testament's use of the present participle for believers, thinks it seems "unlikely that when the present was used, it was aspectually flat," the aorist having been available.[^believe]

So this movement's contemporary frame enters at the reception's open door, and not at the light's shining. The coming is told; the believing is open. [The weight of that placement in the passage's design](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/07-the-aperture/) is argued with the architecture. It is held with very high confidence.

## One denial engaged — and one that is not

The plan stands against one serious denial that these verses mark time at all.

Barth, writing on verse 4: "Obviously no one time is here marked off from another. Revelation as a whole … is here contrasted with history as a whole."[^barth-obviously] He writes it of verse 4, and this commentary extends it to the present passage because Barth himself carries the stance across the Prologue. Every word of the Prologue relates to Jesus Christ, he says, and "it also relates to the Logos as the Revealer of God who announces himself before and even apart from Jesus of Nazareth"; and the Baptist's standing has, "both positively and negatively," a "universal significance that embraces the different times."[^barth-carries]

His observation is not refuted here; it is received. That the times interpenetrate is the very thing this plan is built on. But Barth's own pages mark the times he says are not marked. Of verse 6: "In v. 6 egeneto denotes the historical coming of the witness John." Of verse 14, with verse 6 beside it: "the historical singularity of the eternal, absolute, divine Word." At verse 11 he allows, hedging and leaving it open, that "we must perhaps make a concession to Zahn's view," and that Holtzmann "may not be wrong when he says that the ēlthen refers to a single coming, a historical fact." And at verse 10 the ἦν is "an event in contrast to permanent immanence."[^barth-marks] His observation is received; his conclusion, that no time is therefore marked off, is declined, as it was [at John 1:4–5](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/03-macro-tense-run/).

Keener can sound like a second denial, and he is not one. His remark that "John's verb tenses elsewhere in the prologue hardly seem to model precision" is a hedged caution about the Prologue's other tenses, made against Boismard's argument from the present participle of verse 9. At verse 9 itself Keener reads "come into the world" as indicating "a historical moment rather than an eternal process" — and his conclusion there, that on that reading the phrase "refers in context to the incarnation," is the one this plan shares.[^keener]

## What the tenses say together

So the movement's tenses say, together, what [the architecture named](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/04-temporal-payload/): an advent told in retrospect. An aorist frame; an imperfect interior reaching back beneath it; five summaries of the reception; one completed act of God, standing with the reception and ordering nothing; and inside it all, one door left open.

The plan is the reverse of [the movement before it](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/03-macro-tense-run/). There the present φαίνει, *shines*, set the time, and an aorist summarized against it. Here an aorist sets the time, the imperfects reach beneath it, and the one verb whose time reaches the reader's present stands at the reception's end. The present φωτίζει of verse 9 is read here as naming the light's office rather than a time — though Bultmann and Michaels hear it as the evangelist's own present, and the reading is held with that disagreement in view.

And this is the co-articulation that [the architecture described](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/08-co-articulation/) — the passage's three beats and its temporal plan lying one over the other, each resting on its own witnesses — which the reader has now walked through verb by verb. What the verses say is complete. What the church confesses on their strength — about the world that did not know its Maker, the people who were his own, the children and the name, and the begetting — is the work of the sections that follow.

[^barth-marks]: Barth, *Witness to the Word*, p. 86 (verses 6 and 14); p. 68 (verse 11), where the passage closes, "It is also possible that the concrete ēlthen which gives rise to this interpretation is simply governed by the concrete figure of the one who returns to his homeland … However that may be"; p. 63 (verse 10), "It is act or action."

[^keener]: Keener, *John*, p. 395, where Keener concludes, "That 'coming into the world' applies to the light rather than to 'every person' is likely; that on such a reading it refers in context to the incarnation is still more likely"; and, a few lines on, where Boismard "points to the present form of the participle and concurs with many church fathers," answers: "But John's verb tenses elsewhere in the prologue hardly seem to model precision". Keener, *John*, p. 394: "The phrase 'come into the world' can suggest either birth (of people) or other kinds of origination, but indicates a historical moment rather than an eternal process (cf. 1 John 4:1–6)."
