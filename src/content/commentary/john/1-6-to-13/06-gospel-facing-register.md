The previous section placed this movement inside the Prologue. This one looks past the Prologue's edge, to the book the Prologue opens.

The movement's turn — the refusal of verse 11 answered, on a single δέ, by the reception of verse 12 — has a counterpart in the shape of the Gospel as a whole. What the two verses say in a line each, the Gospel says in two halves: a ministry that ends in refusal, and then a company who received him. The turn is the Gospel's own turn, composed in miniature at the Prologue's centre of gravity.

That is a claim about composition, and it is held firmly. A second claim stands beside it and is held more loosely — that the correspondence is realized at the Gospel's own seam, where the household that refused him is replaced by the household he loves. This section states the first, separates the second, adds the answer the Gospel's last sentence makes to this unit's last phrase, and closes with the reading that has most often been offered in place of all three.

## The two halves in a verse each

εἰς τὰ ἴδια ἦλθεν, καὶ οἱ ἴδιοι αὐτὸν οὐ παρέλαβον — *he came to what was his own, and his own people did not welcome him* — can be laid over the first half of the Gospel, the ministry that runs from the Baptist's witness to the end of the public teaching. ὅσοι δὲ ἔλαβον αὐτόν — *but as many as received him* — can be laid over the second, where Jesus is found among those who did receive him.

Five traditions register the correspondence, each in its own words: Harris, Brown, Carson, Lincoln, Dodd.[^halves] They do not draw the two spans identically. Harris sets them at 1:19–12:50 and 13:1–20:31; Brown at chapters 1–12 and 13–20; Carson puts the first banner over "the first twelve chapters" and the second over chapters 13–21; Lincoln previews the second part in 13:1–17:26, the farewell material only; Dodd, whose second book is the Passion, relates the refusal of the first half to the chapters that follow the traitor's departure. The differences are real and are recorded rather than smoothed: what the five agree on is the correspondence itself — this verse over that half of the book, that verse over this one.

Carson states it in the form the field has given it. "Many have pointed out," he begins, that the words of verse 11 "could be placarded over the first twelve chapters of this book, but over chs. 13–21 we might raise the banner, 'Yet to all who received him …'."[^carson] The reading is a commonplace, reported as such and then adopted in his own voice; that is how it is carried here too.

The names for the two halves are Brown's. "Book of Signs" and "Book of Glory" are his terms, used here as this commentary's own framing and not put into the mouths of the other four, who divide the book by their own names or decline the labels altogether.[^labels]

What lifts the correspondence above a pleasing symmetry is that the Gospel supplies its own summary at each of the two seams. The public ministry closes on the refusal — the evangelist's own account of why so many did not believe, at 12:37–41 — and the book closes on the believing, at 20:30–31.[^seams] The Prologue says in two lines what the Gospel says twice more in its own voice, at the places where its halves end.

## The Gospel's own answering term

A second form of the same claim runs through the same literature, and it is worth keeping apart from the first, because it answers verse 11 with a different verse.

On that reading the dividing term is not the pair *refused / received* but the phrase "his own" itself, which the Gospel takes up again at 13:1, where those who are his own are the disciples he loves to the end. Köstenberger states it plainly: the little word "his own" divides the Gospel structurally into two halves, the first telling of Jesus' rejection by his own and the second of his reception by his own — with verse 12 noted, inside the first half, as an anticipation of the positive response rather than as the second half's heading.[^thirteen] Lincoln, Brown and Harris state the same reversal at 13:1, each at his own comment on that verse.

So the two forms are not one. The correspondence this section holds — verse 11 over the first half, verse 12 over the second — is stated by the five named above. Köstenberger's is a claim about the Gospel's architecture, keyed to a term the Gospel itself repeats, and it reaches past this unit into a book this volume does not treat. It is carried here at that second register and held provisionally: the reversal at 13:1 is the realization of the Prologue's turn in the Gospel's own narrative, and whether the Gospel is *built* that way is not settled from these eight verses.

## The believing, answered at the end of the book

The unit's last phrase before the begetting is τοῖς πιστεύουσιν εἰς τὸ ὄνομα αὐτοῦ — *to those who believe in his name*. The Gospel's own last sentence, before the epilogue, is πιστεύοντες ζωὴν ἔχητε ἐν τῷ ὀνόματι αὐτοῦ — *that believing you may have life in his name*.

Three traditions identify the correspondence between them. Köstenberger and McHugh name it an inclusio — a phrase set at a work's beginning and answered at its end. Bultmann, reading the Gospel's conclusion, says that the emphasis falling on the last word of the book "corresponds with" the first mention of believers at its beginning, and quotes this verse; he does not use the word, and it is not put in his mouth here.[^inclusio]

The two phrases are not identical, and the difference is on the page: the Prologue believes *into* the name, the conclusion has life *in* it. The later phrase answers the earlier; it does not repeat it.

## A turn, not a pivot

The best-known reading of this part of the Prologue makes verse 12b its centre: the point on which a concentric structure turns, the halves of the Prologue folding back symmetrically on either side of it.[^culpepper]

About the weight, that reading is right, and this commentary reaches the same place by other roads. [The unit's one interior connective falls here](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/03-articulation-three-figures/), where the asyndeton that has run from verse 1 breaks at δέ; the chain of linking words that carries the first half of the Prologue stops here; Barth hears the Prologue's first climax at verses 12 and 13, and says that were he dividing the Prologue he would break it after verse 13.[^barth] Four independent observations fall on one place.

About the shape, it is not right. What the evidence yields is a turn in a line, not a fold about a centre: a refusal stated three times and then answered by a reception, marked by the connective and by the verb taken up again across the verse boundary, and followed by a close — not a pivot with matching limbs on either side. The weight is here; the symmetry is not.

## How firmly this is held

The composition — that the turn at verses 11 and 12 is the Gospel's turn in miniature — is held with high confidence, on five traditions reading independently and on the two summaries the Gospel sets at its own seams.

The answer of the Gospel's last sentence to this unit's last phrase is held firmly, though the field is smaller: three traditions, two of whom name the figure and one of whom describes it without naming it.

The realization at 13:1 is held provisionally, and deliberately so. It is a claim about the architecture of a Gospel whose later chapters this volume has not read, and it is recorded here so that the reader can see where it would be tested.

What the movement does with its one non-past verb — the door it leaves open at the believing, and to whom — is the next section's question.
