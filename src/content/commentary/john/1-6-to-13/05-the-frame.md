A movement can be jointed inside and dated in its tenses and still not be placed. The three seams of these eight verses are settled, and so is the time they are told in. What has not been said is where they stand in the composition they belong to — what lies on either side of them, and whether anything holds them there.

The question is not merely tidy-minded. A passage read as the middle of something is read differently from a passage read as a beginning. If verses 6 to 13 are the Prologue's second movement, the reader is meant to arrive at them already carrying the opening verses, and to leave them still owing something to what follows. If they are a self-contained excursus — the Baptist, and then the world's refusal — none of that is true.

This section sets the movement in the Prologue's shape, and then asks what that shape is doing: whether it frames the movement, or fastens it to what surrounds it.

## Between the Foyer and the summit

Two long spans arch over the whole Prologue, and this movement lies under both of them without being an endpoint of either. [The first joins verse 1 to verse 18](/commentary/john/1-1-to-3/02-foyer-bracket/): the Word who was with God at the opening, the Son who is at the Father's side at the close. The second joins verse 3 to verse 18: the one through whom all things came to be is the one who makes the Father known. Both begin before verse 6 and end after verse 13. The movement is inside their reach and outside their frame.

Nearer in stands a second pair, and the movement's relation to it is a different one. Verse 4 and verse 14 face one another — the life that was the light of men on one side, the glory beheld on the other — and [the verses of the previous cycle stood at the near pole of that pair](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/02-gradatio-and-foyer/). These verses do not stand at a pole. They stand between the two. Whatever that pair is doing, this movement is what it holds between its ends.

So the seating, in one line: outside the two long brackets, inside the near pair. That is a claim about position, and everything else in this section is a check on it or a consequence of it.

## The same frame, seen from the other side

There is a second way of describing the Prologue that arrives at the same place from a different direction. Barth sees the Prologue's framework in a descending ladder, and names its rungs: "The Word with God, the Word and the world, the Word among men, the Word itself flesh."[^ladder] Read that way, these verses stand on the third rung — the Word among men — with the fourth, the Word made flesh, still ahead at verse 14.

Two things about that placement should be said plainly rather than assumed. The placement of *these verses* on the third rung is this commentary's, not Barth's: he bounds only one rung in his own words, and the one he bounds is the second, which he assigns to verse 3. And his own use of the ladder comes with a limit attached. He grants a good deal — that the descent is the framework of the Prologue's consideration, and more than that, its general purpose. What he declines to grant is that the Prologue's purpose is *exhausted* by the descent from rung to rung.

That granted-and-declined shape is exactly the distinction this section needs, and it is why the word for what surrounds this movement is *frame* and not *coupling*. A frame sets a thing's place without making its sense depend on what stands at the frame's far corners. The brackets and the pair tell the reader where these verses fall in the Prologue's argument; they do not make the verses unreadable until verse 14 arrives. What verse 14 adds is verse 14's to add. This movement is legible where it stands.

## One form, at verse 1 and at verse 10

Two figures reach out of this movement into the Prologue at large. The cycle settled one of them and left the other open, and the difference between those two states is worth keeping in view.

The first is a shape. Verse 10 runs ἐν τῷ κόσμῳ ἦν, καὶ ὁ κόσμος δι᾽ αὐτοῦ ἐγένετο, καὶ ὁ κόσμος αὐτὸν οὐκ ἔγνω — three clauses joined by καί, turning on one noun that stands in each of them. Verse 1 is built the same way: Ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος, καὶ ὁ λόγος ἦν πρὸς τὸν θεόν, καὶ θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος. And the two openings stand against each other — ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν, *in the beginning was*, against ἐν τῷ κόσμῳ ἦν, *he was in the world*.

That the two verses share the form is settled. Barth remarks the resemblance himself at verse 10, and reads the two ἦν against one another: at verse 1 a being that precedes and encloses all time, at verse 10 a presence that is act rather than a standing condition. Westcott describes each verse's form where each verse falls — the "stately symmetry" of the first, the "peculiarly characteristic" side-by-side clauses of the tenth — without drawing a line between them.[^form]

What is *not* settled is whether the evangelist composed verse 10 to answer verse 1. That is a claim about intention, and it rests on one voice. It is held open here, and it is held open in the right place: the volume's introduction, which will take the Prologue's figures together rather than one movement at a time, is where a question of that kind can be settled.

## Where the opening restated, the movement restates

The second figure is a habit rather than a shape, and it is the commentary's own observation on a datum Barth supplies for a quite different purpose.

Barth is defending the ordinary text of verse 13 against a reader who calls that verse trivial and superfluous, and his answer is that saying nothing new is not the same as being idle. Verse 2, he points out, says nothing new in relation to verse 1; verse 11 says nothing new in relation to verse 10; verse 13 draws out analytically what "children of God" already means. None of the three is trivial for that.[^restatement]

Take the observation out of its argument and a pattern is visible. The Prologue's opening restates at once — verse 2 after verse 1 — and this movement does the same thing at two of the three places its parts meet. At the hinge-movement, verse 11 restates verse 10: he came to what was his own, and his own did not welcome him. At the close, verse 13 restates verse 12: what it is to be given the right to become children of God. At [the turn between those two](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/03-articulation-three-figures/) it does not restate, and the exception is instructive — there οὐ παρέλαβον is answered by ἔλαβον across the verse boundary on the marked δέ, which is the opposite move. The refusal is answered, not repeated.

Restating is a claim about the composition's manner, not about the contents of the verses, and the difference matters because the verses themselves do advance. The series of refusals grows more definite in the subject that refuses and more personal in the object refused, and that advance is argued where the verses are treated, not here. Two readers in the tradition press further and hold that verse 11 only explicates verse 10; their view is recorded at that verse and is not adopted. What is claimed in this section is the narrower thing: this movement builds the way the opening built.

The figure below draws the frame — the two long brackets, the pair that encloses the movement, and the descent seen from the other side — together with the first of these two figures, the form at verses 1 and 10. The second, the restating habit, is carried here in words rather than in the drawing: showing it would require the movement's three joints, and those belong to the figure that draws them.

## How firmly this is held

This section is held with less force than the two before it, and for reasons that can be named exactly.

The two long brackets are settled, and so is the fact that the movement lies outside them. The form shared by verses 1 and 10 is settled. The restating habit is a modest claim about composition and stands on the pattern itself.

What is provisional is the seating as a whole, on two counts. The first is the near pair: reading verse 4 and verse 14 as a *composed* pair — two verses written to answer one another, rather than two verses that happen to face — is held at moderate strength and is not settled here. It settles, if it settles, when verse 14 is treated. The second is arithmetic. Calling these verses the Prologue's *second* movement presupposes a third, and the third has not yet been read; verses 14 to 18 are the next cycle's work, and a reader who declined to divide the Prologue that way would keep the brackets and lose the ordinal.

Saying which part of a reading carries less weight is not a hedge. It is a way of showing the reader which sentence to watch when the next cycle arrives.

What this movement's turn does at a larger scale still — over the Gospel that the Prologue opens — is the next section's question.

Barth's own remark on the same page that "here we are on the second highest rung of the ladder" is said of verse 3, inside his exposition of that verse; he does not say where the third rung begins or ends. [^restatement]: Barth, *Witness to the Word*, p. 81: "V. 2 says nothing new in relation to v. 1, and v. 11 says nothing new in relation to v. 10, yet Zahn does not find them trivial or unnecessary. The sentence stands in Barth's exposition of the ordinary text of verse 13, answering the charge that the verse is "trivial, superfluous, verbose, and unnecessary" if it belongs with what precedes it. Barth holds the second of the three in his own voice elsewhere — verse 11 "is only the explication of v. 10c" (p. 66), and the figure of τὰ ἴδια and οἱ ἴδιοι "serves to underline the *en tō kosmō ēn* of v. 10" (p. 68), on the same page as his reading of the same verse as a sharpening. Generalizing the three instances into a habit of this movement is this commentary's step, not his; and at verse 2, the instance the pattern begins from, Barth's own exposition declines to read the verse as mere recapitulation.
