[The light that gives light to every human being](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/11-true-light-coming/) came into a world, and verse 10 says what the world did. Three short clauses, joined by καί — ἐν τῷ κόσμῳ ἦν, καὶ ὁ κόσμος δι᾽ αὐτοῦ ἐγένετο, καὶ ὁ κόσμος αὐτὸν οὐκ ἔγνω — carry the movement's first refusal: he was in the world, the world was made through him, the world did not know him. Ὁ κόσμος stands as a full noun in the second clause and again in the third. That is a marked figure, since nothing needed disambiguating and a relative clause lay ready to hand. Five things are fixed on this page: how the three clauses relate; what "the world" means, and whether it means the same thing three times; what ἦν asserts; what the middle clause is doing; and what "did not know" is.

## Two beats, not three

The Prologue itself supplies the figure's precedent. [Verse 1](/commentary/john/1-1-to-3/09-three-clauses-two-guards/) is three καί-joined clauses with ὁ λόγος in each, and it opens ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν against this verse's ἐν τῷ κόσμῳ ἦν. Barth names the parallel unsought and reads it as the Prologue's turn from the Word's deity to its turning to the world;[^barth-v1] and the threefold ὁ κόσμος of verses 10–12 is one of the sets of linking words Carson finds lending the text its deliberate pacing.[^carson-pacing]

Inside that figure the movement runs in two beats: 10a with 10b as one creation-anchored claim — the Word present in the world he made — and 10c the turn against it. The tradition states this from four independent directions, two stating the grouping and two converging on it, and one voice states it against interest. Barth: "the two preceding statements are written for the sake of this statement."[^barth-sake] Ridderbos supplies the grammatical category: the first two clauses, "Though linked paratactically … essentially have a concessive meaning in relation to the last clause of the verse: '*Although* he …'"[^ridderbos-concessive] McHugh converges from the middle clause alone: "It makes a poignant transition to 10c."[^mchugh-transition] Köstenberger converges too, setting the first two lines up against the third: "The second line reiterates, in preparation for the third line."[^eec-reiterates] And against interest, Bultmann, who construes the verse as composite, nevertheless renders it *although he was the Creator of the world, the world did not know him*, his own account of the sources having the evangelist's addition produce the concessive.[^bultmann-although]

The category is *concessive*. It is a claim about how the clauses relate, not about their subordination — which is why [the English this commentary gives the unit](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/01-the-text/) carries it as a dash, and not as a conjunction the Greek lacks.

Two things fix the single turn at 10c. The first is the middle clause's pluperfect force: the world *had come to be* through him, which makes that clause ground rather than advance.[^mchugh-pluperfect] The second is where the adversative falls — at the καί of 10c, and not at the καί of 10b. Three independent sources place it there. Harris does so twice: in his inventory of the Prologue's adversative καί (1:5b, 10c, 11), and in his note at the verse, which counts by occurrence and so calls it the "second use."[^harris-adversative] Köstenberger does so twice: in his commentary — "This sets up the contrast (adversative καί) with line three" — and in his translation, which renders the καί of 10b a plain "and" and the καί of 10c "yet."[^eec-adversative] And Ridderbos's concessive locates the adversative at the third clause, as at verse 11.[^ridderbos-adversative]

Emphatic repetition — Morris's reading, and Barth's description of the verse as turning on one concept — is absorbed here, not declined.[^emphatic] The repetition is the vehicle that carries the movement, and verse 1 is the Prologue's own proof that the figure can be emphatic and argue at the same time. What is declined is the reading that would carry the Prologue's habit of restatement into this verse's clauses, so that 10b and 10c restate 10a rather than move. It is declined on the distinction between verses and clauses, which is Barth's own practice. The restatements he names run between verses — "V. 2 says nothing new in relation to v. 1, and v. 11 says nothing new in relation to v. 10, yet Zahn does not find them trivial or unnecessary"[^barth-v2] — while his exposition of this verse asserts the movement inside it. The integration is held with very high confidence.

## "The world": one referent under three aspects

Κόσμος holds one reference across the verse: the inhabited world, the human world taken as the setting of human life. It is McHugh's "the people living on this earth";[^mchugh-people] the human world of Barth's third sense, "history as a world within the world," which Barth regards "as the most probable in the context";[^barth-third] Ridderbos's specifically human world; Bultmann's world of men; and Barrett's world of men rather than the totality.[^human-world] What changes across the clauses is the *aspect* under which that one world stands: the sphere of his presence at 10a, his own handiwork at 10b, the subject that refuses him at 10c.

The reading is stated in its own terms twice. McHugh: "The same meaning of ὁ κόσμος must apply, with the stress here on the people living on this earth."[^mchugh-same] Barth, adopting the human-world sense: "With this sense kosmos would be neutral in the first two statements, its sense being determined by its relation to the Word, this sense then being found in the third statement."[^barth-neutral] And Bultmann states it negatively: it is wrong, he holds, to distinguish two concepts of κόσμος in John, since the one world is seen in different ways.[^bultmann-concepts] Carson's own definition is built the same way — the created order, especially human beings and human affairs, in rebellion against its Maker — referent first, then tone.[^carson-definition]

The rival reading holds that the noun shifts its sense clause by clause: the inhabited earth, then the universe, then humanity in opposition to God.[^rival] It is engaged at full strength and adopted against on its own weaknesses. A noun changing its reference twice inside one sentence, in a marked and emphatic figure, is a hard claim. And Morris's own general note concedes that the senses shade rather than divide: their boundaries "are not hard-and-fast," and John "moves freely from one to another" — although at this verse Morris himself hears "a subtle shift of meaning," a single shift, from 10a–b to 10c.[^morris-senses]

Three major commentators stand against discrete senses, each on his own ground. Bultmann refuses two concepts of κόσμος, as above, while himself differentiating its usages.[^bultmann-usages] Schnackenburg canvasses the three-sense reading on the premise this commentary also works from — a single author — and rejects it as making the thought unclear and inconsequential; he himself holds the κόσμος of 10b to a different sense, "the sum of created things," but on a source-critical account this commentary does not adopt.[^schnackenburg-senses] And Barth cautions against a shift in mid-verse between his own first and second senses, and prefers the one sense. A mid-verse change of meaning, he writes, "is not impossible in John, but it is perhaps as well not to use the resultant exegetical possibility too hastily or too often"; and he allows a single shift: "If we want to assume a shift of meaning, then we have sense (1) in the first and second statements and sense (3) in the third."[^barth-shift]

What the shifting-sense reading sees truly is kept. The aspects are real, and they are exactly what its advocates describe. But the adopted reading owes a discipline, and the discipline is what keeps it falsifiable: **an aspect is claimed only where the clause's own predicate carries it, and where no predicate carries it, none is claimed.**

That discipline settles a collision [the previous verse](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/11-true-light-coming/) sent forward to this one. Köstenberger reads the term's first occurrence, at 1:9c, as "the inhabited universe without any negative connotation."[^eec-neutral] Carson reads the same occurrence and denies it: "Even in v. 9, where 'world' might at first be taken as an instance of *kosmos* with neutral force, John is setting the stage for the massive rejection recorded in v. 10."[^carson-stage] The disagreement is real and precisely joined, and it is decided by the predicate. At the first occurrence and at 10a, the predicates are coming and being-in, and neither carries rebellion: Köstenberger's neutral stands, and it stands with Barth's "neutral in the first two statements." At 10c the predicate is οὐκ ἔγνω, and there the aspect of rebellion is carried: Carson's negative stands, and no one disputes it.

What Carson's prolepsis actually asserts is that the neutral occurrence is doing set-up work for the rejection. That is a claim about composition, and it is the progression this page adopts, argued from Carson's side. Köstenberger says the same thing in his own voice: the expectation would be a welcome, and the reader will shortly be told otherwise.[^eec-welcome] So Carson's prolepsis is *received* as a claim about narrative effect and *declined* as a claim about the term's sense at that occurrence — a distinction his own referent-then-tone definition licenses. The single referent is held with very high confidence. The aspect-discipline is held with high confidence, and no higher, precisely because it is the resolution that could most easily be stretched.

## He was in the world

Ἦν asserts presence, and the presence is an advent. Barth reads the immanence as event — "in contrast to permanent immanence," since verses 5, 9 and 11 forbid a merely continuous relation.[^barth-event] Calvin, Westcott and McHugh read the Word as present; for McHugh the Logos "was, after that first entry, always present in the world."[^present] Köstenberger and Harris read verse 10 as presupposing a coming already accomplished.[^accomplished] Under [the working calibration this commentary adopted at verse 9](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/11-true-light-coming/) — presence as advent — these are not rivals but faces of one movement, because the being of this light in the world *is* its coming. That is why Barth can say on one page that the light was in the world apart from the epiphany of the Word, and on another that the immanence is act.[^barth-faces]

What the imperfect asserts can be stated with its one overclaim removed. It does not say that he was in the world before creation; the locative forbids that. It says that the one who is in the world is the one who simply *was* — the predication of eternal being with which the Prologue opens, carried into a locative clause without becoming a phase that began.

Schnackenburg canvasses two alternatives: 10a as a consequence ("hence, he was in the world," the reading of Zahn, Lagrange and Hoskyns), or as a reminder ("he was, indeed, already in the world," Ruckstuhl's). The canvass is received. Schnackenburg finds both unsatisfactory, verse 10 being concerned not with the Logos's encounter with the individual but with "his historical involvement with mankind as a whole."[^schnackenburg-alternatives] Our own objection is that they make 10a a note about verse 9, rather than the first beat of this verse's own claim.

The old disjunction — whether verses 10–13 speak of the Word's presence before the coming of Jesus, or of the ministry — is dissolved here rather than decided. Presence as advent means that the light's being in the world and its coming into the world are one movement described from two sides. So a verse asserting presence does not choose against the ministry, and a verse presupposing an accomplished coming does not choose against the pre-incarnate Word. McHugh's complementary double meaning — the reception of the Word under the old covenant, repeated at the coming of the Word made flesh — is received as convergent from other grounds.[^mchugh-double] So is Schnackenburg's layered answer, conditional on his demarcation of an underlying hymn: the hymn thinking of the time before the incarnation, the evangelist referring the verse to the incarnate Logos.[^schnackenburg-layered] The near-unanimity of the Fathers for the unincarnate Word is registered on Schnackenburg's own summary.[^patristic] And Ridderbos's refusal of a pre-incarnate phase followed by an "evangelic turn" at verse 14 is adopted: what verses 10–11 describe is "the actual historical situation," pictured against the all-determining background of the eternal Word.[^ridderbos-historical] The reading of ἦν is held with high confidence, and no higher, because it rests on the working calibration, whose full assessment belongs to the treatment of verse 14.

## The world came to be through him

Καὶ ὁ κόσμος δι᾽ αὐτοῦ ἐγένετο is a deliberate echo of [verse 3](/commentary/john/1-1-to-3/11-panta-di-autou/). Barth couples the two verses explicitly and repeatedly; Harris cross-references the verb to verse 3; Boyarin has verse 10 repeating and expanding it; Schnackenburg says it "clearly echoes" verse 3.[^echo]

Its κόσμος is *narrower* than verse 3's πάντα. Carson: the world "is not the universe."[^carson-universe] McHugh: "Though a reference to the whole Universe, as in 1:3a, is logically entailed, this is not the primary meaning here."[^mchugh-universe] Harris's reading of 10b as the whole universe, coextensive with verse 3, is the minority view, and it is registered as such. Beside it stand Bultmann's qualified whole-of-creation usage — "although even here the world of men is seen as the essential factor" — and Schnackenburg's source-critical version.[^minority]

[The absolute scope of verse 3](/commentary/john/1-1-to-3/12-oude-hen/) is untouched. Verse 3 forecloses absolutely, at the broader register; 10b does not restate that scope. It applies the same agency to a narrower, stated object. And the selection is deliberate. McHugh observes that, except here and at Acts 17:24, the New Testament nowhere says that the *κόσμος* was made by God.[^mchugh-datum] John predicates of *the world* what the tradition predicates of *all things*, at precisely the point where the world fails to know him — and that is the whole work of the clause.

It is the ground of the culpability the integration requires, and the anti-Gnostic reading is received exactly there: the world is estranged, not alien. Ridderbos puts it at full force: "The world did not know him, not because he was a stranger but because it was estranged from him, from its origin. The world *should* have known him."[^ridderbos-estranged] Barth: the Word came into the world from above, "yet not, of course, as a foreign body, but as the truth … which the world really ought to know."[^barth-foreign] Bultmann, from the least expected direction: the κόσμος is not the Gnostic fullness of evil, and human beings are world *by virtue of* being God's creation.[^bultmann-creation] And Schnackenburg allows, in a remark of his own, that 10b may have been meant to refute the notion that the world owed its origin to an evil principle — or else to stress the Logos's claim on it by right of creation, or simply the identity of the pre-existent and the incarnate Logos.[^schnackenburg-refute] The echo of verse 3, and the location of the adversative, are held with very high confidence.

## Did not know him

The world did not *know* him. Knowing here is total relatedness. In Ridderbos's account it is rooted in a choice that engages intellect, heart and will, so that "*not* to know the Word is to reject a relationship with him."[^ridderbos-relationship]

The convergence behind that is complete across traditions and methods. McHugh finds the Semitic sense: the verb was "almost certainly intended to be taken also" as meaning to know and to respond with moral commitment, on the parallel at 11b, with Isaiah 1:3, Jeremiah 9:3 and 22:15–16, and Hosea 4:1–6 as his examples of the sense.[^mchugh-semitic] For Schnackenburg, the hymn's "knowledge," like that of the Wisdom texts, is "nowhere a theoretical attitude, a rational apprehension, but the willing acceptance of instruction from God."[^schnackenburg-knowledge] For Bultmann it is not a theoretical perception of truths but recognition — an act of cognition that, if it is complete, must issue in recognition.[^bultmann-recognition] Dodd expressly refuses to read an unknown god here: God, through the Logos, is "present in the world, to be known." And he draws from verse 11's parallel that it "at least suggests that the will and not only the intelligence is at fault," which the clause of verse 10 "may be taken to" state correspondingly.[^dodd] For Morris it is more than intellectual: a failure to know and to love.[^morris-love]

Carson's caution that γινώσκω and οἶδα "seem to be used synonymously" in John bounds this reading rather than opposing it.[^carson-synonyms] The aorist is correctly described as constative: Harris takes ἔγνω as more probably constative, "did not recognize," while allowing that it "could be ingressive" — and what it constates is a whole relation refused. Harris's own gloss says "failure or refusal."[^harris-aorist] Morris, naming no category of aspect, hears in the aorist what "perhaps indicates a single action; the world missed its great opportunity."[^morris-opportunity]

Set against the first movement's [οὐ κατέλαβεν](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/09-soteriology-not-overcome/), the two verbs portray the same event under distinct aspects: the darkness that would not comprehend the light, the world that would not know him. And the three-member series — [one refusal told three times](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/03-articulation-three-figures/) — advances. The advance is claimed at the register the evidence supports, and no further. It is not a growing harshness in the verbs. It is a growing *definiteness in the subject that refuses*, and a growing *personalization of the one refused*. The darkness did not grasp the light, αὐτό, neuter. The world did not know *him*, αὐτόν, masculine and personal. His own people did not welcome *him* — the subject narrowed to a covenant household.

Three commentators support the advance across the last two members. Köstenberger reads a causal sequence: "Because the world failed to recognize its Maker, it did not receive him."[^eec-because] McHugh judges οὐ παρέλαβον somewhat stronger than οὐκ ἔγνω.[^mchugh-stronger] Schnackenburg judges it "much more likely" that the hymn "repeated the thought of v. 10 and intensified it."[^schnackenburg-intensified] Two stand against, and they are recorded as a live minority rather than answered away: Barth, for whom "v. 11 is only the explication of v. 10c,"[^barth-explication] and Bultmann, for whom verse 11 is exactly parallel to verse 10, each explaining the other.[^bultmann-parallel] The articulation adopted here survives both, since neither denies that the object becomes personal and the subject definite; what they deny is an escalation of content, and that is not claimed. The sense of the verb is held with very high confidence. The advance is held with high confidence, on a contested field — and the whole claim of the three verbs is completed at the next verse.

## The refusal, and its limits

What the verse teaches about the world, it teaches of the world as such, and two guards hold that teaching within what the text says.

What the world refuses is not information but a relation, and the refusal is culpable because the middle clause has established that the one refusing owes its existence to the one refused. The refusal is an act, not a deficit: a heart deliberately and culpably closed;[^schnackenburg-closed] a cognition that will not issue in recognition; a choice of intellect, heart and will.

The first guard is Ridderbos's: "The inner contradiction of this not knowing is not explained. The opposition simply posits itself in all its mysteriousness." With it goes his refusal to find in the verse "an unsatisfied dormant sense or preunderstanding that needed only to be activated," since what is needed is "the miracle of a new creation."[^ridderbos-mystery] The commentary declines to supply a mechanism the text withholds.

The second guard is the one the first movement established: the refusal is real, and it is not final. Barth's discrimination is adopted — if there is tragedy here, "in the mind of the Evangelist the cosmos rather than Jesus is the tragic hero."[^barth-tragic] So is his reading of the passage's key: beyond the accusation, "there sounds as the meaning of the whole passage vv. 9–13 the melody of triumph."[^barth-triumph] The Gospel's next use of the word is 1:29 — the world whose sin is taken away.

The verse thus lands as one teachable claim. He was in the world — and the world was his own handiwork, made through him — and yet the world did not know him. Not that it failed to identify him: it refused him, and what it refused was a whole relation, mind and heart and will together, owed to the one from whom it had its being. That is why the sentence is not a tragedy of mistaken identity but an indictment; and why, in this Gospel, it is not the last word. It is the seed of the Gospel's "the world has hated them" and its "the ruler of this world is judged" (15:18–19; 16:11), and it sets up the movement's next step, in which the refusal narrows from the world to a people: the light came to what was its own.
