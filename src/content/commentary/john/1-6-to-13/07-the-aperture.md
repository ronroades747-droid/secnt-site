The last section looked past the Prologue's edge, to the book the Prologue opens. This one comes back inside the passage, to a single word.

Everything this movement narrates is finished. A man came; the light was coming into the world; the world did not know him; his own did not welcome him; those who received him were given the right to become children of God; they were begotten of God. The movement tells it all [from this side of an advent already complete](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/04-temporal-payload/), in aorists that summarize and imperfects that reach back beneath them. And at one place, and only one, the telling opens onto the time of whoever is reading it. This section asks where that place is, why it is there, and how far the observation can be pressed.

## The one verb that is not past

The place is τοῖς πιστεύουσιν, *to those who believe*, at the end of verse 12. It is a present participle, and it does what no other verb in the movement does: it reaches out of the story and into the reader's own time. Harris puts it in a phrase — the change from the aorist of the receiving to the present of the believing shows that belief is ongoing and is "open to those who live after John's time," where John is the evangelist and his time is the time of writing.[^harris]

Two other present forms stand in these verses, and it is worth saying why neither of them does the same work. At verse 9 the light was ἐρχόμενον, *coming*, into the world; that participle gives the manner of the advent inside the long *was* of the verse, and it does not reach forward to the reader. At the same verse the light φωτίζει, *illumines*, every human being — the movement's one present indicative. This commentary reads that verb as naming what the light does as light, the illumination that has been at work since creation and is bestowed in a special way since the Incarnation, rather than as fixing a moment in time.[^schnackenburg] That reading is not unopposed, and the opposition is set out below, because it is exactly where the section's further claim is tested.

What does the door open onto? Bultmann supplies the terms, though not at this verse. Reading the present tense at verse 5 — *the light shines in the darkness* — he says that it refers not only to the earthly work of Jesus, which for the evangelist lies in the past, but to the revelation given to the world through that work, which "now lives on in the community." And two pages later he says that the other side of the same theme, the acceptance of that revelation by those who believe, will be stated at verses 12 and 13.[^bultmann] Carrying his community to this particular door, the participle at the end of verse 12, is this commentary's step, taken on his own placing of the believers at these verses. It is not a claim he makes about the participle.

So the passage tells a completed advent, and it leaves one door open. It opens that door at the believing, and nowhere else.

## Where the door is set

The door is not merely somewhere in the movement. It stands at the end of the turn, and the turn is the most heavily marked place in these eight verses. Four features of the text bear on it, each noticed for its own reasons.

It stands in the sentence where the asyndeton breaks. From the Prologue's opening, every new sentence has begun without a connecting particle — except the one that opens verse 12, ὅσοι δέ, *but as many as*. McHugh counts it that way, sentence by sentence, and [the connective at the turn](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/03-articulation-three-figures/) is the first of this movement's marks.[^mchugh] The participle closes verse 12, inside that same sentence.

It stands just past the point where the linking words thin out. Carson finds a set of words handed from line to line through the first part of the Prologue, up to the receiving at verse 12, and observes that from the middle of that verse on there are few such links, and the pace rises.[^carson]

It stands within the verses Barth hears as a climax. Barth takes verses 12 and 13 together as "a first climax in the prologue," and says that if he were dividing the Prologue he would break it there, after verse 13.[^barth] His climax is the pair of verses as a whole, not the participle; the participle falls inside it.

And it is the phrase [the Gospel's last sentence answers](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/06-gospel-facing-register/). *To those who believe in his name*, here; *that believing you may have life in his name*, at the book's close.

None of these four was noticed in order to locate a door. Together they show that the movement's single opening onto the reader's present was not left wherever it happened to fall. It was set where the movement turns and the book's own ending looks back. That is a claim about the composition of the whole unit, and it is held with very high confidence.

## Two movements, two plans?

A further observation is possible, and it is offered as an observation rather than a result.

In the movement before this one, the present tense sets the time. [In verses 4 and 5](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/03-macro-tense-run/) the light *shines* — φαίνει — and the aorist that follows summarizes against it. In this movement the arrangement is reversed: an aorist sets the time, and the present enters only at the believing. The first movement opens onto the reader at the light's shining; the second at the reader's own faith. Set side by side, the two could be read as inverse plans — one present-set, one aorist-set — and that would be a fact about the Prologue's design across two of its parts.

This reading is this commentary's own. No commentator consulted here states the two placements together, and the observation is not attributed to any of them.

It is also contested, at this movement's own present indicative. Bultmann holds that the meaning of the present φωτίζει at verse 9 "is no different from that of φαίνει v. 5" — the present of the revelation that, for him, is still going on. Michaels says the same thing plainly: the time reference of *illumines* in verse 9, like that of *shines* in verse 5, "is the present, the time when the author writes the Gospel."[^counter] Neither of them is answering the inversion; neither had it in front of him. But if they are right about verse 9, this movement's present enters at verse 9 just as the first movement's did at verse 5, and the two plans are parallel rather than inverse — this movement would open onto the reader at verse 9 as well as at the believing.

The reading given above, that φωτίζει names what the light does as light rather than a moment of writing, is what keeps the inversion standing. A claim about the design of two whole units cannot stand more firmly than the reading of a single verb on which it rests, and that reading is disputed by two careful readers.

## A third station?

There is a further complication, and it lies just beyond this unit.

The Prologue's third movement has its own present, at verse 15: Ἰωάννης μαρτυρεῖ, *John testifies*. McHugh reads it as reaching the same place this section has been describing — the present tense, with the perfect beside it, affirms that John's witness endures "up to the time of the writing of the Gospel. John is still witnessing." He prefers that reading to taking the verb as a vivid historical present, and names the grammarians who take it that other way.[^witness]

If that is right, then each of the Prologue's movements opens onto the reader's present at one place of its own: the first at the light's shining, the second at the believing, the third at the witness. That would describe the Prologue not as two contrasting plans but as three stations along one line. Nothing in this unit can decide between the two descriptions, because the third station is not in this unit. The question belongs to the volume's introduction, where the Prologue is read as a whole.

## How firmly this is held

That the movement's one opening onto the reader's present is set at the believing, at the end of its turn, is held with very high confidence. It is a pattern of the unit's composition, and the features that locate it were noticed independently of it.

That this placement inverts the first movement's is held with moderate-to-high confidence. It is this commentary's own observation, the verb it rests on is read differently by Bultmann and Michaels, and the Prologue's third movement may describe the pattern in a different way. It is recorded here so that the reader can see what would settle it, and it is left for the introduction to the volume.

The movement has now been read at six layers: its edges, its joints, its tenses, its frame, its face toward the Gospel, and its one open door. The next section lays the joints and the tenses over each other.

[^counter]: Bultmann, *John*, p. 53: "The meaning of the present tense φωτίζει is no different from that of φαίνει v. 5; it states that the incarnate Logos is the Revealer, without considering the question whether or to what extent men open themselves to his revelation." Bultmann's closing clause, which sets aside whether anyone receives the revelation, is quoted whole because it bears on how far his reading presses against the present at the believing.

[^witness]: McHugh, *John 1–4*, p. 61, at 1:15: "The present tense μαρτυρεῖ and the perfect κέκραγεν affirm the perduring nature of John's witness up to the time of the writing of the Gospel. His own division places verse 15 inside the Prologue's closing section, just after its climax at verse 14; the reading of verse 15 as the third movement's opening onto the reader is this commentary's.
