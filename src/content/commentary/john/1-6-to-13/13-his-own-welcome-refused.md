The refusal narrows. [Verse 10](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/12-world-did-not-know/) said the world did not know its Maker. Verse 11 says something sharper, in a single line built on one word used twice — εἰς τὰ ἴδια ἦλθεν, καὶ οἱ ἴδιοι αὐτὸν οὐ παρέλαβον. First τὰ ἴδια, neuter; then οἱ ἴδιοι, masculine: he came to what was his own, and his own people did not welcome him. This is where, in Ridderbos's words, "the prologue has reached its low point,"[^ridderbos-low] and it is the verse where the light's coming reaches its covenant destination. Four things are fixed on this page: to whom he came and who refused him, what the verb of coming asserts, what force "did not receive" carries, and how this third refusal stands to the two before it.

## His own domain, and his own people

The evangelist uses one adjective twice in one line, neuter and then masculine, and the two do not mean the same thing.

Τὰ ἴδια is the world as the Word's *own*. It is not his homeland, since, as Schnackenburg puts it, "the true homeland of the Logos is with God (cf. v. 1b)."[^schnackenburg-homeland] It is his domain — in Harris's phrase, "the world of creation (cf. 1:3) and of humans, the whole realm of created existence."[^harris-domain] Bultmann's lexical note independently requires the possession-reading: the word here can mean only *property, possession*.[^bultmann-possession] And Barth's exposition states it as the verse's emphasis: the thought of verse 10 is taken up "with a stronger emphasis on the fact that the cosmos belongs to the Logos."[^barth-belongs] So the neuter is continuous with the previous verse. What the middle clause of verse 10 established creationally — the world made through him — verse 11 states possessively. The agency of creation has become ownership, and the coming is aimed at what is owned.

Οἱ ἴδιοι are Israel — "his own people (as the masculine *hoi idioi* must be translated)," as Carson puts it.[^carson-people] They are the covenant nation that the Old Testament's formulas of possession name (Exodus 19:5–6; Deuteronomy 7:6; 14:2; 26:18).[^possession-formulas] The Gospel then narrates their refusal of him from 1:19 to 12:50. And they are named here in John's own relational idiom of belonging — οἱ ἴδιοι again at 13:1, τὰ ἴδια πρόβατα at 10:3–4 — rather than in the Septuagint's.

That is the answer to the strongest objection to the Israel reading. Bultmann observes that the Septuagint's formula for the chosen people is λαὸς περιούσιος, not this word.[^bultmann-laos] But the evangelist is not quoting the Septuagint's formula. He is speaking his Gospel's own language, the language he will use again at exactly the hinge where his own *receive* him (13:1).

The split referent is carried whole by Harris, and by Lindars as Ridderbos reports him.[^split-whole] The rest of the tradition carries it in halves. Schnackenburg, Bultmann and Michaels read the neuter as the Word's own world.[^neuter-half] McHugh, Carson, Köstenberger, Dodd, Barrett, Morris, Westcott, Lincoln, Keener, Calvin, Brown and, among the Fathers, Cyril read the masculine as Israel.[^masculine-half] The ground for dividing them is the discipline [the previous verse](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/12-world-did-not-know/) adopted — an aspect is claimed only where the clause's own predicate carries it — applied now to the two members. The neuter's predicate is a coming's *destination*, and it carries the widest register. The masculine's predicate is a personal welcome refused, and it carries the covenant register.

The two alternatives are engaged at full strength, and each is declined on its own weakness.

The first reads the neuter as a homeland. It is held by McHugh, Westcott, Morris, Barrett and Brown, by Bauer on Schnackenburg's report, and by Köstenberger's reading of the phrase as an idiom for one's home; Carson's reading tilts toward it.[^homeland] It sees truly that εἰς τὰ ἴδια is a homecoming figure, and what it sees is absorbed by the household-reading of the *masculine*. But it must make the neuter say what verse 1 forbids. Barth takes the word's sense as "home" too, but warns that "one cannot press the comparison but must take it with a pinch of salt": the world is not the Word's home "as though he came from it."[^barth-salt] That is the objection itself, and it is why his exposition lands on possession.

The second reads both members universally, as Barth, Bultmann and Michaels do.[^universal] It sees truly that the Prologue's field is universal up to this verse. It is answered rather than dissolved — by the Gospel the Prologue opens, and by 13:1. Barth concedes a point in passing: the Israel reading, he grants, "would offer an effective crescendo from v. 10 to v. 11," though he rejects it.[^barth-crescendo] Bultmann concedes nothing. For him it is "impossible in the Prologue" to take either member "to mean Israel or the Jewish people."[^bultmann-impossible]

The concentric articulation is the position's theological form, and it is ancient. Cyril of Alexandria: "the whole world is God's own, in regard to its creation … But Israel will more rightly be called his own."[^cyril-own] Its modern echo is Ridderbos's single sentence, which holds all three registers of the one refusal: "the mysterious and culpable resistance of the creature against its creator, of humankind against its own redemption, and of the people of Israel against its God."[^ridderbos-resistance] McHugh's observation that the two terms are connected is answered rather than evaded.[^mchugh-connected] They are connected by the possession itself.

One owner, one estate, one household. The verse narrows from the estate to the household inside a single line, and the change of gender *is* that narrowing.

The masculine reading is held with high confidence, verging on very high. It falls short of very high because the opposition is serious and grounded. The neuter reading, and the concentric integration of the two, are held with high confidence.

## He came

Ἦλθεν is the Prologue's first punctual verb of coming applied to the Word, and the tradition has read it three ways. For some it is the incarnation as a single decisive event — Barrett's "unique coming," Harris's "the single, decisive event of the incarnation."[^incarnation] For others it is the Word's coming to Israel through the law and the prophets across the centuries. For others still it is the beginning of the ministry.

Under [the working calibration this commentary adopted at verse 9](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/11-true-light-coming/) — presence as advent — these cease to be rivals. The coming is real, punctual in the telling, and aimed; and it is the same movement that has been the light's mode since verse 9.

At its covenant destination that movement has a *career*: the word to Israel through Moses and the prophets. Dodd reads the whole verse at that level: the word of the Lord came to his own people Israel, and Israel rejected it. The coming and the refusal together, he writes, "might serve as a brief but not inadequate summary of the whole prophetic movement."[^dodd-summary] Schnackenburg reads the hymn behind the verse in the same direction, though of the world at large: the Word "came" to the world "insofar as it existed historically and was constantly made new offers."[^schnackenburg-offers]

And its direction is the enfleshed arrival, so the verse's pointing toward verse 14 is the trajectory's own. Barth has a phrase for it, offered under a supposition. If Holtzmann is right that the verb names a single historical coming, then the passage becomes "a quiet anticipation of v. 14." Barth leans toward that reading — "Why should this not be so?" — and leaves it open.[^barth-anticipation] The anticipation is quiet precisely because advent is not new at verse 11; it is native to it.

Ridderbos supplies the two sides of the resolution in one sentence. What the evangelist pictures is "the actual historical situation," but he pictures it "against the all-determining background of the eternal Word."[^ridderbos-historical] McHugh's complementary double meaning — the reception of the Word under the old covenant, repeated at the coming of the Word made flesh — is received as reaching the same place from other grounds.[^mchugh-double] So is Schnackenburg's layered answer, conditional on his account of the underlying hymn: the hymn speaking of the Word's coming to the world of human darkness, the evangelist understanding the verse of the historical coming.[^schnackenburg-layered] This is where the old disjunction was said to bite hardest. It bites, and the calibration holds it.

One alternative is declined. Barth offers as possible, without adopting it, that the verb "is simply governed by the concrete figure of the one who returns to his homeland," and "does not have any significance beyond" the coming of verse 9.[^barth-deflationary] That reading leaves the Prologue's first punctual verb of coming idle. The reading of ἦλθεν as the one advent is held with high confidence, and no higher, because it rests on the working calibration, whose full assessment belongs to the treatment of verse 14.

## A welcome refused

McHugh reads the compound παραλαμβάνω in its sense of receiving a religious heritage by living tradition, as at 1 Corinthians 15:3 and Galatians 1:9.[^mchugh-tradition] On that reading Israel's failure is a failure to *take over* the Word as it had taken over the tradition. This commentary receives the rendering McHugh prefers — *welcome* — and declines the freight, at the level of the lexicon.

It declines it on a convergence of grammarians that includes the welcome-reading's own authorities. Barth, against Zahn: "We are not to see in the change from parelabon (v. 11) to elabon (v. 12) any shift of nuance."[^barth-nuance] Moulton, as McHugh reports him in his own note on verse 12.[^moulton] Bultmann, for whom the compound is used in this sense only here in John, and is equivalent to believing.[^bultmann-compound] Köstenberger, for whom the simple verb of verse 12 is "a virtual synonym."[^eec-synonym] The compound carries nothing the simple verb lacks.

But the clause's *force* does not rest on the lexicon. With a personal object, a covenant subject and the homecoming figure, οὐ παρέλαβον asserts a welcome refused. That is the register stated by every tradition that names the figure. Barth: "They ought to receive him willingly and joyfully as one who is at home with them."[^barth-welcome] Ridderbos: "unwilling to receive him as a welcome guest."[^ridderbos-guest] Harris: "the welcome he deserved."[^harris-welcome] Schnackenburg: the verb is "probably from the metaphor of welcoming to a house."[^schnackenburg-house]

Every engaged tradition states at least the personal non-reception of one who comes to his own. Bultmann, who denies both the home sense and a covenant subject, reads the verb as believing.[^bultmann-believe]

The aorist is constative, as Harris describes it: a comprehensive glance over the failure of the majority of the Jews. His "majority" already carries the trace of the remnant that verse 12 will name.[^harris-constative] And McHugh's bound is kept. In itself, the phrase signifies "the absence of a true welcome; it does not necessarily imply a fully deliberate rejection."[^mchugh-bound]

The culpability comes not from the verb but from the covenant context. There, his own Old Testament texts of knowing rejection apply. And there, Cyril's ancient discrimination grounds the charge. Of Israel, he writes, "it would not have been true to say 'knew him not,' when the older law had preached about him and the prophets who came after led them by the hand."[^cyril-taught]

McHugh judges the phrase "somewhat stronger than οὐκ ἔγνω."[^mchugh-stronger] That judgment is received, with its ground moved. The strength lies not in the compound but in the series: the subject is definite, the object is personal, and the refusers have already been taught. The force of a welcome refused, and the decline of the lexical freight, are both held with very high confidence.

## A belonging repudiated

[Verse 10](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/12-world-did-not-know/) fixed the universal register: the world's not-knowing refuses a whole relation owed to its Maker. Verse 11 takes the final step. His own refused *the one whose own they were*. That claim, in Barth's words, is "natural, legitimate, factual, and self-evident."[^barth-claim] It is established by creation in the neuter, focused by election in the masculine, and already taught by the law and the prophets.

The refusal of a welcome is therefore, at bottom, the refusal of self-recognition. The universal reading's own champion states it best. In Bultmann's account, the ἴδιοι are asked whether they are willing to recognize themselves as belonging to their Creator; if they refuse, they thereby assign themselves another origin.[^bultmann-origin] That sentence gains rather than loses force at the covenant level, where the belonging refused had been confessed in Israel's own scriptures.

Köstenberger closes the circle back into the previous verse. The covenant people, refusing, "became no different than the world in her rejection of its Maker."[^eec-world] That is the concentric collapse, and it is what makes the remnant of verse 12 the counter-movement.

And the two guards hold as they held at verse 10. No mechanism is supplied where the text withholds one. And the refusal is real, but it is not final — the non-ultimacy [the first movement](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/09-soteriology-not-overcome/) established. The remnant stands at verse 12. Israel's name remains honoured in this Gospel (1:31, 49; 3:10; 12:13).[^schnackenburg-honoured] Cyril's grace transferred to the Gentiles is recorded, with its patristic severity noted rather than adopted.[^cyril-gentiles] And the Gospel's next word over the rejecting world is still 1:29.

## One refusal, three tellings

The first movement handed forward a question: do verse 5's οὐ κατέλαβεν, verse 10's οὐκ ἔγνω and verse 11's οὐ παρέλαβον tell one event or three, and do they escalate?

The answer reached here is that the three verbs portray *the same event under three aspects*: the darkness that would not grasp, the world that would not know, his own who would not welcome. And the series *advances* — [one refusal told three times](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/03-articulation-three-figures/), growing in the definiteness of the one who refuses and in the personalization of the one refused. It advances at that level, and no higher.

The same-event core stands on Barth's own grouping of the three, made twice,[^barth-grouping] and on Bultmann's reading of the Gospel's usage, in which 3:11's "you do not receive our testimony" carries "the same comprehensive meaning" as the Prologue's three refusals.[^bultmann-comprehensive]

The advance stands on several witnesses. Köstenberger reads a causal sequence: "Because the world failed to recognize its Maker, it did not receive him."[^eec-because] McHugh's "somewhat stronger" stands, with its ground relocated as above. Schnackenburg judges that the hymn "repeated the thought of v. 10 and intensified it."[^schnackenburg-intensified] Cyril brings a worse charge against those who had been taught.[^cyril-worse] Dodd marks "a fresh stage," from the world to Israel.[^dodd-stage] Ridderbos finds "a sort of climax" — conditional on thinking of Israel in verse 11, a condition he then discharges, and said of the coming, from a world made by him to a people adopted as his own.[^ridderbos-climax] Barth has his own word for it: "the ou parelabon is a sharpening of the ouk egnō of v. 10."[^barth-sharpening] And there is the crescendo Barth grants the Israel reading "would offer" — a reading he rejects, so that his crescendo is a witness here only because this commentary adopts that reading at the masculine.

Of these, all but Ridderbos speak of the step from verse 10 to verse 11. The series whole is his: the negative reaction "already referred to in vss. 5 and 10," he writes, reaches its low point at verse 11.[^ridderbos-series]

Two read verse 11 as restating verse 10, and they mark the limit of the claim rather than being answered away. For Barth, "v. 11 is only the explication of v. 10c."[^barth-explication] For Bultmann, verse 11 is exactly parallel to verse 10, each explaining the other.[^bultmann-parallel] What they deny is an escalation of content, and that is not claimed. And each registers the gain in definiteness that is claimed — Barth's "sharpening," and Bultmann's "an expression which becomes clearer by its reference to the efforts of the historical Revealer to win men to him."[^bultmann-clearer] The same-event core is held with very high confidence. The advance is held with high confidence.

## Israel-first, and the guard in the grammar

Did the light's reception run first through Israel? Yes — at the masculine, as the order in which salvation came. And the universal scope of the light *of men*, [established in the first movement](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/07-anthropology-light-of-men/), is not narrowed by it.

*Israel-first is not Israel-only.* That is not a caveat imposed on the verse from outside; it is what the wordplay says. The same line that names the covenant household names the world-estate it stands in. The universal possession is asserted in the neuter of the very sentence whose masculine grieves the covenant refusal.

The tradition states the same guard in its own forms. Carson, in passing: "there is of course a sense in which all people are his own people."[^carson-all] Schnackenburg: the Word who comes to his domain "lays claim to all men."[^schnackenburg-claim]

The verse thus lands as one teachable claim. He came to what was his own — the world he had made — and his own people, the people God had made his own and taught by the law and the prophets, did not welcome him. It is the same refusal the darkness made and the world made, now at its most personal. It is not a stranger unrecognized, but a rightful owner turned away at the door of his own house, by the household that knew his name. And the sentence that grieves Israel's refusal is the same sentence that calls the whole world his: he came first to Israel, but he was never only Israel's.

It is the seed of the Gospel's first half — the public ministry the Prologue here summarizes in a verse, and its verdict at 12:37–41. It is the seed, too, of the hinge at 13:1, where his own are named again and, this time, loved to the end. Nor is the refusal the last word: the very next verse begins with those who received him.
