A passage can be jointed and still leave you unsure when it thinks it is. The seams of these eight verses are one question; the time they are told in is another, and it is settled by something less conspicuous than a connective — by which tenses the evangelist reached for, and in what order. Tenses are easy to read past. They are also, in writing this compressed, where a good deal of the argument sits: whether the light's coming is being announced or remembered, whether the world's failure to know him is a scene or a summary, whether the begetting of verse 13 stands before the receiving of verse 12 or with it. Get the tenses wrong and the passage still makes sense. It makes a different one.

The movement before this one was built the other way round. In verses 4 and 5 a present tense set the time — φαίνει, *shines* — and an aorist summarized against it, which is [the run of tenses in John 1:4–5](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/03-macro-tense-run/). Here an aorist sets the time and the imperfects reach back beneath it. That the two movements invert each other is this commentary's own observation, drawn between these two and no further; whether the Prologue works that way as a whole is a question for the volume's introduction.

This section reads the movement's tenses as one plan, and asks where the plan leaves the reader standing.

## The frame

The movement's first verb is an aorist, and a narrative one: Ἐγένετο ἄνθρωπος, *there came a man*. That verb sets the clock. What follows it is told from a vantage on the far side of what it reports — the evangelist, in Barth's phrase, standing on this side of the epiphany.[^vantage]

It matters that the aorist is a summary and not a starting gun. It does not report the moment John appeared; it takes his whole appearance into one word. Barth says so of this verse and carries the reading: the total appearance, not simply the first moment of his activity. Two further voices stand with him, at their own strengths, and it is worth saying which strengths those are, because the three are not saying the same thing. Harris places the Prologue's aorists as a class among the constatives — a grammatical home for the verb rather than an argument about verse 6, and his own definition of the class expressly admits momentary action. Chrysostom, as McHugh reports him, speaks to what the verb refers to rather than to how it views the action: not this man's coming into existence, but the mission itself, which is the referent a summarizing reading needs. One carrier and two supports.[^summary]

A frame opened by a summarizing aorist is a retrospect and not a threshold. Nearly everything else in this section follows from that.

## The interior

Inside the frame stand three imperfects: οὐκ ἦν, *was not*, of John at verse 8; ἦν, *was*, of the true light at verse 9; ἦν, *he was*, of the Word in the world at verse 10. They do not narrate the visitation the frame looks back on. They reach beneath it. They belong to the register of the opening verses, where what the Word is said to be, he *was*, and they are read there rather than inside the Baptist's aorist.

What they reach back across is the whole history of the light's presence. This commentary reads the light's being in the world as its coming — one judgment, stated here once and applied wherever these verbs recur — and behind that presence stands a career Westcott names: the Word advancing towards the Incarnation by preparatory revelations, who came in type and prophecy and judgment.[^career] Calvin holds a reach of his own, and should be quoted for the one he actually holds. At verse 9 his declared subject is the common light of nature, of which he says that it extends to every age of the world; his covenant claim comes two verses later, at verse 11.[^calvin] The equation between the light's being in the world and its coming is this commentary's, not his.

So the Word is coming from verse 9 and arrives at verse 11, and no single verse is the threshold. The tense-sequence declines to draw one, and that refusal is not a gap in the evidence. It is what the evidence says.

## Why neither traditional reading is defeated

Two readings of these verses have been in the field a long time, and the tense-plan places both without defeating either.

The first of them — the older, and the one the Fathers hand down — hears the reach, and is right to hear it. The Fathers, Westcott, Abbott and Calvin all take the light's presence at verses 9 and 10 to extend back before the flesh, and the imperfects give them their warrant. What that reading then adds is a threshold at verse 11, the point at which a pre-incarnate presence is said to become the incarnate coming. The threshold is not in the tenses. It is supplied.

The other reading takes verses 9 to 11 as the hinge on which the incarnate coming turns, and is right about where the aorists land: ἦλθεν at verse 11 reports an arrival. Carson, McHugh, Ridderbos, Lincoln, Keener, Köstenberger and Harris hold it in one form or another. What it cannot show is that the imperfects narrate that landing. Schnackenburg, who reads verse 9 as the hinge himself, is the one who supplies the observation that stands in the way: in this Gospel an imperfect marks a custom or a state.[^objection]

McHugh, who is on that second list, concedes the point himself. Surveying the two camps he judges that they are perhaps not mutually exclusive alternatives but complementary, and puts it in a sentence a reader of this section will recognise: the reception of the Word of God under the Old Covenant was repeated at the coming of the Word made Flesh.[^complementary] That is close to what this section holds, said from inside the reading with the most reason to resist it.

## The reception aorists

The five verbs of the reception are aorists: οὐκ ἔγνω, *did not know him*, at verse 10; ἦλθεν, *came*, and οὐ παρέλαβον, *did not receive him*, at verse 11; ἔλαβον, *received him*, and ἔδωκεν, *gave*, at verse 12. They summarize the advent's reception at its climax, and all five are constative — each viewing its action whole rather than watching it happen.

That claim rests on the class rather than on any one commentator's parse of any one verb, and it is worth being exact about who says what. Harris takes the Prologue's aorists as constative as a class. Carson holds that the aorist "merely establishes that the writer looks on the action holistically." Schnackenburg has these aorists reflect that the encounter between the Word and the world "took place in the reality of history." The composed symmetry of the refusal and the answer that follows it stands with them.[^class] At individual verbs the evidence is thinner, and is reported as such: Harris labels οὐ παρέλαβον a constative that sums up "in a comprehensive glance the stark failure of the majority of Jews," and Waetjen labels οὐκ ἔγνω a constative that reduces an ongoing response of denial and rejection to a single fact. That is two verbs, not five.[^individual]

Waetjen's grammar is taken here and his reference is not. He reads verses 9 to 13 as the history of the rejection of the Word before the time of Jesus — the older reading, already placed above and not adopted. A parse can be right while the reference built on it is declined, and this is one of those cases.

The crisis some readers hear in these verbs — a decisive rejection, a judgment falling — belongs to what the verbs refer to and not to their grammar. It is treated where the verbs are.

## The climactic aorist

The movement's last verb is an aorist too: ἐγεννήθησαν, *were begotten*, at verse 13. It locates the begetting as one completed divine act — for McHugh "a once-for-all event in the past," for Schnackenburg "an act (note the aorist) of divine and heavenly origin" — and it stands coincident with the reception. Westcott puts the coincidence plainly: "The act of reception coincided with the infusion of the divine principle."[^begetting]

What the tense does not do is order the two. It does not say the begetting preceded the believing, and it does not say the believing preceded the begetting. Barrett makes the first half of that point — "The aorist is not pluperfect in sense" — and Michaels makes both halves at once, finding that the text defines no temporal or causal relationship between believing and being born of God, "either to the effect that individuals are born of God because they believe, or that they believe because they are already born of God."[^ordering]

This commentary does hold that the divine act is the *ground* of the reception rather than its consequence. That reading is its own and is argued at verse 13, not here; it is carried by neither Barrett nor Michaels, both of whom decline the direction and are cited above only for what they deny. And it is a priority of ground, not of time. How the begetting and the receiving stand to one another is a mechanism the passage withholds — and the tense would not be what established it even if the passage told us.

## The present inside the aorists

Two present participles stand inside all this past, and they do two different jobs.

The first is ἐρχόμενον, *coming*, at verse 9. It sits inside the time-embracing ἦν and gives the advent's ongoing mode; it does not reach forward to the reader. It says how the light was coming, not that it is still coming to you.

The second is τοῖς πιστεύουσιν, *to those who believe*, at verse 12. That one does reach forward, and Harris says why: it is "open to those who live after John's time."[^aperture] It is the one verb form in the movement that is not past, and it is the single place where the whole retrospect opens onto the reader's own present.

So the contemporary frame of this movement enters at the reception's open door, and not at the light's shining — the reverse of the arrangement in the movement before it, which is the inversion this section began by noting.

## The run, and the one aorist outside it

Reduced to its stations, the movement runs ἐγένετο, ἦλθεν → ἦν, ἦν, ἦν → the reception aorists → ἐγεννήθησαν, with the present inside. That is this unit's own instance of [the spine of tenses the Prologue runs](/commentary/john/1-1-to-3/03-macro-tense-spine/), worked out at the Prologue's middle section — and the spine's third term is not in it. The movement is built from *was* and *came to be*, and from nothing else.

One further aorist stands outside the run and should be seen. Verse 10 says καὶ ὁ κόσμος δι᾽ αὐτοῦ ἐγένετο, *and the world came to be through him* — the creation term of verse 3 sounding again inside this movement, and set immediately beside ἐν τῷ κόσμῳ ἦν, *he was in the world*. Verse 10 is the one verse of the unit in which both of the spine's terms stand together. Its reference is creational, which is why it is not one of the five reception aorists; it is not outside the movement's articulation but its clearest single instance of it.

## How firmly this is held

The reference is held with very high confidence where it rests on convergence — the retrospect set by the opening aorist, the constative summaries, the coincident act at verse 13, and the door left open at verse 12. Four independent features of the text point the same way, and no reading of them turns the movement into a threshold.

The interior's reach is held at high rather than very high, and deliberately so. It depends on the judgment about the imperfects that this commentary states once and applies wherever those verbs recur, and a reader who declined that judgment would still have the frame, the summaries, the act and the door. Saying which part of a reading carries less weight is not a hedge; it is part of the reading.

Where this movement sits — between the opening verses and the summit at verse 14, and what frames it on either side — is the next section's question.
