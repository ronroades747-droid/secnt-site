The sentence that began at [verse 12](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/14-the-turn-children-of-god/) does not end there. A relative clause with no verb of its own until its last word runs on: οἳ οὐκ ἐξ αἱμάτων οὐδὲ ἐκ θελήματος σαρκὸς οὐδὲ ἐκ θελήματος ἀνδρὸς ἀλλ᾽ ἐκ θεοῦ ἐγεννήθησαν, *who were begotten — not of bloods, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of a husband — but of God.* It is the movement's close, and it says what it is to become a child of God by ruling out every way a human being is ordinarily brought into the world and naming God instead. Six things are fixed on this page: what the relative attaches to; what the three denials exclude, severally and together; what the begetting is; who begets, given that verse 12 said the Word gives; how God's act stands to the human turning; and what the verse is not saying — about baptism, and about the birth of Jesus.

## What the clause is doing, and to whom

The relative οἵ resumes no single word. It resumes the whole referential chain of verse 12 — ὅσοι, αὐτοῖς, τοῖς πιστεύουσιν — as the third of three grammatically odd parallels further defining αὐτοῖς, and it reaches materially to τέκνα θεοῦ, unfolding what that title means.

Barth reaches the attachment by the grammar. The clause is "a third and again a grammatically odd parallel, further defining the autois of the main clause of v. 12", and "Materially, then, the hoi undoubtedly relates to the tekna theou but grammatically it relates to the tois pisteuousin or the hosoi elabon".[^barth-attachment] Köstenberger reaches it without the grammar: "Speaking of becoming God's children, John adds another disclaimer".[^eec-disclaimer] The two stand a century apart. And Bultmann states the material reference against his own interest: "Naturally οἱ ἐγενν. explains τέκνα θεοῦ and not πιστ."[^bultmann-naturally]

The consequence governs everything that follows. Verse 13 does not assert a second, earlier begetting standing beside a later becoming; it says what the becoming *is*. That is also the syntactic ground on which the oldest internal objection to the verse is dissolved, below. The attachment is held with high confidence.

## The three denials refuse one thing

The members are not three factors separately excluded. They are one mode of origin refused three times over, under three aspects — and that one thing is the type of everything the world can do.

**Ἐξ αἱμάτων.** The plural is strange, on that account probably original, and the strangeness carries the sense.[^schnackenburg-strange] It names the generative union of two parents. Barrett: "Probably the blood of father and mother is meant."[^barrett-probably] Augustine had said it of this verse: those born of God were first born "From a mixing of blood, from the blood of male and female".[^augustine-mixing] McHugh finds Augustine's remarks "still unbettered",[^mchugh-unbettered] and Zerwick glosses the plural "pl. of mother and father".[^zerwick-plural]

And in the passage the member names a *claim of descent*. The household that did not welcome him at [verse 11](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/13-his-own-welcome-refused/) was a household defined by lineage, and four readers take the member against that claim. Calvin willingly adopts the view that the verse glances at "the preposterous confidence of the Jews", and suggests that John used the plural "that he might express more fully a long succession of lineage"[^calvin-lineage] — *longa generis successio*, as Barth quotes his Latin.[^barth-calvin] Carson: "heritage and race, even the Jewish race, are irrelevant to spiritual birth."[^carson-heritage] Morris: "The piling up of these expressions is to be understood in the light of Jewish pride of race", and "John emphatically repudiates any such idea."[^morris-pride] Köstenberger: "being a child of God is not a matter of blood relations or ethnicity; thus, Jews must not presume upon their human ancestry and descent from Abraham".[^eec-ethnicity]

**Θελήματος σαρκός** names the impulse toward that union. And σάρξ here is *creaturely and not hostile*. Seven of the major commentators read it so, each in his own words.[^sarx-seven] Bultmann states it against the pull of his own account of Johannine σάρξ, which elsewhere is the sphere of the human "as opposed to the divine" and "exerts on those who belong to it a corrupting power".[^bultmann-corrupting] And Brown reads 3:6, 6:63 and 8:15 in the same non-hostile sense[^brown-flesh] — the very passages Barth's series of hostile uses lists or extends to.[^barth-series]

That series is the one real disagreement among the major commentators on this word, and it is answered rather than outvoted. Barth reads the Gospel's σάρξ as a series set against Spirit, and makes 1:13 its anchor: the impulse is "unavoidably defined by sin," and σάρξ at 1:13 is "unmistakably human nature in its exclusively hostile opposition to God or the Spirit".[^barth-hostile] But what *this* clause sets flesh against is not sin but God. On Barth's reading the verse would be excluding sinful desire from the begetting, while what it excludes is generation. So the member's force is flesh as that which cannot generate the life of God. The word does not carry what the passage carries.

**Θελήματος ἀνδρός** names the husband's initiating will, the last and most personal of the three. The rendering *a husband*, rather than the generic "man," has McHugh, Barrett and Köstenberger behind it.[^husband] McHugh calls the husband "the one who initiates the move towards physical union", and Barth, "the decisive will and act of the male."[^initiates]

**The ἀλλά** then does two things at once. Formally it substitutes within the same slot. Four ἐκ's answer one question, *whence?*,[^harris-four] so that the fourth answer lands as an answer. Materially it *displaces to another order*, because the three have already refused the whole class to which a fourth worldly origin would belong. Harris: "Divine procreation is totally unrelated to human procreation; spiritual generation has nothing to do with physical generation."[^harris-procreation] Ridderbos: "In all three the reference is to the natural process of procreation".[^ridderbos-procreation] And Barth gives the figure its reach: "Sexual propagation is a type of other kinds of establishment that are proper to the being of the cosmos and to being in the cosmos."[^barth-type] Those born of God are born, in his words, "as no one and nothing else is born".[^barth-no-one]

The figure is inside the evangelist's own style. Abbott's observation that his "habit of stating things negatively and positively with a 'but' appears early in his Gospel", with 1:8 the first instance he lists, places it there.[^abbott-habit]

The neutrality of σάρξ is held with very high confidence. The single figure, and the two registers of the first member, are held with high confidence. The two-parent reading of the plural is held with moderate-to-high confidence, since three readers dispose of it otherwise: Ridderbos inclines to a more general sense, Robertson finds the plural unexplained unless it means both parents, and Barth records a *non liquet*.[^plural-dispositions]

## The begetting — creative, not procreative

Ἐκ θεοῦ ἐγεννήθησαν. God's own act, with no cooperating cause; effecting a real change of kind; issuing in a new mode of existence. Schnackenburg: "The birth from God is an act (note the aorist) of divine and heavenly origin".[^schnackenburg-act] The finding is entailed by the figure rather than added to it. What the three denials refuse is generation, and what the ἀλλά substitutes is not another generation but God.

The rendering follows: *begotten*, not "born." Every excluded member stands on the begetting side — the union of bloods, the impulse toward it, the husband who initiates — and the ἀλλά sets God where the husband stood. Morris: "his word 'are born' is the verb commonly used of the male parent in begetting children".[^morris-verb] Brown: "the idea of agency implied in 'begotten' is clearly more appropriate."[^brown-agency] Bultmann judges that the more specific meaning has been suppressed by the more general, "to receive one's origin"; the verse's own members answer him.[^bultmann-origin]

And the finding is bounded at both ends. Cyril of Alexandria holds both bounds with the christological reason attached. The grace conferred gathers, as it were, "into a kinship of nature that which was alien from God the Father"; and yet the evangelist "needs to exercise additional caution … in case anyone should suppose that they are in truth born of the essence of God the Father and arrive at an exact likeness with the Only Begotten."[^cyril-bounds] The lower bound is [verse 12's](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/14-the-turn-children-of-god/) claim about τέκνα, the word of derived nature: a real filiation, not a reckoning. The upper bound is Ridderbos's: the new life of the children of God is "a participation, not in the divine being, but in God's imperishable gift."[^ridderbos-participation] Children in fact, never as the Son is.

"Creative" characterizes the mode of the act. It makes no claim that the verse is shaped by the creation narrative of Genesis, and Menken's study of Genesis in John's Gospel, whose conclusion lists the passages where the Gospel's two main interests in Genesis — creation and fall, and Abraham — are used, does not list this one.[^menken] And the limit is stated rather than smoothed: every source characterizes the act, none describes a mechanism, and this commentary supplies none. The grammar is held with very high confidence and the rendering with high confidence. The mode is held with high confidence and no higher, because it rests on verse 12's finding that the status is not confined to the enfleshed arrival — itself held with high confidence under [the working calibration adopted at verse 9](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/11-true-light-coming/).

## Two agents — the Word gives, God begets

The Word gives the right (v. 12); God begets (v. 13). The θεός of verse 13 is the God of [1:1b–2](/commentary/john/1-1-to-3/07-pros-ton-theon/), and is not the Word — though the Prologue does not yet call him the believers' Father, a title that waits for 20:17 and is not anticipated here.

The two acts are composed and ordered, and Barth states the order. What the Word gives relates to what God does or has done to them: "As the proclamation of a right it issues from existent divine power and its exercise. The Word summons them to be what they already are from God".[^barth-summons] This confirms from below the limit Barth set on his own claim at verse 12, that the Word's giving concentrates the Father's gift and the Spirit's and does not exclude them[^barth-concentration] — since that is exactly what this verse would have broken had the claim been taken without its limit.

The Spirit is not named here and is not imported. Verse 13 gives the source and withholds the mode, and the Gospel supplies the mode itself at 3:5–8. That is where Brown's reading belongs, that "only the communication of the Spirit begets men as God's children".[^brown-spirit] The fuller theological articulation of how Father, Word and Spirit stand in this gift belongs to the commentary's theology of this verse, and is not settled here. The agency is held with high confidence at each of its parts.

## Constitution and turning — a priority of ground, the mechanism withheld

One distinction runs beneath the whole passage: between God's constituting of his children and the human turning to him. Here it is grounded, and the ground is in the verbs. John marks the difference by *voice and source*: active verbs with the people as subjects at verse 12 (ἔλαβον, πιστεύουσιν), a passive with God named as source at verse 13 (ἐκ θεοῦ ἐγεννήθησαν). "Constitution" and "turning" are this commentary's words for what John marks by voice and source. Barth: "It is not what the hosoi are or do, but what is done for them in their relation to the Word."[^barth-done]

A priority is asserted — by the voice-shift, by the completed aorist, and by the giving that issues from the divine act already exercised. Its *manner* is not. Carson is right that "these verses refrain from spelling out the connection between faith and new birth",[^carson-refrain] and this commentary refrains with him. It declines Barrett's order, that "This birth is conditional upon receiving Christ and believing on his name"[^barrett-conditional] — read by Carson as making faith at verse 12 the condition of the new birth.[^carson-condition] That conditioning is what [the reading of verse 12](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/14-the-turn-children-of-god/) declined, where believing in his name was found to define the receivers and not to condition the grant.[^bultmann-order] It declines equally the flat converse, which would spell out what the evangelist declined to spell out. And it reports rather than deploys Ridderbos's appeal to 1 John 5:1.[^ridderbos-1john]

The priority is a priority of ground, not of narrated sequence. The tense encodes no ordering of believing and being begotten. Barrett: "The aorist is not pluperfect in sense".[^barrett-aorist] The distinction is held with high confidence. The order is held with high confidence and no higher — the commentary asserts a priority the text carries and refuses a mechanism the text withholds.

## Galot answered, on the plural text

Galot, the modern advocate of the singular reading, *who was begotten*, whose case McHugh sets out, pressed two internal observations that [the decision for the plural](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/01-the-text/) did not touch. Both are answered here, on the plural text and without emending it.

His second point, as McHugh reports it: "How can those who have already been begotten of God (v. 13) be given the power to *become* children of God?"[^galot-second] — verse 12 saying that believers *became* children while verse 13 says they were *already begotten*. It dissolves at the syntax. The relative explains what the becoming is rather than asserting a prior birth beside it. Brown, meeting the same question in another scholar's form, reaches the same dissolution independently: "this is to impose too exact a logic on the sequence. Verse 13 explains what is meant by God's children".[^brown-logic] What remains of the "already" is real, and it is priority of ground, not chronology.

His other question — why the negatives should be stressed at all, if believers and not the Word are the subject[^galot-first] — is answered from [verse 11](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/13-his-own-welcome-refused/), and the answer runs against the reading he built on it. Denials of bloods, of the flesh's will, of a husband's will have no purchase on a claim about how Jesus was conceived. They have exact purchase on a household claiming God by descent. The feature Galot found inexplicable on the plural is the feature the plural explains and the singular does not.

## Not baptism

The Fathers read verses 12 and 13 in baptismal settings, and Schnackenburg reads baptism into the verse: "The Christian reader cannot but think of baptism (cf. 3:5)".[^schnackenburg-baptism] The claim is doubled in his own pages — authorial in his commentary on verse 12, receptional here.[^schnackenburg-doubled] Its warrant is nowhere in verse 13's language. Eight of the major commentators read at their own pages on this verse, and none finds baptism in it.[^baptism-eight] Barrett denies it in terms at verse 12, on the word ὄνομα: "ὄνομα is not enough to suggest that John has baptism in mind."[^barrett-baptism] And Morris denies it of the passage, against Schnackenburg by name: "there is nothing in the passage about baptism; John is writing about faith, not any ecclesiastical action."[^morris-baptism]

What is received is real. There is the idiom's baptismal colouring. And there is Schnackenburg's anti-Gnostic point, that sonship is acquired and not innately possessed,[^schnackenburg-gnostic] which survives entire because the finding about the mode of the begetting asserts it. The patristic baptismal material is setting, not referent: Cyril of Jerusalem's "you, who are to be illuminated" addresses candidates for baptism, and Augustine's "the second birth is from God and the church" develops the verse rather than identifying its referent.[^fathers-setting] A text preached at a baptism is not thereby a text about baptism.

## Not an allusion to Jesus' birth — though the resemblance is real

The church's oldest reading of the verse, as a testimony to the virginal conception, is answered on its merits and not on the manuscripts alone.

The correspondence is real — real enough to generate a variant: Latin witnesses, a Syriac line, Irenaeus, Tertullian and the *Epistula Apostolorum*.[^variant] But Barrett spends that correspondence explaining the variant one sentence before offering it as evidence of the evangelist's intention, and it cannot do both.[^barrett-both]

Brown reports Bernard's reading from the physiology the ancients assumed, on which "the three negatives in the verse rule out woman, lust, and man. Such an interpretation eliminates any use of the text to prove the virgin birth of Jesus."[^brown-bernard] Barth, in the 1925/26 text of his lectures, makes the point from ἐξ αἱμάτων: read of Jesus, the verse "says much more than the virgin birth, ruling out all human cooperation", while the birth told in Matthew and Luke is "one without a father but not without a mother."[^barth-mother] That removes the text from the service of a virginal conception, which excludes no mother. Barth's counterargument from the context and Schnackenburg's argument that the singular would "anticipate the subsequent affirmation of the Incarnation" stand independently.[^anticipate]

The Fathers perceived something real and located it wrongly. That is this commentary's finding. The three denials refuse one mode of origin, and the virginal conception is the one narrative in the tradition where that same mode is refused of a person. Barth's account is a different one: the patristic singulars "need not be quotations (with one important exception). They may just be free allusions", since in the light of the virgin birth it is natural to speak of Christ in the phrases of this verse "without thinking that this is its real meaning."[^barth-allusions] And Tertullian, pressing the singular hardest, turns out on Schnackenburg's reading to be "an indirect witness to the antiquity of the plural in Africa".[^tertullian]

Both rulings — not baptism, and not an allusion to Jesus' birth — are held with high confidence. The virgin-birth question's remaining places in the Gospel, 1:45 and 6:42, lie beyond this passage.

## The verse does its own work

Dodd calls verse 13 "merely a comment on the idea 'children of God'".[^dodd-merely] The observation is right, and Barth and Schnackenburg make it too: "Strictly speaking, this is not saying anything new", and verse 13 "explains how to 'become children of God'".[^restatement] Dodd's "merely" fails.

It fails first by parallel. Barth: "V. 2 says nothing new in relation to v. 1, and v. 11 says nothing new in relation to v. 10, yet Zahn does not find them trivial or unnecessary."[^barth-parallel] The Prologue's own method includes analytic restatement. It fails second by function. The clause is, in Barth's words, "very much needed"[^barth-needed] against a misreading of verse 12 that Zahn actually accepted — that receiving and believing are the presupposition of the giving, as though the receiving purchased the right.

So the verse guards forward and backward at once. It protects verses 5, 10 and 11 from being read as though unbelief were natural and faith obvious;[^ridderbos-protect] and it protects verse 12 from being read as though the turning constituted the children. And it is a relative clause with no independent verb, so the seam of the Prologue cannot fall between verses 12 and 13. The movement's back edge is where [the reading of the movement's bounds](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/02-bounded-movement/) placed it, after verse 13, and not before.

## Not something the world can produce

The verse thus lands as one teachable claim: to become a child of God is not something the world can produce. Not by the union of two parents, not by the impulse toward it, not by a husband's decision — not by any of the ways a human being is brought into being, which are here made to stand for everything the world can do — but of God.

God creating, not God procreating. A real change of kind, without anyone made divine. Children in fact and not merely in name, though never as the Son is.

The Word gives the right and God does the begetting, and the giving rests on the begetting. God's act comes first as the ground — though John does not say how it meets faith, and this commentary does not say either.

It is the seed of the Gospel's "unless one is begotten from above" (3:3–8), where the mode this verse withholds is supplied, and of the Epistle's "everyone who believes that Jesus is the Christ has been begotten of God" (1 John 5:1). And it is the movement's close. The sentence that opened with those who received him ends with God — which is what the reception, all along, was resting on.

[^bultmann-order]: Bultmann holds the same order — believers are God's children "by virtue of their faith" (*John*, p. 59) — and grounds it on verse 12: that procreation by God is not the "root and presupposition" of faith, as Holtzmann thought possible, "is shown clearly by v. 12" (p. 59 n. 4). The ground is the reading of verse 12 declined there.

He adds that "The two-sidedness in the redemptive order consisting of God's gift and human responsibility, the secret of which the Fourth Gospel does not unravel, will force itself on our attention again and again in the Gospel."
