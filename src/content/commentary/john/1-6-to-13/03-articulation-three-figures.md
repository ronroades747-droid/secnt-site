Where a movement begins and ends is one question. How it is jointed inside is another, and it is the harder of the two, because a reader who has settled the outer walls can still read the interior as a single undifferentiated stretch. These eight verses are not that. They divide, they divide in three, and the divisions are not a convenience laid over them from outside: each one is marked in the text's own grammar or its own vocabulary, and the division tradition has been finding them there, by several unrelated routes, for a century and a half.

This section sets the three joints, and puts at each of them the figure that stands there.

## The three seams

The first beat closes at verse 8. Verses 6 to 8 are the parenthesis about the man sent from God — introduced, given his commission, and then fenced off from the thing he witnesses to by a flat negative: *he was not the light*. That negative is the seam. It has no work to do inside the parenthesis, which has already said what John was for; it is there to clear the ground for the sentence that follows.

The light resumes at verse 9, and verses 9 to 11 then run as one movement. Its three verbs make a crescendo — ἐρχόμενον, *coming*; ἦν, *was in the world*; ἦλθεν, *came to his own* — a rise Westcott heard in the verbs themselves and called a climax, and Ridderbos heard in the argument, verse 11 being for him the place where the Prologue reaches its low point.[^crescendo] The two are not saying different things. The coming intensifies and the reception worsens, and they intensify together: the nearer the light comes, the more particular the refusal becomes.

The turn comes at ὅσοι δέ, *but as many as*. It is the one connective that opens a sentence anywhere inside this unit — every other sentence from verse 1 through verse 11 begins without one, in a chain of asyndeton that McHugh traces across the whole Prologue up to this point.[^turn] It is also where the Prologue's catchword chain runs out. Up to here each clause has been handing a word forward to the next, and from just past this point the links thin and, in Carson's account of the Prologue's pace, "the rise in pace adds forward movement." Two independent features of the text's surface, grammatical and stylistic, mark the same place. Then verses 12 and 13 close as a single sentence, and the unit is over.

So the movement runs: parenthesis, hinge-movement, the reception's turn. At the three joints of that articulation stand three figures — each one established at its own verse in the exegesis that follows, and each one located here.

## One refusal, told three times

Across the hinge-movement, and reaching back across the seam into the first movement, one refusal is told three times: οὐ κατέλαβεν, *did not overcome it*, at verse 5; οὐκ ἔγνω, *did not know him*, at verse 10; οὐ παρέλαβον, *did not receive him*, at verse 11. The series is not repetition. It advances on two axes at once. The subject becomes more definite — the darkness, then the world, then his own — and the object becomes more personal, from a light to a person to a person with a claim on those refusing him. The third refusal is the worst not because the verb is stronger but because of who is doing it.

## The verb-pair across the verse boundary

At the turn the figure is a pair of verbs set across the verse boundary. Verse 11 ends οὐ παρέλαβον, *did not receive him*; verse 12 opens ἔλαβον, *received him* — the same verb with its prefix and its negative both dropped, and the marked δέ carrying the answer. The point of the pair is what it declines to do. It would have been easy, and rhetorically satisfying, to extend the refusal into a fourth term. Instead the refusal is answered. Three refusals and a reception, not four refusals.

## The triad that answers

At the close, verse 13 sets three negations against one affirmative: οὐκ ἐξ αἱμάτων, οὐδὲ ἐκ θελήματος σαρκός, οὐδὲ ἐκ θελήματος ἀνδρός, ἀλλ᾽ ἐκ θεοῦ ἐγεννήθησαν — *not of bloods, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of a husband, but of God they were begotten*. Ridderbos reads the verse as reaching back to the negative statements of verses 5, 10 and 11 and guarding them: they are there to keep the refusals from being misread, as though unbelief were the natural response and faith the obvious one.[^backreference] That guarding function is his. The further observation — that a triad answering a triad answers it member for member, by position in the series rather than by any matching of content between the members — is this commentary's own reading of the figure, made here and at verse 13.

## What the division tradition carries

The seams are not this commentary's discovery, and the witnesses reach them by methods that have nothing to do with each other.

Barrett, Lincoln and McHugh each print a division of the Prologue that closes a section at verse 8 and opens one at verse 9 — Barrett from detailed exegesis, Lincoln from the present form of the text, McHugh from the change of introductory verb.[^division] Lincoln heads his own section on verses 9 to 13 "the two types of response," which names the turn without arguing for it; and Barrett, summarising the same verses, writes that the evangelist "is careful to note and allow for the few who heard, believed, and received." Neither is drawing a seam at the turn as a structural claim; both are registering the reversal there as they read.

The source-critics reach the same two joints for an entirely different reason, and their extents are worth naming rather than collapsing, because they do not agree with one another.[^sourcecritics] Bultmann's insertion is verses 6 to 8 exactly, and he assigns verse 12c and verse 13 to the evangelist's own comment; his seams fall where this commentary's do. Schnackenburg's insertion is also verses 6 to 8, but his reconstructed hymn contains no part of verse 12 at all, so his second seam falls between verses 11 and 12 rather than inside verse 12. Brown and Lincoln both close the first insertion at verse 9 rather than verse 8, Brown printing verses 6 to 9 as a parenthesis and calling verse 9 the editor's transition; both then place the second seam inside verse 12, as Bultmann does. What is received from all four is the observation that seams exist at these places. The explanation offered for them — that the seams are the marks of insertion into a pre-existing source — is declined here, and declining it costs nothing: a seam drawn by an unrelated method and landing in the same neighbourhood is evidence for the articulation whatever one thinks caused it.

## The split at verse nine

Where the tradition divides, it divides at verse 9, and the division is instructive rather than embarrassing. Brown's parenthesis runs to verse 9 and takes it backward, into the Baptist material. Schnackenburg's reconstructed strophe joins it back to verse 4. Keener's stanza takes it into the opening section with verses 4 and 5. Gregory of Nyssa, citing the Prologue in the fourth century, repeatedly fuses the true light of verse 9 with the shining in the darkness of verse 5, so that his running text moves from verse 5 to verse 9 without the Baptist verses between.[^split]

Every one of those readings pulls verse 9 backward. The printed divisions of the last century and a half pull it forward. That is not a contradiction to be resolved: verse 9 is a hinge, and a hinge seen from outside shows two faces. What this commentary holds is that its forward face is the primary one — the light resumes and the movement opens — with a real but lesser backward face toward verse 5. The split in the tradition is what that double aspect looks like when each reader has to choose one side and print it.

## How firmly this is held

The seams themselves are held with very high confidence. Three independent kinds of evidence converge on them: the text's own grammar at the turn, the printed division tradition, and the source-critical reconstructions arriving at the same joints from the opposite direction. The reading of the interior of verses 9 to 11 — what the crescendo of the three verbs locates in time — is held at high rather than very high confidence, and deliberately so; it depends on a judgment about those verbs that this commentary states once and applies throughout, and it is treated where the verbs are.

What the three beats carry in time, and why an advent is being narrated from the far side of it, is the next section's question.
