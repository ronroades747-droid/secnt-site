The unit opens where a man's coming-to-be opens it and closes where a begetting closes it, and both edges are the evangelist's own. Where a movement starts and stops is not a decorative question. It settles what belongs to the argument and what stands outside it — whether the witness sent from God is inside this movement or before it, whether the sentence about being begotten of God closes what precedes or opens what follows. Get the edges wrong and every reading downstream inherits the error. This section sets them, and shows what carries them.

## The front edge

The front edge is the parenthesis's own. At verse 6 the Prologue's grammar changes. Ἐγένετο ἄνθρωπος, *a man came to be*, is narrative style, set against the ἦν, *was*, that has governed verses 1 through 5 — and Barrett puts the change in a sentence: with verse 6 "for the first time the stage of history is reached."[^front] The form is the sacred-narrative idiom by which the Greek Old Testament introduces a man into a story, and on the Prologue's own spine it does something further: this is the first time the Prologue's verb of becoming is applied to a person. The Word *was*. The man *came to be*. That contrast is not incidental to the movement; it is the movement's opening move, and the verb that makes it is the same verb the Prologue has been using of creation.

## The back edge

The back edge is the close of the long sentence that runs through verses 12 and 13. What follows it is marked as new in three ways, and the three are independent of one another.

The first is a matter of connectives. From verse 12 onward the Prologue's clauses stand without them; the καί that opens verse 14 is the first connective the Prologue has used since verse 12, and it opens rather than continues.[^edges] The second is a matter of subject. At verse 14 ὁ λόγος, *the Word*, returns as a named subject — "for the first time since v. 1," as Barth observes, the term having been carried implicitly through everything between.[^edges] The third is a matter of shape: in Ridderbos's account of the Prologue, at verse 14 "the third and last segment of the three-part cycle begins."[^edges] The movement does not trail off at verse 13. It is closed there, and something else starts.

## What carries the edges

Neither edge rests on this commentary's judgment alone. Both are carried by the division tradition — and carried from independent methods, and from opposite sides of the question whether the Prologue reworks an earlier hymn.

Eight traditions set a seam after verse 13. Ridderbos does, reading the Prologue in the form it has come down in. Schnackenburg does from the other side, working source-critically and still concluding that in the light of the Prologue's present structure one should distinguish three sections — verses 1 to 5, verses 6 to 13, and verses 14 to 16 or 18. Barth hears at verses 12 and 13 "a first climax in the prologue," and says that were it a matter of dividing the Prologue he would make his break at that point, after verse 13. Westcott sets the seam in his own analytical table. Michaels and the Evangelical Exegetical Commentary set it while denying that the Prologue is a unity as a composition at all — which makes them witnesses of a particular kind, since the boundary survives in the hands of readers who have every reason to draw it elsewhere. Keener sets it by counting lines, in a reconstruction he offers with open hesitation. And Bultmann sets it at the close of his own section.[^division]

Nine set the movement's opening at verse 6, the International Critical Commentary and the critical division among them.[^division]

## The voices that look backward

Two observations at the front edge look, at first, as though they blur it, and neither does.

The first is Ridderbos's, on the same page where he says that at verse 6 a new cycle of thought begins: verses 6 and 7, he notes, "link up with the statement in vs. 5." The second is Bultmann's, that verse 5 already anticipates what verses 9 to 13 will unfold.[^backward] Both are true observations, and this commentary receives them as true. What they describe is not a seam in the wrong place but the backward face of a hinge — the way a movement's threshold still faces the room it has just left. A reader standing at verse 6 can see verse 5 behind him. That is what a threshold is for.

It is also, on Ridderbos's own account, what a concentric composition does. He suggests that in verses 9 to 13 one can perhaps speak of a second concentric cycle of thought, in which the categories of the first come back — this time, after what has been said about John as witness, in their kerygmatic implications.[^backward] A second cycle that returns the first cycle's categories will of course reach backward. Reaching backward is how it returns them. The reach does not dissolve the boundary it reaches across; it presupposes one.

## Why the edges hold

The bounded movement is held here with very high confidence, and it rests on two grounds of different kinds.

The first is the convergence just described: eight traditions at one seam and nine at the other, from methods that do not share premises and from scholars who disagree about almost everything else in the Prologue, including whether it was composed as a unity at all.

The second is structural, and it is the stronger of the two. The eight settled readings this commentary reaches in the verses that follow were not reached independently of one another. Each was made with reference to its neighbours: the parenthesis about John closes where the light resumes; the low point of the world's refusal is answered across a verse boundary; the one-sentence close of verses 12 and 13 gives the climactic act that the unit's tense-plan has been moving toward; and the frame that opens the whole opens on the very person the parenthesis introduced. Take away the reading of 1:6–13 as a single movement and those eight resolutions do not become eight separate small problems. They become unintelligible. The movement is not an overlay on the readings; it is what the readings jointly require.

That is the unit's outer shape. What happens inside it — how the movement is jointed, and the three figures standing at its joints — is the next section's question.
