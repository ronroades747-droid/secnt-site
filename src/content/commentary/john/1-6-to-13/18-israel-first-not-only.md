The exegesis has read the verse, and the theology turns to what it teaches. [The verse of his own](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/13-his-own-welcome-refused/) was read clause by clause: he came to what was his own, and his own people did not welcome him. This page does not argue those clauses again. It asks what the church, on the strength of them, has learned to confess — and the second refusal yields a doctrine of election and its refusal.

It is held with the guard the verse itself supplies.

## First through Israel

The light's reception ran first through Israel. Israel was the covenant household inside the estate that was his, the people taught by the law and the prophets. For that reason, as Cyril saw, it could not be said of them merely that they did not *know* him.[^cyril-known] The world did not know its Maker. His own had been told who he was.

Their refusal is the Gospel's first half in a verse. The public ministry the Prologue here summarizes runs from the Baptist's witness to the verdict of 12:37–41, and it is the story of his coming to his own and their not receiving him.

Three things follow from this, and one thing is refused.

## A belonging repudiated

First, the guilt of the refusal is the guilt of a belonging repudiated. His own refused the one whose own they were. What they turned away was not a stranger's claim but a rightful owner's.

The refusal of a welcome is therefore, at bottom, the refusal of self-recognition. The universal reading's own champion states it best. In Bultmann's account, the ἴδιοι are asked whether they are willing to recognize themselves as belonging to their Creator; if they refuse, they thereby assign themselves another origin.[^bultmann-origin] That sentence gains rather than loses force at the covenant level, where the belonging refused had been confessed in Israel's own scriptures. And so the covenant people, refusing, "became no different than the world in her rejection of its Maker."[^eec-world]

That is the doctrine's hard edge. Election does not lessen the guilt of refusal; it is what makes the refusal personal. A people taught to know him, and named as his, did not receive him.

## The refusal is real, and not final

Second, Israel's name remains honoured in this Gospel.[^honoured] It is the Israel to which he is revealed (1:31), the King of Israel confessed (1:49), the teacher of Israel (3:10), and the King of Israel acclaimed (12:13).

And the remnant stands at the very next verse. [Those who received him](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/14-the-turn-children-of-god/) were given the right to become children of God. The refusal is real, and it is not final. The Gospel's hinge at 13:1 will name his own again, and this time he loves them to the end. That last reading is a claim at the level of the whole Gospel, and it is [held provisional](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/) outside this volume's scope.

## First, and never only

Third, the order in salvation's history is real: he came first to Israel.

And here is the one thing the doctrine refuses: the narrowing that this order has so often been made to carry. The universal scope of the light *of men* is not diminished by the covenant refusal. The same line that grieves the household's refusal calls the whole world his. He came to what was his own, the world he made, and within it to his own people.

*Israel-first is not Israel-only.* That is not a caveat imposed on the verse from outside; it is [what the wordplay says](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/13-his-own-welcome-refused/). The guard is written into the verse's own grammar.

Cyril drew a severer conclusion: when Christ was not received, the grace passed to the Gentiles.[^cyril-gentiles] His reading is recorded here, with its patristic severity noted, and it is not adopted.

## The register of the claim

The doctrine rests on what the exegesis held, and is held where the exegesis held it. That "his own people" names Israel is held with high confidence, verging on very high; it is held short of that because the opposing reading is serious and was answered rather than dissolved. That "his own" place is the world he made, his own domain, is held with high confidence, and so is the reading of the one line as a narrowing from the estate to the household within it. The hinge at 13:1 belongs to the Gospel as a whole and is held provisional here.

The guilt of a belonging repudiated, and the order of first and never only, are not further findings beside these. The first is what the covenant reading entails. The second is what the wordplay itself says.

## The pastoral hearing

The pastoral hearing is plain. Privilege is not possession, and being taught is not the same as welcoming. The household that knew his name is the one that turned him from the door.

And the line that grieves his own people's refusal is the same line that calls the whole world his. That some did receive him — and what was given to them — is where the theology turns next.
