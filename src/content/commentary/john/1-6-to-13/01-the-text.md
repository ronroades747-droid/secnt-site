The exegesis of this unit proceeds on a fixed text, and this first section establishes it. The situation is unusual in a way that clarifies rather than complicates: across eight verses there is exactly one point of decision, and it falls at the last of them. Verses 6 through 12 raise no textual question at all in any source this commentary engages. Verse 13 raises one, and it is among the most consequential in the Prologue — whether the verse describes the begetting of believers or the begetting of Christ. Naming which is which is part of the commentary's discipline of disclosure: the reader is shown where the working text rests on a judgment made here, and where it rests on nothing more contested than the agreement of the whole tradition.

## The text where it is not in question

The unit is read on the common text of the critical editions, adopted without emendation. The printed text was checked against the standard critical edition, with the Tyndale House edition consulted at verse 13, before the working text was settled.

Verses 6 through 12 carry no variation unit in any engaged source. The apparatus of the sixth edition of the United Bible Societies' Greek New Testament records none; its companion textual commentary carries no entry at any of them; the recent Evangelical Exegetical Commentary raises no reading; and the major commentaries this volume reads directly are silent verse by verse. The silence is not an absence of attention but a convergence: there is nothing at these verses for an apparatus to record.[^sweep]

Gregory of Nyssa's citations, where they exist, agree with that text. He carries τὸ φῶς τὸ ἀληθινόν, *the true light*, at verse 9; εἰς τὰ ἴδια ἦλθε, *he came to what was his own*, at verse 11; and ὅσοι ἔλαβον … γενέσθαι, *as many as received … to become*, at verse 12, in the *Oratio catechetica* and elsewhere. None of them carries a reading that diverges from the critical text.[^sweep]

One question at verse 9 looks textual and is not. Whether the participle ἐρχόμενον, *coming*, attaches to the light or to every human being raises no variant at any layer of the tradition; every witness reads the same words in the same order, and what divides interpreters is how to construe them. It is wholly a matter of reading, and it is taken up in the treatment of verse 9 (§5.3).

## The crux at verse 13

The one named crux in the unit is whether the relative clause of verse 13 predicates the begetting of believers, in the plural — οἳ … ἐγεννήθησαν, *who were begotten … of God* — or of the Word, in the singular, ὃς … ἐγεννήθη, *who was begotten*, so that the verse becomes the Fourth Gospel's own testimony to the virginal conception. The stakes are plain. On the singular, John says at the threshold of the incarnation what Matthew and Luke say in their infancy narratives; on the plural, he says instead that those who received the Word were begotten of God, and the verse belongs to the reception, not to the Christology.

This commentary reads the plural, and the decision is as firm as text-critical decisions become.

**The documentary evidence.** Every Greek manuscript without exception reads the plural, and both Bodmer papyri carry it. The unanimity is of the Greek and of nothing else, and it is worth stating with that precision, because the singular's whole attestation is versional and slight: one Old Latin manuscript, Codex Veronensis (*b*); an eleventh-century lectionary from Toledo; and the Curetonian Syriac — the last incoherently, since it carries a plural relative pronoun above its singular verb, so that a literal rendering would run *those who … was born of God*. The apparatus tradition rated the plural at its highest level of certainty through the fifth edition of the United Bible Societies' text; the sixth prints no variation unit at the verse at all, and its companion commentary therefore carries no entry. At the level of the printed editions the question is closed.[^documentary]

**The transcriptional evidence.** A change from plural to singular is triply motivated, and a change the other way is not motivated at all. A scribe had every reason to move toward the singular: the desire for an explicit Johannine witness to the virginal conception; the pull of the singular αὐτοῦ, *his*, immediately preceding; and the *a fortiori* reasoning Carson names — if Christians are born of God, how much more true is this of Jesus. A change from singular to plural, on the scale the manuscript evidence would require, has no comparable explanation; as Schnackenburg puts it, it is hard to imagine that such a testimony would have been later eliminated. Scribal tradition tends to become more christological in transmission, not less, and does not dilute a reading of that value.[^transcriptional]

Two readers turn the singular's own credentials against it. Tertullian, the earliest and strongest witness to the singular, charges that the plural is a Valentinian falsification — and both Barth and Schnackenburg observe that the charge defeats itself. Readers of the plural would have taken ἐγεννήθησαν with the preceding τοῖς πιστεύουσιν, *to those who believe*, in any case, so that the supposed falsification frustrates its own purpose; and the plural ascribes birth from God to all believers, which is not the Valentinian doctrine of a select seed but its contradiction. Tertullian is therefore an indirect witness to the antiquity of the plural in Africa. The two reached that conclusion some forty years apart and without contact.[^transcriptional]

**The grammar and the context.** The sentence's own construction demands the plural. Barrett gives two grounds: the plural reading is demanded by τοῖς πιστεύουσιν in the previous verse, and by the sense of the passage. To these this commentary adds a third of its own: the corporate ὅσοι ἔλαβον, *as many as received*, which the relative clause caps, requires a plural subject at its close. And Johannine usage never predicates begetting-by-God of Jesus, while predicating it repeatedly of believers.[^grammar]

**The patristic singular, and what class of witness it is.** Irenaeus and Tertullian sharpened into text what Justin and Hippolytus already heard. That is best read as the same behaviour Gregory's citations of this verse exhibit — a text being *applied* to Jesus in use, not a rival manuscript text standing behind the application. The witness class that carries the singular is adaptation, and adaptation is precisely the class this project's own collation discipline ranks below citation-grade attestation. That the singular is deployed in polemic is a further observation of this commentary's own; the collation discipline classifies by verbal form alone and knows nothing of the setting in which a Father writes. Barth had already made the same discrimination in his lectures on John: the patristic passages in which Christ is spoken of in the singular need not be quotations — with one important exception, which he concedes and nowhere names — and may just be free allusions, since in the light of the virgin birth it is natural to speak of Christ in the phrases of this verse without taking that to be the verse's meaning.[^reception]

**The state of the question.** The modern adjudicating consensus is complete across traditions and methods, with the printed editions behind it. The singular's twentieth-century advocacy — Zahn, Blass, Loisy, R. Seeberg, Burney, Braun, Boismard, Mollat, Dupont, Galot, and the *Jerusalem Bible* — is an imposing minority, and its case is chiefly internal. Its one substantial external argument is Braun's: that Irenaeus, and perhaps Justin, should be preferred above all other external authorities for their proximity to Ephesus. Its best mediating proposal is an Aramaic dittography, by which a copulative *waw* repeated at the end of verse 13 would have turned an Aramaic singular into a plural; its own proposer judges it a reasonable explanation of all the data while predicting that it is unlikely to commend itself to many, and lets the plural stand as his text without pressing the emendation.[^consensus]

The plural is held with very high confidence. This commentary's option of locking a working text while acknowledging that the evidence does not adjudicate the original from the secondary was considered and declined, for a reason specific to this verse: that option requires the transmitted evidence to divide, and here it does not divide. What would shift the decision is a Greek manuscript witness for the singular — of which none exists, and none is in prospect.

## What survives the decision

The decision extinguishes the singular as text, not as datum. What survives is what Barrett states: the probability that John, writing the plural, was alluding to Jesus' birth, and declaring that the birth of Christians followed the pattern of the birth of Christ himself. That is the reading the second century sharpened into a text — right about the resonance, wrong about the words.[^survives]

Galot's two internal observations survive with it. The three negations of verse 13 have no evident point, he argues, if believers are their subject; and a verse which says that believers *became* children of God in verse 12 seems to say in verse 13 that they were *already begotten*. Both are received here as genuine exegetical pressure points, and both are answered where the verse is expounded, on the plural text and without emending it (§5.7).

## Three lesser data

Three further items at verse 13 are recorded at their own registers and not elevated.

The spelling ἐγενήθησαν appears in a small group of witnesses, as though the verb came from the root γίνομαι rather than γεννάω. It is a natural mistake for a copyist, since both verbs are used of being born, and it bears on neither the sense nor the number.[^lesser]

The third member of the triple negation, οὐδὲ ἐκ θελήματος ἀνδρός, *nor of the will of a husband*, is omitted by two manuscripts and by many patristic quotations. The tripartite form stands as text on the overwhelming majority.[^lesser]

And Gregory's adaptations of the verse are unstable in a way worth recording. The order of the members is reversed in both adaptations that carry them; a shorter form appears to stand beneath one of them; and the presence of the third member is uncertain. The collation discipline that governs this evidence discounts order and omission as citing habit — and yet leaves that third member's inclusion in doubt, as a possible echo of an attested shorter form rather than mere looseness. It is recorded at that register and no higher.[^lesser]

With the working text fixed — verses 6 through 12 uncontested, and the plural established at verse 13 — the exegesis can turn to what the evangelist has built on it: first the compositional architecture of the unit, and then the six verses in turn under the three beats they compose.
