The exegesis of the unit is complete, the doctrine has been drawn from it, and the scholarship behind both is on the record. What remains is to hear these eight verses as those who will preach and teach them must hear them, and to name where the work they began goes next. Neither task re-exegetes. Both gather, and what they gather already has a shape. The last service this commentary can do for the unit is to hand it over in the form the evangelist gave it.

## The pastoral hearing

The unit is composed, and it preaches as it is composed. It is [one movement in three beats, with one turn and one open door](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/03-articulation-three-figures/), and the teacher can walk a congregation through the same beats the evangelist built: a witness sent, a light coming to its own and refused there, and a reception that God's own begetting makes children of. Every step below is something the text's own words put there, not something the teacher's piety adds.

It begins where the second movement begins, with a man: *There came a man, sent from God; his name was John.* Before the light is shown coming, God sends [a witness to point to it](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/10-witness-sent/). His whole existence in these verses is his commission. His greatness is exactly that he is not the light, and through him people are meant to believe. The teacher can say plainly what the Baptist's office teaches. The light does not arrive unannounced. And the one who points away from himself is doing the greatest thing a human being can do.

Then the movement turns to the light, and to what the world did with it: *He — the Word — was the true light … coming into the world. He was in the world, and the world came to be through him — and yet the world did not know him. He came to what was his own, and his own people did not welcome him.* The refusal is named honestly. It is named three times — once at verse 5, where the darkness did not grasp the light, and twice here, at verses 10 and 11 — and it is not softened. It is not [a stranger unrecognized but a Maker refused](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/12-world-did-not-know/), and then [a rightful owner turned from the door of his own house](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/13-his-own-welcome-refused/) by the household that knew his name. Its guilt is grounded in creation, not in a rule: the world owed its Maker a relation, and refused it whole.

And the teacher is not asked to explain what the text does not. Why should a creature made for this light turn from it? John does not say. Ridderbos leaves the inner contradiction of that not-knowing where the text leaves it: unexplained, and mysterious.[^ridderbos] The preacher may leave it there too. To explain the refusal would be to make it reasonable, and the verses do not make it reasonable.

Then the movement turns again, on a single word: *But as many as received him — to them he gave the right to become children of God.* Here the teacher can give a congregation something exact. What was given was not a feeling. It was not a power to be worked up, and not a privilege inherited. It was [a right](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/19-right-that-carries-effect/) — a standing granted by the one who alone had it to grant, and a right that makes real what it names.

And what the children became, they became by God's own act, and by nothing the world can do: *who were begotten — not of bloods, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of a husband — but of God.* The reception is not a human achievement. The sentence names the receivers first, and then tells them they did not do it. [God's act comes first as the ground](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/20-word-gives-god-begets/). That is a priority of ground, not of time: the begetting is [one completed act, coincident with the reception](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/16-the-units-tenses/), and the verse sets no order between them. How it meets the believing, John does not say, and the teacher need not.

The pastoral note the unit strikes is therefore neither the despair of the refusal nor the pride of the reception. It is the note the evangelist himself strikes by where he stands. He stands [on this side of an advent that has already come](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/21-advent-in-retrospect/). He tells a refusal that is already told, and a begetting that is already done. And he leaves one thing open, which is [the believing](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/07-the-aperture/). That is a word a congregation can be given. The coming is finished. The door is not shut. It stands open *to those who believe in his name*.

## The forward arc

The unit hands its work forward on every register the Prologue runs. What it held with confidence but not yet closed, and what it read under a working judgment that later verses will test, are named here, so that the handoff carries each one by name.

**Into the third movement.** Immediately ahead lies the seam the movement itself makes. After verse 13 the next sentence opens with *and*. It is the first connective since verse 12's *but*, and [it opens something new](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/). The Word, ὁ λόγος, is named again for the first time since verse 1, and the Prologue's third movement begins. That movement is where the following are taken up.

**At verse 14.**
- *The frame.* This movement stands enclosed between verse 4 and verse 14 — the life that was the light of men on one side, the glory beheld on the other. That the two are a pair the evangelist built is held here with confidence, and not yet closed. It awaits the treatment of verse 14, where the expectation is that it will rise. The place of this whole movement within the Prologue rises with it.
- *The working calibration.* This unit's imperfects were read under [the working calibration adopted at verse 9](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/11-true-light-coming/) — the light as always coming. Under it, the right to become God's children was held not to be confined to the Word's arrival in the flesh. That calibration is reassessed where its own question lies, at the Word's becoming flesh, and every reading held under it moves with it. Among the evidence that will press there is Raymond Brown's account of the Spirit's begetting. Brown recalls 7:39 — "For there was as yet no Spirit, since Jesus had not been glorified" — and, reading what John says of the Spirit elsewhere, writes that only the giving of the Spirit begets God's children.[^brown]
- *The one life.* [The one life this commentary has traced](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/21-advent-in-retrospect/) reaches its nearer end there, where the light of verse 4 is disclosed as glory. That end is engaged at verse 14, and not here.
- *Two becomings.* At verse 6 the Prologue applied its verb of becoming, ἐγένετο, to a person for the first time: a man came to be, on mission. At verse 14 it applies the same verb to the Word himself, who *became* flesh. The pairing of the man who came to be on mission with the Word who became flesh is [registered at verse 6](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/10-witness-sent/) and carried forward to verse 14, where its weight belongs.
- *Barth.* Barth's account of what the Prologue's tenses carry goes forward untouched. He hears the *was* of verse 15, like that of verses 9 and 10, as denoting "his historical appearance considered as a whole".[^barth] This commentary engaged that reading here only as evidence on its own question. It is weighed at verses 14 and 18.

**The begetting's mode.** Verse 13 names the source of the begetting and withholds its mode. The Gospel supplies the mode at 3:5–8, born of water and the Spirit, and the exposition of it belongs to the treatment of that chapter.

**The Prologue as a whole.** The third movement has its own present tense at the witness: Ἰωάννης μαρτυρεῖ, *John bears witness* (verse 15). The first movement set its one present at the light's shining (verse 5). This movement sets its present at the believing (verse 12), the one door it leaves open. Laid side by side, the two have looked like two plans, one the inverse of the other. Verse 15's present may show them instead to be three stations of one plan. That is a question for the Prologue read whole. With it go three other observations this commentary has made and not settled:
- the Prologue's word for what has come to be and remains, ὃ γέγονεν, does not appear in this movement, which runs on *was* and *came to be* alone;
- verse 10's three clauses turn on one noun as verse 1's do, and the two verses may be a deliberate pair;
- the movement restates at two of its joints, as the Prologue's opening verses do.

**Across the Gospel.** The turn composed at verses 11 and 12 is realized as the Gospel's own. The public ministry the Prologue summarizes in verse 11 closes on the refusal at 12:37–41. The second half of the Gospel opens at 13:1 with his own named again, and loved to the end (held provisional at the Gospel's register). And the unit's last phrase is answered by the Gospel's last sentence: *that believing you may have life in his name* (20:31).

The three seeds the movement plants grow in the body of the Gospel:
- the witness that opens the narrative (1:19–34; 3:27–30);
- the light that has come into the world, and the judgment that follows its refusal (3:19–21; 12:46);
- the begetting from above (3:3–8).

A reader who has walked these eight verses is equipped to hear each of these when the Gospel reaches it, as the growth of what was planted here.

From here the Prologue climbs to its summit. The light that came to its own, and was refused there and received there, becomes flesh.

> Father, who sent a man to bear witness to the light, that all might believe through him: your Word came to what was his own, the world he made, and the world did not know him, and his own people did not welcome him. You did not leave the refusal as the last word. To as many as received him you gave the right to become your children, begotten not of bloods, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of a husband, but of you. Keep us from the despair that forgets the gift and from the pride that forgets the giver. Make us, like John, glad to point away from ourselves. And since the coming is finished and the door is not shut, give us to believe in his name. Amen.

[^ridderbos]: Herman N. Ridderbos, *The Gospel according to John: A Theological Commentary* (Eerdmans, 1997), p. 44, on verse 10: "The inner contradiction of this not knowing is not explained. The opposition simply posits itself in all its mysteriousness."

[^brown]: Raymond E. Brown, *The Gospel according to John (XIII–XXI)* (Anchor Bible 29A; Doubleday, 1970), p. 711, on 16:7. Brown quotes 7:39 — "For there was as yet no Spirit, since Jesus had not been glorified" — and, a few sentences on, offers a second answer to why the Spirit's coming is for the disciples' good: "Or, if we call upon other passages where John describes the Spirit, only the communication of the Spirit begets men as God's children (3:5, 1:12–13); and in God's plan it is the Spirit that is the principle of life from above."

[^barth]: Karl Barth, *Witness to the Word: A Commentary on John 1*, ed. Walther Fürst, trans. Geoffrey W. Bromiley (Eerdmans, 1986), p. 111, on the "strangely retrospective houtos ēn" of verse 15, "which undoubtedly, like the ēn of v. 9 and v. 10, denotes, not his concrete existence and nature, but his historical appearance considered as a whole." On the verb of becoming at verse 14 he writes, p. 87: "In interpretation of the egeneto here as in vv. 3, 6, 12, one must not put the main accent on the idea of coming into being." He does not discard the distinction between *being* and *becoming*: what he relocates is the accent within the verb, to "the epiphany, the concrete historical existence of the Word in all its breadth" (p. 87).
