The [parenthesis](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/10-witness-sent/) has said what John was not. Verse 9 turns from the lamp to the light, and with a single verb the main line resumes: Ἦν, the imperfect that governed verses 1–5 and that verse 8 has just refused to the Baptist. What follows is the movement's second beat — the true light, coming into the world, illumining every human being.

Four things are fixed on this page, in order: how the verse is construed; what the light's coming is, and how its presence in the world is to be read; what "true" says of the light; and what the illumining of every human being asserts, and how it stands with the fact that most did not see. A fifth follows from them — which way the verse faces.

## The construal — the light is what comes into the world

The Greek admits two readings, and the ambiguity is on the page. Either the present participle ἐρχόμενον goes with τὸ φῶς — *the true light … was coming into the world* — or it goes with πάντα ἄνθρωπον, as the Authorized Version and Luther took it: *the true light, which lighteth every man that cometh into the world*.[^av] This commentary reads the participle with the light, and the verse as an assertion of the light's advent, on five independent grounds that converge.

**The Johannine idiom.** *Coming into the world* is, in McHugh's phrase, "little short of a technical term" in this Gospel — for the light (3:19; 12:46, both with φῶς) and for Jesus (6:14; 9:39; 11:27; 16:28; 18:37). McHugh calls it the decisive argument; for Carson it is the "most convincing support"; Barth grounds the construal on the phrase being constantly a function of the light or of Jesus; and it stands first among Harris's grounds.[^idiom]

**The syntax.** Had the evangelist wanted the participle to describe *every man*, he had his construction ready — πᾶς with the article and a participle, πάντα τὸν ἐρχόμενον, which McHugh calls "one of his favourite constructions." And the rabbinic phrase the attributive reading appeals to, *all who come into the world*, is always plural, where the construction here is singular.[^syntax]

**The verse's own structure.** Its third line develops its first, and its opening Ἦν answers verse 8's οὐκ ἦν — what Holtzmann called a fine contrast.[^structure]

**Subject-continuity.** The subject of Ἦν must be the subject of ἦν in verse 10 and of ἦλθεν in verse 11, and the masculine αὐτόν of verses 11–12 shows that subject to be the Logos. The argument is Bultmann's, made from inside the opposing construal; Schnackenburg and Harris concur in the result.[^subject] The subject of Ἦν is therefore the Word, carried from verses 1–2 across the parenthesis, and τὸ φῶς τὸ ἀληθινόν is predicate: *he was the true light*.

**The sequel.** Verse 10 presupposes a coming accomplished: the one who was coming into the world *was in the world*.

The attributive tradition's one real observation — that *coming into the world* is redundant as an epithet of *every man* — is received and redirected. It shows that the phrase carries assertoric weight, and only the light-reading gives it any. The tradition's antiquity is weighed and not hidden: the early versions, Eusebius, Cyril of Alexandria, Chrysostom, and Luther after them.[^antiquity] What that antiquity shows is that the ambiguity is real, not that the evidence divides. A construal question is settled by the composition's own usage, and the usage runs one way.

Bultmann offers the attributive reading a way out. On his account ἄνθρωπον is "an explanatory gloss (of the translator)": the underlying Semitic phrase, *every one coming into the world*, is itself an idiom for "every man," and the Greek translator supplied the word to explain it.[^gloss] That account is received at its observation and declined at its explanation. The observation stands — the idiom does lack the word. But this commentary reads the Gospel as composed, and on Bultmann's own account the word belongs to that composition. To excise it as a clue to the interpretation is, in Schnackenburg's word, "arbitrary."[^arbitrary] And once the word is kept, the rabbinic parallel no longer matches the verse, for, as McHugh says, "the Aramaic phrase cited contains no equivalent for ἄνθρωπον."[^equivalent]

The grammatical articulation is deliberately loose. The construal does not rest on reading ἦν … ἐρχόμενον as a strict periphrastic, *was coming*. McHugh counts nine instances of the imperfect with a participle in John, and finds that they "seem on the whole to put a distance, rather than a close link, between the two words" — and here a relative clause intervenes as well.[^distance] Schnackenburg adds that the periphrastic imperfect elsewhere marks a custom or a state, not the occurrence of a new event.[^aspect] Both objections are received as controlling. The verse says two things in sequence. The Word *was* the true light: the predication, on the same time-embracing ἦν that carries the Prologue's opening verses. And that light was *coming into the world*: the advent, the participle carrying the movement. Absorbed with it is the modal reading Harris adopts with Beasley-Murray, that the illumining of every person happens "by his coming into the world."[^modal] The relative clause and the participle are one action described from two sides.

The construal is held with very high confidence.

## The light's coming — presence as advent

What the advent-participle asserts is the light's *posture*: its way of being in the world is coming.

The light's being is constant and complete from verse 1. Nothing accrues to it; the ἦν is the same time-embracing imperfect as at verses 4 and 8, as Barth observes, and the eternal-state predication of the Prologue's spine is untouched.[^constant] What moves is the light's approach to human *perception*.

That advent has a salvation-historical body. The one uncreated light is shared with humanity again and again — in creation's light-giving order, in the garden, at the exodus, in prophecy. The creational reach is real and is held where the [light of men](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/07-anthropology-light-of-men/) holds it, as background; it is never the created luminary of Genesis 1. Westcott describes the Word "advancing towards the Incarnation by preparatory revelations," and Abbott the Light "continually coming" to all mankind before it definitely came in the Incarnation.[^durative]

And the advent has a terminus, which is not verse 14 alone. Verse 14 names the *modality*. In Barth's sentence, "The paradox of the statement in v. 14 is not that he came into the world, for this was said already in v. 9, but that he came in this way, in the flesh"; and in Ridderbos's, "this modality of its coming is not mentioned until vs. 14."[^modality] But the movement toward perception completes at the hour: the lifted-up Son drawing all to himself (12:32), and the confession of the Gospel's most demanding doubter — "My Lord and my God" — closing at 20:28 the arc that 1:1 and 1:18 open. The beatitude of 20:29 then establishes that sight is the arc's climax and not its requirement. The terminus is perceptual and confessional, and economic; it is never ontological. What the resurrection completes is not the light, but its being *seen*.

Schnackenburg's sentence is the nearest parallel to this reading in the major commentaries, and it is adopted: the incarnate Logos's illumination is "active since creation and always remaining (present) active," and "has been bestowed in a special manner since the Incarnation."[^schnackenburg-arc]

Read this way, the verb-chain of verses 9–11 runs as one composed movement without strain. Ἐρχόμενον is the advent-posture. Ἐν τῷ κόσμῳ ἦν is presence, and real presence — "The being of this light in the world is its coming," as Barth puts it.[^being] Ἦλθεν is the historical arrival at his own.

The two rival readings of the participle are absorbed rather than refuted. The reading of ἐρχόμενον as an imminent future — *was just about to come* — names the advent's incarnational edge truly: McHugh's light entering the world "at a particular moment in time … presumably in a[n] entirely new sense."[^edge] The durative reading names the advent's salvation-historical body truly. Each errs only when it is made the whole.

This is the commentary's own synthesis, built on components each of which is attested: Barth's simultaneity; Westcott's and Abbott's durative content; Schnackenburg's arc from creation to the Incarnation; Ridderbos's coming that brings every person within reach; Köstenberger's arrival-sequence, in which the light that was coming into the world is then in it.[^components] It is the working calibration of the whole unit, and the exegesis of the verses that follow invokes it by name. It is held with high confidence, and not very high, because no single source states the whole. Its reassessment belongs to the treatment of 1:14, where the modality it reaches toward is adjudicated.

## "True" — the original light, and not a copy

The double article, τὸ φῶς τὸ ἀληθινόν, marks the light *par excellence*. It is the emphasis-pattern of the true bread at 6:32 and the true vine at 15:1, and the sense is fixed where the lexical evidence converges from every direction.

Calvin defines it: "*Veram ergo lucem dixit, cui natura proprium est lucere*" — he calls him the true light whose own nature it is to shine, with no other source of its brightness anywhere.[^calvin-true] Barth: "the original, uncreated, primary light, the direct and immediate revelation of life that bears witness to itself." Schnackenburg: "a transcendent power of illumination, which comes from his godhead." Harris: "genuine, authentic, ultimate, as opposed to provisional or imperfect, rather than to false."[^true] McHugh reads the adjective as "a literary marker, identifying that which will, in the end, prove the only real and genuine object of our knowledge or desiring"; Bultmann, as the authentic light, and so the divine.[^marker] The Septuagint supplies the word's freight, ἀληθινός being freely predicated there of the God who is true to his word.

The adjective's placement, in the parenthesis's wake, is deliberate. Verse 9 resumes the main line past a man who has just been denied the light's identity. But the sense is not aimed at John. The Baptist is the occasion, not the target, and three of these same voices close that door — two by different routes. Barth denies that there is any foil at all: "There can be no question here of opposition to a false or imitation light, for John the Baptist is not regarded as such." Bultmann grants that the contrast can be seen in the Baptist, "But it would be wrong to restrict it to him." And Schnackenburg widens it to the whole field — the true light "in contrast to all other ostensible bringers of light."[^target]

The exclusivity that Brown, Köstenberger and Keener rightly hear is preserved as the archetypal sense's *entailment*.[^exclusivity] The one original light has no rival, and every other light, John included, shines derivatively — a lamp, and not the light (5:35).

The sense is settled at that broader register, while the historically specific claimants to the title are named and given their full weight. The first is the Torah: the light of the law "given for the enlightenment of every man," as the *Testament of Levi* has it.[^tlevi] The second is Wisdom, in the Jewish tradition of light. "Any reader of the Old Testament would know that the law and Wisdom give light … but John's point is that the Word who came into the world is the light, the true light, the genuine and ultimate self-disclosure of God to man."[^carson-true]

The sense itself is held with very high confidence. That the Baptist is the composed occasion and not the target is held with high confidence.

## The illumining of every human being

The verb is stronger than verse 5's. Φωτίζειν is more than φαίνειν. Verse 5's verb names the shining; this one names what the shining does to a person. Barth draws the distinction, and it is adopted here against Bultmann's flattening of the two: "Not just an activity but an effect of light is meant here. It illumines men, it fills them with light, it sets them in the light."[^effect] Bultmann's own lexical note supplies the bridge on which the resolution stands. To enlighten is "to give the possibility of sight."[^possibility]

The light's illumining of a person is therefore *actual* wherever the advent brings encounter. Carson's guard is absorbed, not evaded: the text "speaks not of the Word serving as (potential) light for every man, but of giving light to every man," and on the reading adopted here the giving is real.[^carson-guard] What is given in the encounter is the possibility of sight, not sight coerced.

The dividing face of the light is received as the encounter's *consequence*, not as the verb's sense. Barrett reads the light shining on every man "for judgement"; Carson, the light that divides the race; Bultmann, the paradox of a light that leaves unbelief blind, drawn out at 9:39–41.[^dividing] That division is what verses 10–13 narrate and 3:19–21 unfolds. What the verb intends is salutary enlightenment, light given *for* walking in the light.[^salutary]

The creational endowment is held where the light of men holds the creational reach — real, background, and not this verse's assertion. Here belong Calvin's common light of nature, Cyril's seed of wisdom engrafted in each creature, and Morris's appeal to Romans 1:20.[^endowment]

### Every human being — offered, and sufficient for each

The universal is settled in a particular mode: *offered to each, and sufficient for each*. By its coming the light is for every human being, in Ridderbos's words, "that by which alone he or she can live," and every person has come "within reach of the light."[^ridderbos-reach] Köstenberger makes the purpose-and-outcome distinction in his own terms: giving light "to every person" was "the purpose of Jesus's coming though sadly not the actual outcome due to many people's unbelief."[^purpose] *Without distinction* is entailed: no class boundary conditions the reach. *Accomplished in each* is declined, on the text's own sequel.

Barth reads πάντα restrictively, on the analogy of verse 7's πάντες: *every man* means every man who is illumined at all.[^restrictive] That reading is engaged against, at full force, on three grounds. It would hollow the universal scope of the [light of men](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/07-anthropology-light-of-men/) from outside that reading's own governance, since this clause is that scope's corroborating distributive. It runs against the parenthesis's own universal purpose-clause, *that all might believe*. And the commentators answer it on the merits: the sun, in Köstenberger's analogy, "does not selectively give light only to certain persons and exclude others"; and Calvin stands by the literal sense of *all*.[^answer]

Yet Barth's restriction carries a truth the resolution keeps, and it is admitted in the same breath: *source-exclusivity*. "Everyone who receives light receives it from this source. Obviously also the man mentioned in v. 6!" Barth quotes Bengel's dictum to the same end — *Quisquis illuminatur, ab hac luce illuminatur*, whoever is enlightened at all is enlightened by this light — and Augustine had put the idea plainly: "John does not enlighten every person; Christ does."[^source] That converges with Ridderbos's "by which alone" and with Bultmann's Revealer — "he and only he is the Revealer for all men."[^revealer] The universal reach and the exclusive source are one claim seen from its two ends.

### The light that most did not see

Here the resolution meets its hardest question, and it answers the question here rather than leaving it to the verses of reception. How does a light that illumines *every* human being stand with the fact that most did not see?

Perception, not provision, is the contested register. Nothing in refusal shows that the light failed to reach; the sun-analogy excludes that failure. What refusal refuses is perception. Chrysostom's account of the mechanics is the core of the answer: "if some, willfully closing the eyes of their mind, would not receive the rays of that light, their darkness arises not from the nature of the light but from their own wickedness as they willfully deprive themselves of the gift. For the grace is shed forth upon all, turning its back on no one …"[^chrysostom] Calvin's light of reason, clouded by sin, is received at the creational register; the endowment that sin obscures is not the illumination this verse gives.

And under the reading of the light's coming set out above, the account gains its arc. Since the advent is the light's movement toward perception, the possibility of sight is given in every encounter and pressed with increasing fullness through the advent's whole body — until the hour, when the Gospel stages perception's completion in its own hardest case: the doubter who is shown, and confesses. The narrative thereby demonstrates both halves at once. Refusal was never the light's deficiency, since Thomas's demand is met by the same light that shone all along. And sight is not thereby made the requirement, since the blessing and the book are for those who believe without seeing (20:29–31).

The text's own guard against a universalist misreading of the Prologue's universal statements sits downstream, at verse 12, where Köstenberger places it.[^guard] The anatomy of refusal — what *did not know* and *did not receive* are — is the next two verses' work, done on the account fixed here.

The illumination is held with high confidence; the universal scope it carries forward, with very high confidence.

## Which way the verse faces

Verse 9 attaches to what follows. It opens the movement of the world and its reception, and its backward face is subordinate, though it works.

The forward direction is carried across traditions that otherwise divide, and in part against interest. McHugh's own sectioning opens a new unit at verse 9, and he says why: "it is more logical to place it in this section than in the preceding one."[^mchugh-section] In Ridderbos's description, verses 9–13 are a second concentric cycle, returning the opening theme in its kerygmatic implications.[^cycle] Köstenberger has verse 9 moving "on from John's witness to the light to the light itself, resuming poetic cadence."[^cadence] And Bultmann, no friend of the composed reading, has the evangelist returning to his source at verse 9, with verses 9–11 one description of the revelation-event.[^bultmann-return]

The subordinate face keeps two real functions. Ἦν answers verse 8's οὐκ ἦν, as one of the construal's own grounds. And the adjective's placement is the composed occasion: the verse turns from the lamp to the light in the very act of opening the light's own drama. Barth gives that backward face its theological ground. The coming is not an appendix to the witness but its ground — "Because the primary light is coming into the world there is a Baptist, a martyria."[^barth-ground] Brown's grouping of verses 6–9 is thereby answered rather than dismissed.[^brown] What that grouping honours is carried in the subordinate face, while the parenthesis's extent stands where the [treatment of verses 6–8](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/10-witness-sent/) set it.

The hinge is held with very high confidence, on the Prologue as it stands, and it is the seam at which the [movement's articulation](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/03-articulation-three-figures/) is seated.

The verse thus lands as one teachable claim. The Word was the true light — the original light of God, of which every other light is a lamp — and that light's way of being in the world is coming. Shared with humanity again and again from creation onward, and arriving in the flesh, it shines on every human being without exception, giving each the possibility of sight. Where it is not seen, the eyes have failed and not the light. And its coming did not rest until the hardest doubter looked at the risen Lord and said, "My Lord and my God." It is the seed of the Gospel's "I have come as light into the world" (12:46), and it sets up what the movement now narrates: the light that illumines every human being came into a world that did not know it.
