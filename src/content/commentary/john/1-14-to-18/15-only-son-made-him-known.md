The Prologue ends on a sentence without a connective: Θεὸν οὐδεὶς ἑώρακεν πώποτε· μονογενὴς θεὸς ὁ ὢν εἰς τὸν κόλπον τοῦ πατρὸς ἐκεῖνος ἐξηγήσατο, *No one has ever seen God. The only Son, himself God, who is in the bosom of the Father — he has made him known.* It sets a denial against a declaration and lets them stand side by side. The text is the one [the commentary on the text](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/01-the-text/) settles, μονογενὴς θεός without the article, and the critical edition's punctuation is left standing. That page decides the reading. This one asks what the reading says.

Read in summary, the verse says this. No one, Moses included, has ever seen God as he is in himself. The one who has made him known is the only Son, who is himself God and who is, without temporal restriction, in the Father's bosom. He is not an exception to the denial, one who saw what others could not. He is one who does not stand among the "anyone" at all. His being is the ground of his telling, and he alone has told.

Six things are fixed on the verse: the denial; the construal of μονογενὴς θεός; the participle and its preposition, ὁ ὢν εἰς τὸν κόλπον; the bosom of the Father; ἐκεῖνος ἐξηγήσατο; and how the declaration answers the denial.

## Θεὸν οὐδεὶς ἑώρακεν πώποτε — the denial

No one, ever, has seen God in his being. This is held with very high confidence for its sense and its scope, and with high confidence for its occasion and for what it says of the theophanies.

**God as he is in himself.** The anarthrous θεόν names God as he is in himself, not an indefinite "a god". Westcott: "It is impossible, so far as our experience yet goes, for man to have direct knowledge of God as God." Barth: "The lack of an article before theos shows that as in v. 1c and v. 18b we are dealing with the essence of God." Harris, in his grammar of John: "God as he is in himself, God in his being (anar. θεός) has never been seen by either the physical or the spiritual eye of humans."[^denial]

**Without exception, and for all time.** The perfect with πώποτε states a fact without exception. Barth draws it from "the express oudeis pōpote (no one at any time!)", and finds that the perfect "has here present or supratemporal significance, so that it is not part of a narrative but is presenting a universal fact". The seeing denied is not the eye's alone. Bultmann: ὁρᾶν "must not be restricted simply to visual perception". Barth: "In John this cannot be limited to physical seeing, although we have to start out with this basic sense." The perfect is one of the two places where the unit's narrated time [breaks off](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/04-temporal-payload/).[^perfect]

**Its occasion: not even Moses.** Moses at Sinai is the denial's occasioning instance, carried over from [verse 17](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/14-law-grace-and-truth/). Lincoln reads the clause as one that "continues the contrast of the previous verse, denying any claims that might be made for Moses". Brown: "Naturally it is the failure of Moses to have seen God that the author wishes to contrast with the intimate contact between Son and Father." Keener recalls that Moses "could not see all God's glory because God declared that no one could see his face and live" (Exodus 33:20), and "John declares first the sense in which that affirmation remains true".[^occasion]

Moses is the denial's highest case, not its only target. Barth: "Not even those who are prophets! Not even the man called Moses!" Bultmann reads the denial as reaching against any striving to gain or possess "knowledge of God outside the revelation given in the incarnate Son of God". What Moses was denied is the sight of God's face, not the law's standing as God's gift. Barth: "In the negative statement of v. 18 we are no more to seek a disqualification than in v. 17a." The comparison is between the one who did not see and the one who makes known, not between law and grace. Lincoln's contrast is read in that form here.[^target]

**The theophanies.** What the patriarchs and prophets saw was not God in his being, but a form, a manifestation, the glory. Westcott: "The Theophanies under the Old Dispensation did not fall under this category." Morris: "The theophanies of the Old Testament did not and could not reveal God's essential being." Harris, in his study of the deity of Christ: "no Jew would have denied that on occasion, through self-disclosure, God permitted himself to be seen in some “form,” that is, indirectly or partially." The Gospel bears this out in its own words: "Isaiah said these things because he saw Jesus' glory and spoke about Him" (12:41).[^theophanies]

Two readings of the theophanies are recorded and not adopted:
- **The Word as the one seen.** This is the historically specific form the reading took in the church's early centuries, and the tradition is divided on it. Origen suggests, with a "perhaps", that the saints before Christ's coming were recorded as seeing God "because they saw the image of the invisible God". Eusebius has them see the Word of God. Augustine's judgment falls on those who say the Son was seen before the incarnation. Of them he writes, "they are greatly deluded and making a horrible mistake", and of Moses, "Moses saw a cloud, an angel, a fire."[^wordseen]
- **Calvin's comparison.** Calvin reads the evangelist as keeping to the contrast between the fathers and ourselves: "I rather think that the Evangelist here abides by the comparison already stated, namely, how much better our condition is than that of the fathers". Barth thinks such a reading relativizes the statement.[^calvin54]

## Μονογενὴς θεός — the only Son, himself God

μονογενής is substantival, "the only Son", in the sense it has [at verse 14](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/11-glory-beheld/). θεός stands in apposition to it, so that the Son is God in his being, as the Word is [at 1:1c](/commentary/john/1-1-to-3/08-theos-en-ho-logos/). ὁ ὢν εἰς τὸν κόλπον τοῦ πατρός is a further apposition to the same subject. The subject is definite, and ἐκεῖνος resumes it. This is held with high confidence, provisionally.
- **Harris** draws the apposition from the substantival reading. If μονογενής is equivalent to the only Son here, "the corollary is that θεός stands in epexegetic apposition to μονογενής". And: "The anarthrous θεός is not indefinite. Since ἐκεῖνος in John 1:18 is specific, its antecedent μονογενής, further defined as θεὸς ὁ ὢν κτλ., must be definite."
- **Carson:** "probably the right reading is monogenēs theos, ‘the unique and beloved one, [himself] God’—taking ‘God’ appositionally."
- **Barth:** "The phrases that follow, theos and ho ōn eis ton kolpon tou patros, define the main term monogenēs more closely."
- **Schnackenburg**, on the condition that θεός is read: "θεός is probably in apposition to (ὁ) μονογενής: the only-begotten, whose being is divine". He judges the reading itself undecided, and on the same page leans both ways.
- **Houghton**, the textual commentary, heads its entry on the verse "the only Son, himself God". In its own discussion it glosses the reading "only-begotten God", or "God the only Son".[^construal]

**The grounds.**
1. **The article.** In John an attributive μονογενής qualifies an articular noun. Harris: the anarthrous text "renders difficult (although not, of course, impossible)" the translations "the only begotten God" or "the unique God", "for elsewhere in the Johannine corpus when μονογενής is an attributive adjective (viz., John 3:16, 18; 1 John 4:9), the noun it qualifies is articular". So the text without the article leans to the substantive.[^article]
2. **Verse 14.** At verse 14 μονογενής has just stood without a noun and carried sonship by itself. Houghton gives "God the only Son" as a rendering, "because μονογενής could itself convey the notion of sonship, as at John 1:14 and Heb. 11:17".[^v14]
3. **The following clause.** Bultmann's ground against θεός is that it "neither fits in with the preceding θεὸν κτλ., nor can it take the appositional phrase ὁ ὢν κτλ. The latter must have υἱός". Ridderbos follows him by name. Barrett finds that υἱός "seems to be required by the following clause", while allowing that this "may simply make it lectio facilior". The reading is decided on the text page. What concerns the construal is the second limb. If μονογενής is substantival, ὁ ὤν stands in apposition to it, not to θεός, and the clause does not require υἱός. Harris grants that "υἱός better suits what follows". He notes that John did not write εἰς τὸν κόλπον τοῦ θεοῦ, which "might have made a preceding μονογενὴς θεός virtually impossible". Against the conjecture that the Greek mistranslates an Aramaic original, he holds that the phrase is not impossible, "particularly if μονογενής is substantival". Applying the substantival reading to Bultmann's second limb is this commentary's own argument.[^bultmann]

**One subject, three designations.** The three designations are one subject's. Harris: "The antecedent of ἐκεῖνος cannot be restricted to μονογενής or θεός or ὁ ὢν κτλ.", and ἐκεῖνος "catches up all three designations of the Logos". What that insight is absorbed into is a single subject. A reading that would make ὁ ὤν a third, absolute title is not taken: "it is certainly awkward to have an absolute title of Deity (ὁ ὤν) qualified by a prepositional phrase."[^titles]

**The adjectival reading is declined.** The article datum makes "the only begotten God" or "the unique God" difficult, though not impossible. Barth states the theological objection: "We do not read ho monogenēs theos, as though one of the gods, or, nonsensically, the one God were called monogenēs." Köstenberger: "“Only begotten God” would be nonsensical; but even “unique God” is at first hard to fathom." He offers the appositional reading in its place, with a "perhaps".[^adjectival]

**The punctuation.** The critical edition prints no comma within the second clause, and its text stands unpunctuated. The construal is carried by the rendering and by this page. Barth objects: "Nestle's omission of the comma is misleading here." His own text of the verse sets a comma after monogenēs.[^comma]

**Why provisional.** The first ground leans on the reading without the article, which is itself held provisionally. Two things would weaken it and reopen the adjectival reading: a better case for ὁ μονογενὴς θεός, with the article, or evidence of an attributive μονογενής with an anarthrous noun in John's idiom.

**Not the manner of birth.** On this construal the phrase names the Son as God and as the Father's only one, not his mode of origin. Harris: "As far as the evidence of the NT is concerned, it may be safely said that μονογενής is concerned with familial relations, not manner of birth." He goes on: "Neither the virgin birth of Jesus nor the “eternal generation” of the Son is in John's mind when he uses the adjective μονογενής." Robertson presses origin into the phrase. For him it is "God only begotten", "the Eternal Generation of the Son" of Origen's phrase. That is declined with the adjectival reading. The church's doctrine of eternal generation is engaged as the historically specific articulation of the Son's eternal relation to the Father, as [the commentary on verse 14](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/11-glory-beheld/) placed it, and it is not denied.[^origin]

## Ὁ ὢν εἰς τὸν κόλπον — in the bosom, without temporal restriction

The Son is, without temporal restriction, in the bosom of the Father, and that includes his incarnate life. This is held with high confidence.

**The preposition.** εἰς stands for ἐν after the participle of being: "in the bosom". Barrett: εἰς "is used for ἐν, a Hellenistic usage". Wallace states the principle: "Stative verbs override the transitive force of prepositions. Almost always, when a stative verb is used with a transitive preposition, the preposition's natural force is neutralized". Schnackenburg and Bultmann agree on the equivalence, on grounds of their own. Schnackenburg: "As often in the Koine, εἰς with acc. is certainly the equivalent of ἐν with dat." Bultmann: "Εἰς stands for ἐν (like πρός with acc. in v. 1 for παρά with dat.)", citing the grammars.[^eis]

**The relation is carried by the nouns.** What the phrase names, the Son's intimate communion with the Father, is carried by κόλπος and πατήρ, not by the preposition. Harris: "Any notion of dynamic interpersonal relationship found in verse 18 stems from the nouns κόλπος and πατήρ, not from the preposition εἰς." Wallace: "this text affirms only their intimate relationship".[^nouns]

The dynamic reading of εἰς is held in different forms:
- Zerwick and Grosvenor: "εἰς perh. for ἐν but rather (according to Johannine usage) dynamic".
- Westcott: "The phrase is not strictly “in the bosom,” but “into the bosom”".
- McHugh finds the distinction between εἰς and ἐν alive in John, and "particularly significant in this text".
- Abbott's datum is its strongest case: "so far as is alleged, εἰς κόλπον without a verb of motion occurs nowhere in the Bible, nor in Greek literature". He infers "a spiritual meaning".

It is declined on the stative verb.[^dynamic]

**The participle.** ὁ ὤν is the present of characterization: it says what the Son is, not when.
- Harris reports, as a related interpretation, the reading "indicating the eternal presence proper to the Son". The words are Zerwick and Grosvenor's, whose own sentence is hedged: "perh. atemporal indicating the eternal presence proper to the Son". In his grammar of John, Harris sets that sense beside a second, the Son's permanent presence with the Father after the exaltation, and chooses neither.
- Westcott allows that the words "may point to the exaltation of the ascended Christ; but in connexion with “God the only Son” (μονογ. θεός) it is more natural to take them as an absolute description of the nature of the Son, so that the participle will be timeless".
- Barth: "Having become totally flesh on earth, he is totally with God in heaven." He says it of the Son "Naturally not as sarx … but as Logos", and grounds it in verse 14: "his Incarnation means no limitation of his being as Logos."
- Bavinck: "even though walking about on earth, he still continues to be “in the bosom of the Father” (John 1:18)".
- Harris finds the Son's "uninterrupted fellowship with the Father while on earth" in keeping with Johannine thought.[^participle]

That the incarnate life is included is not read off the participle alone. Harris: "In itself ὁ ὤν need say nothing about the relationship of the Son to the Father during Christ's earthly ministry." It is read from the Prologue's whole account of the Word made flesh. Calvin argues at two points. The Son did not first enter the Father's bosom at the incarnation: "For who will dare to affirm that the Son entered his Father's bosom for the first time, when he came down from heaven to assume human nature?" And, by the communion of properties, "he who, according to the flesh, dwelt as the Son of man on earth, was also God in heaven". This is the reading the commentary on the Prologue's opening verses received at the frame's closing pole, and it is received unchanged.[^incarnate]

**Not simultaneity.** Schnackenburg holds the union too. The Son "remains most intimately united to his Father, even in his earthly life", and the formula of verse 18 "comprises both aspects". What he rejects is a further reading: "It is improbable, however, that the meaning is: while the only-begotten still remained in the bosom of the Father, he also brought us revelation as the incarnate Son." John presents the descent and the return as successive acts. Harris draws the same line. The simultaneous presence of Jesus in heaven and on earth "seems likely only if ὁ ὢν ἐν τῷ οὐρανῷ be the preferred reading in John 3:13—which is improbable". This commentary does not claim that the participle states simultaneity as the mode of the revelation. It reads the participle as characterization.[^simultaneity]

**The return, declined.** The reading that ὁ ὤν is said of the Revealer returned to the Father is Bultmann's, at "more probable": "It is more probable, however, that ὤν is to be taken as a true present, and that it is therefore said of the Revealer who has returned to the Father." It is declined on two grounds. Harris: "Attractive though this view is, it blurs the logical connection between ὁ ὤν and ἐξηγήσατο." Michaels: "there is no “now” in the sentence". McHugh holds the return reading himself, and records the patristic objection against it: "One serious objection to this interpretation is that it is nowhere found in the Greek Fathers." He bounds it ("At this point Origen is not extant"), reports Chrysostom and Cyril reading the εἰς as ἐν, and answers it: "But if the evangelist in his day used εἰς and ἐν with different meanings, then subsequent writers, by not adverting to this, would have misinterpreted this text". Gregory of Nyssa, read at first hand, writes the phrase in the form ὁ ἐν τοῖς κόλποις ὢν τοῦ πατρός. That is recorded as his form, not as his construal of εἰς. A secondary allusion to the return is allowed, as Harris allows it: such an allusion "need not be excluded".[^return]

**The Nicene reception.** Gregory of Nyssa against Eunomius refuses the argument that being in the Father's bosom limits the Son's being. If the only-begotten God is not, in the proper sense, because he is in the Father's bosom, then everything that is in anything is stripped of being. In his refutation of Eunomius's confession he writes of the only-begotten God who is in the bosom of the Father that he is in the bosom, ὢν ἐν τοῖς κόλποις, not having come to be in it, οὐχὶ ἐγγενόμενος. The ground he stands on is proper being, not time.[^gregory]

## Τὸν κόλπον τοῦ πατρός — intimacy, the ground of the making known

The phrase names the exclusive intimacy of love and knowledge between the Son and the Father, and that intimacy is the ground of his making the Father known. The image is not pressed. This is held with high confidence.
- **Harris:** "It denotes the exclusive and privileged intimacy of a deeply affectionate interpersonal relationship."
- **Carson:** "It apparently conveys an aura of intimacy, mutual love and knowledge."
- **The images.** Keener suggests "the affectionate image may be that of a son on his father's lap". Ridderbos sets that image aside: "It is not that of a child seated on his father's lap but that of two adults reclining next to each other at the same table, able to converse intimately".
- **The ground.** Harris: the clause "introduces, not the confirmation of the ἐξήγησις, but its ground".[^bosom]

**13:23.** The beloved disciple ἐν τῷ κόλπῳ τοῦ Ἰησοῦ is a probable echo of verse 18 at the level of the image. It bears on the disciple's witness, and not on the bracket of verses 1 and 18.
- Köstenberger draws it so: "just as Jesus was in close proximity to God the Father and thus uniquely qualified to reveal him, so the beloved disciple was uniquely qualified to reveal Jesus due to his close proximity to him."
- Lincoln: "so this disciple's intimate relationship with Jesus throughout the climactic events of his ministry prepares him to be the witness who makes known the true significance of Jesus".
- Brown draws it to intimacy: "the Disciple is as intimate with Jesus as Jesus is with the Father."
- Morris allows the echo, "It may be", and limits it. Ridderbos finds the allusion "certainly possible, but it would certainly be very indirect".[^1323]

**Verse 1.** The κόλπος of verse 18 says again, in another way, what [πρὸς τὸν θεόν](/commentary/john/1-1-to-3/07-pros-ton-theon/) said at verse 1: the same relation, named now as the Son's to the Father. Schnackenburg: "“In the bosom of the Father” is a metaphorical expression of the evangelist (cf. 13:23), which renders the “with God” of v. 1 in another way." Dodd sets the two phrases side by side: "The Logos was with God, εἰς τὸν κόλπον τοῦ πατρός." Barth reads them together at the level of sameness and personal intimacy. The Son is with the Father "as the pros ton theon of vv. 1 and 2 expresses—that he is at home with him, that he knows the Father as a child knows its father, that he lies on his bosom". He finds the thought of turning to in neither preposition.[^v1]

## Ἐκεῖνος ἐξηγήσατο — he, and he alone, has made God known

ἐκεῖνος resumes the whole subject with emphasis, he and no other, and ἐξηγήσατο says that he has made God known. This is held with high confidence.

**Ἐκεῖνος.** Barrett: "ἐκεῖνος resumes the subject in a characteristically Johannine manner." Brown: "This phrase is set off by itself in casus pendens and then resumed in the last clause of vs. 18 by ekeinos (“that one”)". It answers the οὗτος of verse 2, *this one was in the beginning with God*, with which the Prologue first resumed the Word.[^ekeinos]

**Ἐξηγήσατο.** As a word, it is to tell or recount. Michaels: it "can be a rather straightforward, even colorless, term for reporting something or telling a story", and "Here it is almost certainly the former." Carson: "we might almost say that Jesus is the exegesis of God." Morris: "It indicates that Jesus has now given a full account of the Father", and he adds at once: "This does not mean that there is nothing more to be learned of him." Others find the revelatory sense of the Greek religious world in the word itself. Barrett: "It is this usage that appears in John". Harris, more cautiously, takes the verb as "He has revealed [him]".[^exegesato]

What the verb says here, the making known of God himself, whom no one has seen, is carried by the sentence. The object is implied from the denial. The subject is the one who is God and in the Father's bosom. And the telling answers the question of Sirach 43:31. Carson: "Ben Sirach could ask who could describe (ekdiēgēsetai) God (Ecclus. 43:31); John declares that the incarnate Word made him known." The word does not carry what the sentence carries. Evidence of a technical revelatory sense of ἐξηγέομαι in the Jewish or Christian usage of the time would move the sense into the word, without moving the force the sentence carries.[^sentence]

**Revealing God by revealing himself.** Barth: "He who reveals stands over against him whom he reveals in such a way that to reveal him he has only to reveal himself."[^reveal]

**"Led the way", declined.** The reading of the verb as "has led the way" into the bosom of the Father is declined. Schnackenburg: "it is impossible to explain the aorist ἐξηγήσατο satisfactorily (contrast the perfect ἀναβέβηκεν in 3:13)". Brown notes that the verb "is not attested in this meaning in the NT or in early Christian literature", and that "it is precisely in the Lucan writings that the verb does not mean “to lead.”"[^led]

**The object.** The unexpressed object is God, from the denial, known in the declaration as the Father. Harris: it "may be (τὸν) θεόν from verse 18a or τὸν πατέρα from verse 18b. Little hangs on the choice". And: "It was the essential fatherhood of God that the Son disclosed."[^object]

**The aorist.** It is constative. Harris: "Ἐξηγήσατο is a constative aorist, encompassing in a single glance the whole span of Christ's earthly life together with his death and resurrection." With the perfect and the participle it closes the unit's account of [the incarnate life told in retrospect](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/04-temporal-payload/).[^aorist]

## No exception, but one who is not among the "anyone"

The first clause denies that anyone has ever seen God. The second does not admit an exception to that denial. It names one who is not among the "anyone": he is θεός, and he is in the bosom of the Father. His three designations are the ground of his competence: the only Son, himself God, the one who is in the Father's bosom. ἐκεῖνος gathers them as the subject of ἐξηγήσατο, so that he, and he alone, has made God known. This is held with high confidence.
- **Barth:** "We are not told that he is the exception, that he sees and knows theon, but more simply and strongly that he is theos, that he is there where none of us can see."
- **Harris:** "It was an only Son (μονογενής) who himself possessed deity (θεός) and therefore both knew the Father and was qualified to make him known."
- **Brown:** "It is the unique relation of the Son to the Father, so unique that John can speak of “God the only Son,” that makes his revelation the supreme revelation."
- **McHugh**, on the missing connective: "As in 18b, any conjunction would have weakened, probably destroyed, the strength of this verse."[^synthesis]

**The other way of reading it.** Michaels reads the clauses as rule and exception. The assertion that Jesus told about God "presupposes that he is the exception to the principle laid down in the first half of the verse". "Because he is himself God, and “right beside the Father,” he (and he alone) has seen God and can therefore “tell about” God". He compares 6:46. Carson notes that John "will go on to add, ‘except the one who is from God’ (6:46)", and grounds the Son's knowing in "this intimacy". On Michaels's reading too the ground is the Son's being, not a vision granted to him. The two readings differ on the word "exception", not on the ground.[^exception]

**The readings that add εἰ μή.** A few witnesses write the exception into the verse. The critical edition reports εἰ μὴ ὁ μονογενὴς υἱός, *except the only Son*, in the supplement of Codex W, in the Old Latin, and in part of the Latin tradition of Irenaeus. Michaels reports the same witnesses. They are evidence of how the verse was received, and are declined at the working text, as [the commentary on the text](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/01-the-text/) records.[^eime]

**The sentence takes up verses 14 and 17.** μονογενής takes up verse 14's μονογενοῦς παρὰ πατρός. The one through whom the grace and the truth came to be is the one who has made God known. Moses, who did not see God's face, could not make him known. Harris: "if verse 17 as well as verse 18a be considered", Christ "had not merely seen God (a privilege denied even Moses); as himself θεός, he had always resided near the Father." Augustine reads the denial as answering an objection: "In case anyone might say, “And did not grace and truth come through Moses who saw God?” immediately he adds, “No one has seen God at any time.”" Ibuki's suggestion that verse 18 stands to verse 17 as a parallel, which Carson reports conditionally, is recorded for the passage's architecture.[^v1417]

**The bound.** The reading of the sentence stays within the verse. How verse 18 completes [the frame of the Prologue](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/05-frame-completed/), and the θεός that stands at its beginning and its end, belong to the architecture. What would move this reading is a reading in which the subject of the declaration has seen God *as one of us*, a vision granted rather than a being possessed. The working text does not write it.

## What the verse hands on

- Its content at the closing pole is received by [the frame's completion](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/05-frame-completed/).
- Its perfect and its participle are the second break in [the unit's narrated time](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/04-temporal-payload/).
- The denial, the Son in the bosom of the Father, and the making known are drawn out in this commentary's account of the theology of God made known.
- With it the Prologue closes. The Word who [was God](/commentary/john/1-1-to-3/08-theos-en-ho-logos/) in the beginning is named God again at the end.

How firmly each is held:
- **The denial:** no one has ever seen God in his being: very high for its sense and scope; high for its occasion, Moses, and for the theophanies.
- **The construal:** the only Son, himself God: high, provisionally, because the first ground leans on the reading without the article.
- **Ὁ ὢν εἰς τὸν κόλπον:** in the bosom, without temporal restriction, the incarnate life included: high.
- **The bosom of the Father:** intimacy, the ground of the making known: high.
- **Ἐκεῖνος ἐξηγήσατο:** he, and he alone, has made God known: high.
- **The synthesis:** no exception, but one who is not among the "anyone": high.
