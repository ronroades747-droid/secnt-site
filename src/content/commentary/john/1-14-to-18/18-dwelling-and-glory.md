The confession's second claim follows from the first. The Word who became flesh dwelt among us, and his glory was beheld. [The clause *and dwelt among us, and we beheld his glory, glory as of the only one from the Father, full of grace and truth*](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/11-glory-beheld/) has been read for what it says: what the verb evokes, who beheld, what the glory is, and whose glory it is. This page does not weigh those words again. It asks what the church, on the strength of them, confesses about the Word's dwelling among us and the glory seen in him.

The claim has four parts, and each is held as firmly as the exegesis held it: God's dwelling with his people; the glory, seen in the flesh; that glory and the light of verse 4; and the only Son whose glory it is.

## God's dwelling with his people

The verb ἐσκήνωσεν evokes God's dwelling among his people as Israel knew it in the tabernacle. It speaks in the idiom in which Sirach sang of Wisdom pitching her tent in Jacob. This is held with high confidence.

**A connotation, not a direct reference.** What the verb carries is a connotation. It is not a direct reference to the tabernacle or the temple, and it is not a continuous typology of the Exodus. The Shekinah, the rabbinic name for God's dwelling presence, is a possible echo and is not asserted. The exegesis weighs each of these, and they are not argued again here.

What the connotation gives the confession is its idiom. The Word's dwelling among us is spoken of in the language of God's own dwelling among his people. The verse does not say more than that, and the confession holds it at that strength.

**The temple — recorded, not built on.** Some read further, and find at this verse the theme that Jesus takes the place of the tabernacle and the temple.
- Lincoln holds that the place of the divine dwelling and presence among humans "is seen to be in Jesus rather than in tabernacle or temple, a fulfilment-and-replacement theme that will be developed in the rest of the narrative".
- Brown hears it at the clause. Jesus, he writes, "is the replacement of the ancient Tabernacle", and "The Gospel will present Jesus as the replacement of the Temple (2:19–22), which is a variation of the same theme."
- Keener holds it with care: John "may emphasize that Jesus, rather than the temple or tabernacle, is the true locus of God’s activity among humanity".
- Ridderbos denies that the verse refers directly to the tabernacle and the temple. In the same sentence he points ahead: "(cf., however, also 2:19–22!)".[^temple]

The theme is recorded here, among those who hold it. It is not fixed at this verse, and the confession does not build on it.

## The glory — God's own presence and character, seen in the flesh

The glory beheld is God's own presence and character, made manifest in the incarnate Word. It is seen in his signs, and conclusively in his death. This is held with high confidence.

Barth says what was seen in the plainest terms: "*Etheasametha tēn doxan autou* thus means unquestionably that we perceived his *deity*. We saw the Word in the flesh, and the Word was God; it was the divine *doxa* that we perceived."[^barth] His ground is the first verse. What the witnesses perceived was divine, because the Word they saw in the flesh was God.

Two things follow, and each is held with high confidence.

**The flesh does not hide the glory.** The flesh is the place where the glory is shown, not a veil to be seen through. Ridderbos says it at this clause, against the reading of the flesh as the glory's hiddenness: "The flesh is the medium of the glory".[^medium] The Word did not appear in the flesh as in a disguise; [that was excluded with the confession's first claim](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/17-the-incarnation/). So the flesh is not what the glory had to overcome in order to be seen. It is where the glory was seen.

**The glory is seen by those given to see.** The hiddenness is a hiddenness to unbelief, and not a property of the flesh. Those who saw Jesus in the flesh perceived the glory by God's gift, through his whole earthly life, in the signs, and conclusively at his death. That the hiddenness lies in unbelief, and not in the flesh, is this commentary's own reading, on the grounds [the exegesis set out](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/11-glory-beheld/).

**Full of grace and truth.** The glory has the content the verse gives it. It is the glory of the only one from the Father, and he is full of grace and truth. The pair renders the covenant love and faithfulness God proclaimed to Moses at Sinai, in Exodus 34:6, now the character of the incarnate Son. This is held with high confidence. The evangelist probably took the second word, truth, as the reality of God revealed. So the glory beheld is not God's presence alone. It is God's character, his covenant love and faithfulness, seen in a human life.

## The glory and the light of verse 4

The glory beheld at verse 14 is the same reality as the life and light of verse 4, *in him was life, and the life was the light of men*. It is the light of the Word in whom was life, now manifest in the flesh to those given to see it. This is held with high confidence, as a claim about what the two verses say.

The confession that follows from it is this. The glory the witnesses beheld is not something other than what verse 4 names. It is the life that was in the Word, [the life that was the light of men](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/07-anthropology-light-of-men/), seen now in a human life.

Whether the evangelist composed the two verses as a pair, so that the Prologue's second movement stands between them, is a question about the Prologue's design. This commentary holds that he did, with high confidence and provisionally, as its own reading of that design, set out [at the Foyer's inner pair](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/07-foyers-inner-pair/). The claim made here does not rest on it.

## The only Son, in a relation that precedes the sending

The glory is the glory of the only one from the Father. Each part of what that names is held at its own confidence.
- **Uniqueness, not begetting.** μονογενής means alone of its kind, or, where a parent is in view, an only child. Begetting is in neither sense. This is held with very high confidence, on [the lexical case set out with the exegesis](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/11-glory-beheld/).
- **The Father's only Son.** With πατρός beside it, the word names the Father's only Son. This is held with high confidence.
- **Beloved, before the sending.** In John's usage it names the beloved Son, in a relation to the Father that precedes his sending. This is held with high confidence. On Keener's reading, the relation is there long before the Son's public glorification, probably before the incarnation.[^keener] Reading the word with verse 18 and 3:16, so that the relation precedes the sending, is this commentary's own reading.

**Eternal generation.** The church's doctrine of the Son's eternal generation is the historically specific articulation of the eternal, unique filial relation the passage asserts. It is the church's articulation of that relation, engaged at full weight and not fixed as the clause's own assertion. This commentary denies nothing of it.
- Bavinck sets out the doctrine's grounds. In Scripture, he writes, the Son "bears several names that denote his relation to the Father", only-begotten among them, and the doctrine of eternal generation "was based on these names and a few texts cited above". Of the relation itself he holds that "he from all eternity sustained an utterly unique relation to the Father".[^bavinck]
- Cyril of Jerusalem confesses the Son "begotten not as a word pronounced but as a Word substantially existing and living".[^cyril]

This commentary places the doctrine's ground in the passage and in the Gospel's testimony, and not in the word μονογενής alone, which names uniqueness. Not everyone who reads the word so draws the doctrine from John. Köstenberger does not call the doctrine unscriptural, and argues "only that John did not conceive of the relationship in those terms".[^kostenberger] The exegesis records that dissent, and it is not answered here.

**The Son's sonship, and the children's.** The Son's sonship is his own; the children's is given. Those who received him were given the right to become children of God, and were [*begotten — not of bloods, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of a husband — but of God*](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/20-word-gives-god-begets/). Their begetting is participation in God's gift, not in his being. The only Son is the Son in his own right. This is held with high confidence.

## What the confession hands on

The confession's second claim, then, is this. The Word who became flesh dwelt among us, spoken of in the idiom of God's own dwelling with his people. His glory was beheld: God's own presence and character, shown in the flesh and not hidden by it, seen by those given to see. It is the light of the Word in whom was life, now manifest. And it is the glory of the only Son, full of grace and truth, whose sonship is his own. How firmly each is held:
- **The dwelling:** God's dwelling with his people, in the tabernacle's and Wisdom's idiom, a connotation and not a direct reference: high. **The Shekinah:** a possible echo, not asserted. **The temple theme:** recorded, not built on.
- **The glory**, God's own presence and character manifest in the flesh: high. **The flesh** as the glory's place, not its veil: high. **The hiddenness** as a hiddenness to unbelief: high, as this commentary's reading.
- **Grace and truth** as God's covenant love and faithfulness: high.
- **The glory and the light of verse 4:** the same reality, as a claim about what the verses say: high. **That the two verses were composed as a pair:** high and provisional, as this commentary's reading.
- **Μονογενής:** uniqueness, not begetting: very high. **The Father's only and beloved Son**, in a relation that precedes the sending: high. **Eternal generation:** the church's articulation, engaged at full weight, not fixed as the clause's own assertion. **The Son's sonship his own, the children's given:** high.

What was received from that fullness, grace in place of grace, is taken up next.
