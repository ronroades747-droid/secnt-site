The Word became flesh, and the verse goes on at once to say what that becoming was: καὶ ἐσκήνωσεν ἐν ἡμῖν, καὶ ἐθεασάμεθα τὴν δόξαν αὐτοῦ, δόξαν ὡς μονογενοῦς παρὰ πατρός, πλήρης χάριτος καὶ ἀληθείας, *and dwelt among us, and we beheld his glory, glory as of the only one from the Father, full of grace and truth*. Everything after σὰρξ ἐγένετο [unfolds the clause](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/10-word-became-flesh/) that opened the verse. The commentators letter the parts of the verse differently, so this page names them by the Greek: the dwelling, καὶ ἐσκήνωσεν ἐν ἡμῖν; the beholding, καὶ ἐθεασάμεθα τὴν δόξαν αὐτοῦ; and the definition of the glory, δόξαν ὡς μονογενοῦς παρὰ πατρός, πλήρης χάριτος καὶ ἀληθείας.

Six questions are fixed on the clauses of this one sentence: the dwelling; the beholding; the glory; how the glory is defined; who is named in the genitive; and the fullness, with the pair of words it governs.

Read in summary, the verse says this. After the Word became flesh, he *dwelt among us*. *We beheld his glory*: those who saw Jesus in the flesh, the evangelist among them, perceived by God's gift the glory, which is God's own presence and character made manifest in the incarnate Word. It is *glory as of the only one from the Father*: the Father's only and beloved Son, unique in kind. And he is *full of grace and truth*: the covenant love and faithfulness God proclaimed to Moses, now the character of the Son whose glory it is.

## Ἐσκήνωσεν — God's dwelling with his people

The verb evokes God's dwelling among his people as Israel knew it in the tabernacle. It speaks in the idiom in which Sirach sang of Wisdom pitching her tent in Jacob and ministering in the sacred tent (Sirach 24:8, 10). This is held with high confidence.

**The tabernacle.** McHugh finds the background in the Hebrew verb and noun for God's dwelling, which first occur together of his indwelling in the midst of Israel in Exodus 25. These, he writes, "explain why John chose to write ἐσκήνωσεν". Ridderbos hears in the verb a reminiscence of the Old Testament's many statements of God dwelling in the midst of Israel, "the fundamental motif being the tabernacle in the wilderness in which God's presence in Israel and his glory were manifested."[^tabernacle]

**Wisdom, held with it.** Schnackenburg hears Wisdom first. The image, he writes, "recalls primarily the Wisdom speculation", where Sirach 24:8 uses the same verb. But he holds that, since the Logos's earthly presence is the vehicle of grace and salvation, "the theme of God's dwelling among the people of Israel must also have been envisaged, and applied here to the Logos."[^wisdom] Dodd sets out the parallels from the Wisdom literature in a table, and its row for this clause is Sirach's tent. Evans sets out the parallels from Sirach 24 in the same way.[^tables] That Sirach 24 itself joins Wisdom and the tent, so that the two backgrounds are one resonance, is this commentary's own reading of the text of Sirach.

**A connotation, not a direct reference.** Ridderbos holds that "there is no direct reference here to the tabernacle and temple as the place of God's dwelling in Israel", though the verb still bears a plain salvation-historical overtone. Michaels finds the tent imagery evoking the Exodus, but "In our text there is no direct reference to the Exodus nor to God's ancient covenant with Israel." Schnackenburg sees separate themes here, not a sustained typology.[^connotation]

**The Shekinah, a possible echo.** The link to the Shekinah, the rabbinic name for God's dwelling presence, is recorded as a possible echo and is not asserted. Dodd allows that the word ἐσκήνωσεν "may echo the Hebrew שכן", from which the word Shekinah was formed. Schnackenburg holds that the late Jewish term "can hardly have influenced Jn 1:14". Carson finds the connection "less than certain". Bultmann allows that "It seems possible" the verb was chosen as a play on the common use in the Orient of the "dwelling" of the Godhead. He reports the rabbinic term in the same note, and judges the rendering it would yield, "He manifested himself among us", unlikely as a continuation of σὰρξ ἐγένετο. Where he takes the link up again, it stays conditional: "If ἐσκήνωσεν is a play on the notion of the שכינה".[^shekinah] None of these writers reads the rabbinic texts in his own voice here, and neither does this commentary.

**Recorded, and not built on.** Two further readings are recorded and are not fixed at this verse. The first is the theme that Jesus replaces the temple. Brown, Lincoln and Keener hold it, and Ridderbos himself points ahead to 2:19–22. It is taken up later in the commentary, at the weight these writers give it and no more. The second is the reading of Köstenberger's commentary, which ties the temple theme to the Gospel's occasion, the aftermath of the temple's destruction. It is recorded as the author's thesis and is not drawn out.[^temple]

## The dwelling's transience — not in the verb; true of the sojourn

The verb does not carry transience, and that judgment is this commentary's own, from the way the verb is used. It is held with high confidence.

Every other use of the verb in the New Testament is in Revelation, and none of them, Michaels notes, has "to do with a temporary dwelling". Westcott grants that the word "describes properly the occupation of a temporary habitation". Yet he finds the verb "used without any reference to the notion of transitoriness", and he leaves open whether the term indicates it. McHugh finds it hard to think that John chose the form to stress how transitory Jesus' life on earth was. Ridderbos hears in it "the act of taking up residence and then staying". Morris grants that the verb "may thus denote a temporary visit", and then adds, "But this cannot be insisted upon".[^notverb]

Two writers find transience in the word itself. Calvin has the Word remain among us "as a guest, for a short time", since "the word which he employs (ἐσκήνωσεν) is taken from *tabernacles*". Barth is "inclined to think" that the clause is deliberately ambivalent. He finds in the term "the transitory aspect" as well as the dwelling, and offers, "Perhaps better than both", the rendering "to lodge".[^inword]

Three others find it not in the verb's sense but elsewhere: in what the statement implies, in what John means, or in an antithesis.
- **Bultmann** finds it implied by the statement. The Word stayed among us as a man, "and he did so—for this is what is implied, even if the emphasis is on the positive content of the statement—as a guest who took his leave again." In the note on that sentence, the verb means "not only to 'live in a tent' but also to 'dwell', in a general sense, and is used especially in the sense of 'taking up quarters'".
- **Barrett** is the nearest of the three to the word. He gives the verb's proper sense as "to live in a tent", and then "to settle", "to take up one’s abode", and says "The latter meaning is to be adopted here". Then: "Probably John means no more than that the Word took up a temporary residence among men."
- **Harris** glosses the verb "live in a tent [σκηνή]," "live," "dwell," "settle". The temporariness comes in his antithesis between the first verse and this one: "The one who was eternally 'in communion with God' (v. 1b) temporarily 'sojourned among us' (v. 14b)."[^elsewhere]

What all five see is received as true of the sojourn the Gospel narrates, and not as the verb's sense. Westcott says it in one line: "His historical Presence was real if transitory."[^westcott] The word does not carry what the passage carries.

## The aspect — constative, unfolding the becoming

ἐσκήνωσεν is best read as constative: *dwelt*, a life lived among them, which the equally constative ἐθεασάμεθα beholds. This is held with high confidence.

Harris: the verb "may be ingressive (“took up residence”) but is more probably constative". Zerwick allows either and judges "The latter favoured by the following ἐ-θεασάμεθα". The single moment of arrival is σὰρξ ἐγένετο's. Harris takes ἐγένετο as "denoting a single occurrence", and ἐσκήνωσεν and ἐθεασάμεθα as "referring to prolonged action".[^aspect] And the dwelling unfolds the becoming. Ridderbos: "The words “and dwelled among us” further explicate the preceding words." Bultmann: "V. 14b explains the miracle of the incarnation of the Logos".[^explicate]

That the ingressive edge some hear in ἐσκήνωσεν belongs to the moment of σὰρξ ἐγένετο is this commentary's own synthesis. Ridderbos leaves the aspect open, between the beginning of the residence and its whole period, and for Bultmann "It makes no difference". Robertson reads the verb as ingressive, and that reading is recorded.[^open] How the tenses of the whole movement bear on the life they tell [is set out with the movement's temporal reference](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/04-temporal-payload/), and that reading confirms the constative here without moving it.

## The beholding — among the eyewitnesses, a given perception, through the ministry

The Word dwelt *among* us, and Barrett renders it so: "Rather, he took up residence in our midst." The "we" are those who saw Jesus in the flesh, the evangelist among them. McHugh reads ἐν ἡμῖν first of "those who personally ‘heard, saw and touched’ Jesus", in the words of 1 John 1:1.[^among] This is held with high confidence.

**A seeing that was also given.** Their seeing was physical sight, and in it the glory was perceived by God's gift. McHugh writes of the verb: "In John, it often means to see one thing with the physical eyes, and to perceive therein, by the gift of God, something else, not self-evident, but profoundly true". Calvin: "It was only a few, whose eyes the Holy Spirit opened, that saw this manifestation of *glory*." Köstenberger distinguishes those who saw only the signs from the believers who saw the glory in them. Schnackenburg has the witnesses apprehend the glory of the Logos "in and in spite of the flesh", "by means of the “signs”". And Westcott argues from the letter to the Gospel. In 1 John 1:1 the "beholding" "must be understood literally", and so "the same word in the same tense and in the same general connexion cannot reasonably be understood otherwise in the Gospel."[^given]

**The time.** The beholding spans the whole earthly life, and conclusively the death. Dodd gives the whole ministry the character of the third day, the day of glory, with the death as the glory's decisive showing. For Michaels, "we looked at his glory" is "a testimony to Jesus’ entire life on earth". Lincoln: "for those with the eyes of faith, there was glory all the way through Jesus’ life."[^time] The reading that confines the beholding to the glorification alone is declined.

**The widening.** The beholding widens to the church, and the widening is the text's own, at verse 16. Westcott: "The circle of the eye-witnesses passes into the larger fellowship of the Christian Church". Dodd holds that, now he is no longer seen, faith is still how the glory is seen. Bultmann argues that later believers see too. He endorses von Campenhausen's description of the "we" here as a *pluralis ecclesiasticus*: von Campenhausen, he writes, "takes the correct view".[^widening] That case is received at the widening of verse 16 and not at this clause. Bultmann's and Barrett's reading of the "we" here as the church is declined at this clause.

## Where the light's advent ends

Two settlements made earlier in the commentary are completed at this clause, and [the movement's landing page](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/) states both. They are named here so that the verse is read with them.
- **The light's advent toward perception.** The light was always present to the world it made, and its coming is one advent, read through John 1:6–13. That reading's end point is deepened at this clause, and not moved. The hour stays the end of the light's advent toward perception. Verse 14 settles what the perception is: the glory perceived in the flesh, through the signs, by those given to see, during the ministry. The fullness of that perception, and of the life it opens, comes at the glorification. This is held with high confidence.
- **The end of the Word's one life within the Prologue.** Within the Prologue, the account of the Word's one life ends where the light is seen, ἐθεασάμεθα τὴν δόξαν αὐτοῦ. Its condition is σὰρξ ἐγένετο, the point where the Word arrives in the flesh. This is the editor's ruling. The shape of the sentence is Dodd's: the Logos, the place of life and light, became flesh, and the effect was that we beheld his glory.[^shape]

The two ends are kept distinct. The first lies in the Gospel, at the hour; the second lies in the Prologue.

## The glory — God's own presence and character, manifest in the flesh

The glory beheld is God's own presence and character, made manifest in the incarnate Word. It is seen in his signs, and conclusively in his death. This is held with high confidence.

**What the glory is.** For Dodd, the glory, כָּבוֹד, is God made known in his being, nature and presence, in a way human experience can meet. And in the earthly life he finds "no longer any thought of visible light or radiance accompanying Christ", yet faith could apprehend God's presence and power there. Barth: "*Etheasametha tēn doxan autou* thus means unquestionably that we perceived his *deity*." Schnackenburg ties the vision of the glory to the believer's grasp of the signs (2:11; 11:40). Dodd hears the words of Cana as "clearly intended to recall" the Prologue's.[^whatglory]

**The flesh reveals.** The flesh is the place where the glory is manifest, not a veil to be seen through. Ridderbos: "Any suggestion that in the Fourth Gospel one can separate “flesh” and “glory,” history and revelation, violates the most specific aspect of that Gospel’s character". Keener writes that "his whole enfleshment, and especially his mortality and death, constitute the ultimate revelation of God's grace and truth".[^reveals]

**The hiddenness is to unbelief.** Barth: "He is concealed and yet, to those who perceive, not concealed, in the possession of perfect divine doxa." He grounds the concealment in the flesh, taken apart from the Word's own act of revealing: "If Jesus Christ does *not* do this work of his, the *sarx* can and must conceal, hide, and shut". "But just as certainly", he continues, "it can and must reveal when Jesus Christ does this work of his through it." And of those who did not perceive: "The glory of the Logos was hidden from them. It had to be, for it manifested itself in the flesh."[^barthhidden] Bultmann's paradox names the same two sides: "It is in his sheer humanity that he is the Revealer", and the glory "is to be seen in the σάρξ and nowhere else". So does Schnackenburg's "in spite of".[^bultmannparadox] That the hiddenness is to unbelief, and not a property of the flesh, is this commentary's own reading of all three. It rests on [the exclusion of disguise](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/10-word-became-flesh/) already fixed at the opening clause: the Word did not merely appear in the flesh.

## The glory and the light of verse 4

The glory beheld at verse 14 is the same reality as the life and light of verse 4, *in him was life, and the life was the light of men*. It is the light of the Word in whom was life, [the life that was the light](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/07-anthropology-light-of-men/), now manifest in the flesh to those given to see it. This is held with high confidence, as a claim about what the two verses say.

Dodd says it most fully. In the incarnate Christ men saw "the 'glory' of the eternal Logos", the series of statements about the eternal light in the Prologue reaching its climax in σὰρξ ἐγένετο, with the beholding as its effect. The beholding follows "in consequence of the incarnation of the Logos, i.e. of the eternal Light".[^dodd] The others concur, each in his own terms, and none as fully:
- Barth concedes, as one of a series of concessions he then qualifies, that verses 4 and 5 speak "of the same light" whose arising verses 14 and following depict. He speaks there of the light, not yet of the glory.
- Bultmann looks to the Revealer for "the light which he as Creator signified", and does not name the glory there.
- Westcott: "Christ, “the Light of the world,” is seen by the believer to be the manifested glory of God."
- Ridderbos, writing away from this verse, summarizes the Prologue's "glory of the light as a fullness of grace and truth".
- Keener grants that a link between light and glory "may not have been obvious to all ancient readers", and finds it "quite possible" that John alludes to his portrayal of Jesus as light.[^concur]

Barth also distinguishes the two terms: "*Doxa* differs from *phōs* in vv. 4f., if I am right, in the fact that it does not denote God’s divine self-manifestation as such but the qualities of God that are at work in it". Later he sets the sphere of the glory beside that of the revelation of redemption. He holds the two "not the same", "although the one may be seen in the other as its basis within the deity".[^barthdistinct] This commentary receives his distinction as a distinction within what the manifestation shows, the ground seen in what it grounds, and not as a second reality.

This is a claim about what the two verses *say*. Whether they were composed as a pair is a question about the Prologue's architecture, and it is held there, [at the Foyer's inner pair](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/07-foyers-inner-pair/). The content claim rests most strongly on Dodd. The others concur in shorter statements, and Keener hedges.

## Ὡς — defining, not comparing

The glory is the glory of the only Son, and not merely like it. The ὡς defines; it does not compare. This is held with very high confidence.

Schnackenburg: "the ὡς does not mark a restriction (“as though”, “as it were”) or make a comparison, but defines the glory precisely and indicates its exact nature". Brown: "The meaning of “as” is, of course, not “as if” but “in the quality of.”" Calvin: "The word *as* does not, in this passage, denote an inappropriate comparison". The editor of the *Ancient Christian Commentary* reports that Theodore of Mopsuestia went on to address "those who make too much of the particle “as,”" as if John were comparing Jesus with an only son.[^hos]

The comparative reading has one major commentator in its favour. Lincoln holds that, with no definite articles in the clause, it "is best taken in terms of a human comparison rather than as a direct reference to Jesus as the only Son in relation to the Father". The Father and the Son, he adds, are "only anticipated by the human analogy". Barth reports the comparative reading as Holtzmann's and Bauer's, and answers that the ὡς "is not necessarily saying" that what follows is a comparison.[^comparative] It is the same reading as the one declined below as proverbial, a father's only son in general, and it is declined at both places on one ground: the context fixes the Father.

## Μονογενής — the Father's only and beloved Son, unique in kind

Each part of the word's sense is held at its own confidence.

**Not "begotten" in the word.** The word means uniqueness: alone of its kind, or, where a parent is in view, an only child. Begetting is in neither sense. This is held with very high confidence.
- Dodd: μονογενής, "from μόνος and γένος", "does not mean (at this period at any rate) 'only-begotten' (μονογέννητος), but 'alone of his kind', 'unique'".
- Brown: "there is little Greek justification for the translation of monogenēs as “only begotten.”"
- Morris: "the Greek term means no more than “only,” “unique.”"
- Keener: "the concept of “begetting” is not present".
- McHugh concurs on the word's primary sense, "unique, alone of its kind", though his conclusion at this verse is offered with care.[^unique]

The dissent is weighed:
- Ridderbos holds that "a text like 1 Jn. 5:18 proves that there is every reason to assume that here, too, “only begotten” means more than simply “only,”".
- Schnackenburg holds that "According to the Greek, the notion of origin cannot be excluded from μονογενής", but that "in view of the Hebrew sub-stratum, it cannot be certainly affirmed". In a note he adds that the evangelist "certainly combines" origin with this attribute of the Son. Yet at this verse, he writes, "It might have been part of the Son of God Christology of the evangelist; but once more he appears to remain for the moment within the tradition of the voice from heaven at the baptism".
- Köstenberger and Swain translate μονογενής as "only" or "one-of-a-kind Son". In a note they add: "we do not believe that the evidence against the traditional interpretation of monogenēs is as strong as is sometimes suggested."[^dissent]

This commentary receives origin, where the evangelist combines it, at the next two parts, from the context and not from the word. It reads the verse Ridderbos cites as bearing on the relation of Father and Son, not on the meaning of the word. That Schnackenburg's last sentence leans away from origin at this verse is this commentary's reading of it.

**With πατρός, the Father's only son.** With a father beside it, the word means an only son. This is held with high confidence. Barrett: "though μονογενής means in itself ‘only of its kind’, when used in relation to father it can hardly mean anything other than only(-begotten) son". Dodd says it more flatly still: "one who is μονογενής relatively to a πατήρ can be no other than the only *son*".[^onlyson] McHugh prefers, with care, that the word at this verse means "quite unique, in a class of his own", and that sense is declined. He keeps a father in the phrase, the Word "seen as someone quite unique, coming from another most appropriately designated as a father". But he does not let the father beside it give the adjective its sense.[^mchugh] That the πατρός beside it brings the sense of an only child into play is this commentary's reading. The missing article before πατρός remains a difficulty, as Barth notes, and it is kept. Harris names what settles it: "the present context that focuses on God the Father and his Son".[^article]

**The beloved Son, in a relation that precedes the sending.** The term carries the Son's special relation to the Father, as the object of his love, and that relation comes before the sending. This is held with high confidence. Keener finds that the "beloved" nuance "probably comes to the forefront in Johannine usage", and that Jesus is μονογενής "not in the sense of derivation but as unique and the special object of divine love". He finds too that "Jesus’ special relation to the Father exists in this Gospel long before his public, official glorification, probably in his preincarnate state". As an epithet for Christ the term is the evangelist's own, as Schnackenburg notes (1:18; 3:16, 18; compare 1 John 4:9).[^beloved] Reading it with 1:18 and 3:16, so that the relation precedes the sending, is this commentary's own reading. Bultmann argues for a different sense at this verse, on the ground that the verse comes from the evangelist's source. That case is declined, because this commentary reads the text as it stands.[^bultmann]

**Eternal generation.** The church's doctrine of the Son's eternal generation is engaged here, and is not made the word's own assertion. The doctrine is the historically specific articulation of the eternal, unique filial relation the passage asserts. This commentary denies nothing of it, and places its ground in the passage and in the Gospel's testimony, not in the word alone.
- Bavinck sets the relation outside time: it "was not born in time but exists from eternity". He treats the eternal generation itself at length.
- Calvin calls him "*the Only-begotten*, because he is the only Son of God by nature". He has named the eternal generation at the first verse, and in his comment on the first words of this verse writes of "*the Speech* begotten by God before all ages".
- Cyril of Jerusalem confesses him "begotten of the Father eternally and ineffably".
- McHugh holds that "It is certain that after Irenaeus the term is used both of the pre-Incarnate and of the Incarnate Word with the meaning ‘only-begotten’."
- Köstenberger does not call the doctrine unscriptural, and argues "only that John did not conceive of the relationship in those terms". Köstenberger and Swain, who translate the word "only", hold that "this lexical move does not by itself defeat the Augustinian doctrine".[^generation]

The doctrine is taken up again later in the commentary.

The lexical question is settled here, and is not reopened when the word returns at verse 18.

## Παρὰ πατρός — and the Son against the children

παρὰ πατρός goes with μονογενοῦς and names the Father whose only one he is. This is held with high confidence.

Schnackenburg takes it so. The preposition "need mean no more than a genitive", though "it is also possible that it is a short way of saying “the only-begotten (Son), coming from the Father”". Harris construes it with μονογενοῦς too. For him the phrase is equivalent to "[such glory as belongs to the only Son] who came from the Father", and "alludes to the Son’s mission, not to his eternal generation". Brown finds "no major difference in meaning no matter which word the phrase modifies". He adds: "If we read “an only Son coming from the Father,” the reference is to the mission of the Son, not his procession within the Trinity; the “we have seen” makes this certain." Barrett reads it of the Son, who "not merely is his Father’s Son, but proceeds from him in personal though never independent existence".[^para]

This commentary reads the phrase as a definition of who the Son is. It receives the mission, the Gospel's constant thought, from the context, not as the preposition's sense, and that reading is its own. The proverbial reading takes the phrase as a father's only son in general, "such honor (δόξαν) as an only son receives from his father", in Goodspeed's words as Harris reports them. It is declined, and with it the comparative reading at ὡς, which is the same reading.[^proverbial]

Here the Gospel first names the Father and the Son. Barth: "Here for the first time the Logos is to be called the Son of God."[^firsttime] The Son's sonship is his own, and the sonship of verse 12's children is given: *to them he gave the right to become children of God*. That is [the bound fixed where the children's birth from God was read](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/15-begotten-of-god/): their begetting is participation in God's gift, not in his being.

## Πλήρης — the Father's only Son is full of grace and truth

πλήρης describes the one named in the genitive, the μονογενής. The Father's only and beloved Son is full of grace and truth, revealing that attribute of the Father, and grace and truth still define the glory, because the glory is his. This is held with high confidence, and that confidence is capped by the grammar.

**The construction.** πλήρης is taken here with the genitive, with the Son. Grammatically it can be read three ways: with the Word as a nominative, with the glory, or with the genitive.[^text]
- Schnackenburg takes it with the genitive "In the present text", since "the δόξα, as that of the only-begotten Son, needs no further description". In the hymn he reconstructs behind the Prologue, it was a nominative.
- McHugh: "If πλήρης is genitive, then it may qualify αὐτοῦ or μονογενοῦς or πατρός (or both the latter). Grammatically, there is no way to decide." Yet "it is practically certain that in the mind of the evangelist it applied to the former (the μονογενής) as revealing that selfsame attribute of the latter (a father …)".
- Harris: "Its antecedent may be ὁ λόγος (nom.) or, more probably, αὐτοῦ or μονογενοῦς (gen.; cf. vv. 16–17), but scarcely δόξαν (acc.)."
- Barth: "Modern scholars usually relate plērēs to autou". And "To me an epexegetical relation to *monogenous* seems even better."
- Ridderbos has the phrase "further qualify him who has just been called the only begotten from the Father". If the adjective is taken as indeclinable, he adds in a note, one could link it with the glory "or, even better, with αὐτοῦ".
- Michaels: it "probably modifies “a father’s One and Only.”" Barrett: "it refers to Christ, and looks back to μονογενοῦς and αὐτοῦ."
- Bultmann denies it of the glory, "for it is the Revealer who is being described". He takes it either with the Word, conditionally, after Schlatter, or with αὐτοῦ.[^construction]

**Verse 16 confirms it.** Schnackenburg: "Πλήρωμα takes up πλήρης".[^fullness] Verse 16's *from his fullness* reads back to the Son.

**Against.** Some take it with the glory:
- Carson: "it seems best to take the expression as a modifier of ‘glory’".
- Keener: "we would suggest that the “fulness” probably modifies “glory”", from the allusion to Exodus 33 and 34.
- Robertson: "Probably" with the glory, "or genitive with μονογενους", and he allows the nominative as well.
- Lincoln, by paraphrase: Jesus' glory "full of grace and truth".

Others take it with the Word:
- Westcott, with "the main subject of the sentence".
- Morris: "There is probably most to be said for the first view".
- Waetjen: "Of course, it is the Logos that is full of grace and truth, not the glory of the Logos", though he allows the genitive link too.

Köstenberger predicates it of "the Word-become-flesh" without discussing the construal.[^against]

**Why the confidence is capped.** The confidence is capped because the indeclinable πλήρης rests on a usage the grammars grant. Harris: "When followed by the gen., πλήρης is indecl." Zerwick: "often indecl. in HGK." Carson reads the same grammar toward the glory, "because ‘full’ is regularly indeclinable". Keener reaches the glory by another road, from the Exodus allusion, and he hedges.[^cap]

**"No major difference"?** Brown finds "no major difference in meaning". Morris calls it "a problem of Greek grammar rather than of meaning, for on any showing it is the incarnate Word who is full of grace and truth". Michaels finds the difference between the Word and the Son small, "because in either case the phrase refers to “Jesus Christ”". He finds the construal with the glory "less satisfactory", because grace and truth are more fitly said of persons.[^difference] This commentary finds that the construal makes little difference between the Word and the Son, and only in part against the glory. That finding is tested where the fullness returns, at verse 16.

## Χάρις καὶ ἀλήθεια — God's covenant love and faithfulness

The pair renders the חֶסֶד וֶאֱמֶת God proclaimed to Moses at Exodus 34:6, his steadfast love and faithfulness, now the character of the incarnate Son. This is held with high confidence.

**The Old Testament root.** Schnackenburg finds an Old Testament pairing here, not a Greek idiom. McHugh calls the phrase "a perfect rendering of words in the Hebrew text of Exod 34:6, and if taken as an evocation of this text, it suits the context to perfection". For Lincoln it is "the prologue’s equivalent of ‘abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness’". Keener judges that the accumulation of allusions to Exodus 33 and 34 in these verses "leaves little doubt that John’s phrase is a conscious allusion". Barth asks whether, in the light of verse 17, there does not have to be such an allusion.[^root]

**The Septuagint objection.** The Septuagint normally renders חֶסֶד by ἔλεος, not χάρις, and the objection is answered from the evangelist's practice. Brown: "John’s use of Scripture is often not faithful to LXX". Dodd allows the representation, with John's own shift of sense.[^lxx]

**The two terms.** In the hymn, Schnackenburg suggests, ἀλήθεια may have kept the faithfulness of its Hebrew. The evangelist probably took it in a sense nearer to divine reality itself.[^truth] χάρις leads the pair, since only χάρις is taken up again at verse 16.

**Declined.** Bultmann rejects the reference to Exodus 34:6, on the Johannine sense of ἀλήθεια.[^bultmanntruth] His ground is received for the pair's second word, and it does not exclude the first.

**Recorded.** Michaels reads the pair as the grace or gift of truth, which is to say of the Spirit, and names the figure a hendiadys, as at 4:23–24.[^michaels]

The pair is taken up again at verse 17, where *the grace and the truth came to be through Jesus Christ*.

## What the verse hands on

The clause hands on what later verses take up:
- The glory's pole at verse 14, grounded here as a claim about what the verses say, goes to the Prologue's architecture.
- The fullness goes to verse 16, and the pair of grace and truth to verse 17.
- The lexical sense of μονογενής goes to verse 18 as settled.
- The aspects go to the movement's temporal reference.
- The dwelling, the glory and the light of verse 4, and the end of the Word's one life within the Prologue are taken up again later in the commentary. The temple theme is recorded there and not built on.

How firmly each is held:
- **The dwelling:** God's dwelling with his people, in the tabernacle's and Wisdom's idiom: high. The Shekinah: a possible echo, not asserted.
- **Transience:** not in the verb, and true of the sojourn: high.
- **The aspect:** constative, unfolding the becoming: high.
- **The beholding:** among the eyewitnesses, a given perception, through the whole ministry: high. The widening to the church is received at verse 16.
- **The light's advent toward perception:** its end point deepened, not moved: high.
- **The glory:** God's own presence and character, manifest in the flesh: high.
- **The glory and the light of verse 4:** the same reality, as a claim about what the verses say: high.
- **The ὡς:** defining, not comparing: very high.
- **Μονογενής:**
  - not "begotten" in the word: very high;
  - with πατρός, the Father's only son: high;
  - the beloved Son, in a relation before the sending: high;
  - eternal generation: engaged, not made the word's assertion.
- **Παρὰ πατρός** with μονογενοῦς, the Son against the children: high.
- **Πλήρης** of the Son: high, capped by the grammar.
- **Χάρις καὶ ἀλήθεια** as God's covenant love and faithfulness: high.
