The previous section declined a chiasm of the whole and left one pair to be weighed: the light of verse 4 and the glory of verse 14. This section weighs it. The pair was first drawn from its near end, in [the commentary on verses 4–5](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/02-gradatio-and-foyer/), where the life that was the light of men was set as one pole of the Foyer and verse 14 as the other. Here it is seen from its far end. The life-and-light of verse 4 and the glory beheld at verse 14 are held as the Foyer's inner correspondence, and two kinds of evidence support it: what the two poles are, and where the text places them.

## What the two poles are

The two poles are one reality. *In him was life, and the life was the light of men*: ἡ ζωὴ ἦν τὸ φῶς τῶν ἀνθρώπων. *And we beheld his glory*: ἐθεασάμεθα τὴν δόξαν αὐτοῦ. The commentary on verses 4–5 grounded this with Dodd, for whom the series of statements about the eternal light in the Prologue reaches its climax when the Logos, the place of both life and light, becomes flesh.[^dodd] This commentary's reading of verse 14 receives it at that end: the glory beheld is the life-and-light of verse 4, now manifest in the flesh.

Others say it in their own voices, and each says it in his own way. Barth: "Certainly vv. 4–5 are speaking of the same light as that whose epiphany or arising is depicted in vv. 14ff."[^barth] Bultmann holds it of the light and of the life. The light that shone in Jesus is "none other than that which had always shone in creation", and the life Jesus gives is "not *another* ζωή" than the pre-existent Logos gave.[^bultmann] Nolland finds in the Logos become flesh the "more intense counterpart" of a shining "since the world began".[^nolland] Pagels holds one *logos* in two manifestations: "previously manifest as light (1:3–4)", it at last appears "in the world in human form".[^pagels] And Keener, with a double hedge, allows it: though a connection between light and glory may not have been obvious to every ancient reader, it is "quite possible" that John alludes to his portrayal of Jesus as light.[^keener]

These are not one claim repeated. Barth speaks of the same light, Bultmann of a continuity between creation and redemption, Nolland of a counterpart more intense than what it answers, Pagels of a single *logos* manifest twice, and Keener of an allusion that is quite possible. What they share is the content of the two poles. None of them says that the evangelist composed the two verses as a pair.

## Where the text places them

The second kind of evidence is placement. Each pole is followed by a witness that confirms it. After the first movement comes John the Baptist, sent to bear witness to the light (verses 6–8). After verse 14 comes John again: *John bears witness about him, and has cried out* (verse 15). Ridderbos says that these references to the Baptist's witness "come at two strategic points in the prologue: first, after the thematic beginning in vss. 1–5, before it is further elaborated in vss. 9–13, and then after the pronouncements in vs. 14 about the revelation of the glory of God in the incarnation of the Word, before those pronouncements are explicated in vss. 16–18".[^ridderbos]

Morna Hooker makes the same observation, and it reaches this commentary through Leon Morris, who quotes her. Each passage, she says, refers to John "as the witness who confirms the truth of what has just been said": that light is shining in the darkness, and that we have seen the glory of the incarnate Logos. Ridderbos refers to her article for the same observation. So the placement evidence is a single argument, carried by two commentators, and Hooker's article has not been read here in its own publication.[^hooker]

## What remains the commentary's reading

That the evangelist composed verse 4 and verse 14 as a bracket is this commentary's own reading of that evidence, and it is disclosed as such. The content is grounded: the two poles are one reality. The placement is in the text: a witness follows each pole. That the two verses were *built* to answer each other, as a frame within the frame, is the commentary's inference from both.

It is held with high confidence, and provisionally: the confidence is real, and it is limited, for three reasons. No commentator read directly here states the bracket in his own voice. The placement evidence is one argument, though it has two carriers. And the standard this commentary set for the brackets it holds firmly, [where the Prologue begins](/commentary/john/1-1-to-3/02-foyer-bracket/), is not met.

## The case against

Nothing in the record sets the two poles against each other as different realities. What stands against the pair stands against its composition, and it is recorded here.

No chiastic proposal whose pairings the record reports pairs verse 4 with verse 14. M.-É. Boismard sets verse 14 opposite verses 9–11, and R. Alan Culpepper sets it opposite verses 9–10.[^chiasm] Both set verses 4–5 opposite verse 16, a pairing this commentary declines with Brown, who finds it "highly imaginative", as [the previous section](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/06-the-framework/) set out.[^brown-23]

The one scheme that sets verses 3–5 beside verse 14 is Mary Coloe's strophic reading, which reads the Prologue's structure against the creation account of Genesis 1. Carlos Sosa Siliezar, who reports it, judges it "a forced structure".[^sosa] And Craig Koester observes that the imagery changes at verse 14: "Instead of cosmic images like light and darkness, the text alludes to Israel's particular history", the glory that filled the tabernacle.[^koester] He speaks of a change of imagery and of scope, not of the reality named.

One thing in the record might look like a case against the pair and is not. Brown, Carson and Schnackenburg read verse 4 of creation and not yet of the Incarnation. Brown judges that a jump from creation in verse 3 to the coming of Jesus in verse 4 "seems exceedingly abrupt". Carson, if verse 4 is read with the three verses before it, finds it "more likely" that the life in the Word is related to creation and not to salvation. And Schnackenburg: "Thus v. 4 still looks to creation's dawn."[^creation] That is not a case against the pair. It is the reading the pair presupposes, for the pair holds that the life-and-light of the Logos at creation is what is beheld as glory in the flesh. Barth sets aside the same Incarnation reading of verse 4 in the paragraph where he calls the light of verses 4–5 and of verses 14ff. "the same light".[^barth] And Carson allows, of verse 5, that John may want his readers "to see in the Word both the light of creation and the light of the redemption the Word brings in his incarnation".[^carson-120]

## How firmly this is held

- **The content, that the two poles are one reality**: grounded with Dodd, received at verse 14, and said in their own ways by Barth, Bultmann, Nolland, Pagels and Keener.
- **The placement, a witness after each pole**: in the text, on a single argument with two carriers.
- **The composition, that the evangelist built the two verses as a bracket**: this commentary's own reading, held with high but provisional confidence.
- **The case against**: against the composition only. No chiastic proposal pairs verse 4 with verse 14, Coloe's scheme is judged forced by the one who reports it, and the imagery changes at verse 14.

The commentary on verses 6–13 held, provisionally, that the second movement is enclosed by this pair. That reading now stands on the ground named in this section: the second movement is what the content pair encloses, at the same provisional confidence. The pairing has been assessed here, and it has not been raised. If a major commentator, read in his own words, were found setting verse 4 and verse 14 against each other as different realities, the reading would be withdrawn.

The figure draws the pair from its far end, with its provisional force marked, as the commentary on verses 4–5 drew it from its near end. The witness that follows verse 14 is the next section's subject.

[^dodd]: C. H. Dodd, *The Interpretation of the Fourth Gospel* (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1953), p. 206: "The climax of the series of statements about the eternal light in the Prologue is the statement that the Logos, the τόπος of both life and light, σὰρξ ἐγένετο, and the effect of this, the evangelist adds, was that ἐθεασάμεθα τὴν δόξαν αὐτοῦ (i. 14)." At p. 207 he draws the identity nearer: "in consequence of the incarnation of the Logos, i.e. of the eternal Light, ἐθεασάμεθα τὴν δόξαν αὐτοῦ, δόξαν ὡς μονογενοῦς παρὰ πατρός". Dodd grounds what the two poles share. He does not say that the two verses were composed as a frame.

[^barth]: Karl Barth, *Witness to the Word: A Commentary on John 1* (lectures of 1925/26; ed. Walther Fürst, trans. Geoffrey W. Bromiley; Eerdmans, 1986), p. 42.
