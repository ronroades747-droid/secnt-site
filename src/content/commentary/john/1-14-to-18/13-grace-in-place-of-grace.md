After the witness, the ground: ὅτι ἐκ τοῦ πληρώματος αὐτοῦ ἡμεῖς πάντες ἐλάβομεν καὶ χάριν ἀντὶ χάριτος, *for from his fullness we have all received — grace in place of grace.* The Baptist's words [closed with verse 15](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/12-witness-who-still-speaks/), and the evangelist now gives the reason for the confession of verse 14, that the Word become flesh was [full of grace and truth](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/11-glory-beheld/).

Five things are fixed on this one sentence, read clause by clause, with verse 17 used as evidence and not decided here: what the ὅτι grounds; whose fullness this is; who "we all" are, and what the aorist gathers; what the καί introduces; and what χάριν ἀντὶ χάριτος says.

Read in summary, the verse says this. *For*, because the ὅτι gives the ground of verse 14's confession, with the Baptist's witness inside it. *From his fullness*, because the fullness is the Son's own fullness of grace and truth, from which a share is received while the source stays full. *We have all received*, because the eyewitnesses of verse 14 are widened at *all* to the whole believing church. The dash, because the καί introduces what was received without choosing among the senses the scholars give it. And *grace in place of grace*, because ἀντί keeps its own sense: each grace gives way to the next from the one fullness, unceasingly. It is not "grace upon grace", and the καί is not "and".

## Ὅτι — the ground of the confession, with the witness taken in

The ὅτι is causal. It gives the ground of verse 14's confession that the incarnate Word, the Father's only Son, was full of grace and truth, and a catchword fixes the link: πλήρης, *full*, is answered by πληρώματος, *fullness*. This is held with high confidence.

**The link to verse 14.** Ridderbos: "Vs. 16 is intended as a confirmation of vs. 14, and its causal 'for' (ὅτι) is not isolated by the insertion of vs. 15. Rather, it incorporates vs. 15 into what is explained." Schnackenburg: "Πλήρωμα takes up πλήρης".[^link] Westcott ties the words to verse 14's *full of grace and truth*: we know his character as such because we have received from him. The Baptist's witness set between, "pointing to the true nature of Christ, marks the source of this spiritual wealth."[^westcott13] Abbott finds that each ὅτι of the passage "seems to" ground one statement on a like one before it, *full* and *fullness*, *grace* and *grace*.[^abbott2180] Barrett, Robertson, Carson and Lincoln tie the verse to verse 14, Barrett "more probably" than to the Baptist's words. Bultmann sees verse 15 come between the two connecting words.[^tie] The catchwords [span the witness](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/03-the-articulation/) in the passage's articulation, and the reading ὅτι against the rival καί was [secured with the text](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/01-the-text/).

**The statement, not the fact.** The ὅτι grounds the *statement*, not the fact. Receiving from him is how "we" know him full, not what makes him full. Barth draws the distinction: the beholding of his glory, and the witness grounded in it, "is now explained by the fact that this fulness is in him alone".[^barth115]

**The witness taken in.** Verse 15 is [a witness integral to the passage](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/12-witness-who-still-speaks/), set at verse 14 to attest the glory. So the ὅτι does not pass over it as a parenthesis. It confirms verse 14 with the Baptist's witness inside the confirmation.

**Three readings are declined.**
- **The Baptist speaking on.** Origen, Barth and Calvin hear the Baptist still speaking here. That reading was declined when the Baptist's words were found to end with verse 15.[^speaking]
- **The "for" as Christians confirming the Baptist's verdict.** Harris has the evangelist confirm the Baptist's testimony "by appealing to the uniform experience of believers". Morris: the point of the "for" "is perhaps that Christians in general can support this verdict". This does not explain the catchword.[^verdict]
- **A loose connective.** For McHugh, ὅτι "may then be considered a very loose connective, which scarcely subordinates its clause at all". So read, the clause loses the causal ground the catchword gives, though McHugh himself keeps its link to the fullness of verse 14.[^loose]

The confidence is high and not very high. Whether the ὅτι passes over verse 15 or takes it in turns on how verse 15 is read, not on anything in verse 16 itself.

## Τὸ πλήρωμα αὐτοῦ — the Son's fullness of grace and truth

The fullness is the fullness of grace and truth that verse 14 named in the Father's only Son. This is held with high confidence. Barrett: "It looks back to v. 14 where it is said that Christ was 'full of grace and truth'; of this full complement of grace and truth 'we' have all received." McHugh, Keener and Michaels read it so.[^fullness]

**Its depth.** The depth of that fullness is the divine life the Son has from the Father. Schnackenburg names "the riches of divine life which the Logos receives from the Father (5:26) and from which he enriches his own (10:10)". Westcott names "the full measure of all the divine powers and graces which were concentrated absolutely in Christ, the Incarnate Word." For Bultmann the Revealer "allows man to share in the fulness of his divine Being."[^depth]

**No technical weight.** The word carries no technical weight from Gnostic speculation or from the later Pauline letters. Carson: "Thus 'fulness' here bears no technical, gnostic sense." Schnackenburg, McHugh and Barrett set the Gnostic pleroma aside. Robertson, on the other side, hears the Pauline term used here by John of the incarnate Word, and Brown leaves its connotation open.[^technical]

**A share, the source undiminished.** ἐκ is partitive. "We all" received a share from that fullness, which remains undiminished. Harris renders "we have all had a share". Michaels: "We do not receive all of Christ's 'fullness' (plērōma), but draw 'grace and truth' from it as from a boundless supply." For Chrysostom, after the overflowing "he still remains full and undiminished, in spite of supplying others."[^share]

**Whose fullness, either way.** At verse 14, *full of grace and truth* was read of the Son, and the question whether πλήρης belongs to the Son or to the glory was found to make little difference between the Word and the Son, and only in part against the glory. That test falls here, and it holds. On the reading of [the commentary on verse 14](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/11-glory-beheld/), the fullness is the Son's. Keener, who ties πλήρης to the glory, still reads the fullness of verse 16 back to "full of grace and truth". The fullness is the Son's either way.[^either]

## Ἡμεῖς πάντες ἐλάβομεν — the eyewitnesses, widened to the church

"We all" are the eyewitness company of verse 14, widened at *all* to the whole believing church, present and to come. This is held with high confidence.

**The widening.** Westcott: "The circle of the eye-witnesses passes into the larger fellowship of the Christian Church." Ridderbos: "'We all' is now the appropriate expression because not only the 'we' of vs. 14 is meant but the entire community of those who believe in Christ." Keener: the first person plural "would naturally refer primarily to the eyewitnesses of v. 14, but the verb indicates that it embraces also all who believe through their witness".[^widening] Carson: "The 'we' who have received this new grace may have begun with John and the earliest eyewitnesses (cf. 1:14), but it now includes all who share the same faith (cf. 20:29)." Schnackenburg includes "Christians of a later date who did not see him during his life on earth", and Chrysostom "all the fullness of the faithful who then were and now are and hereafter shall be".[^church] The receiving is wider than the beholding, and that widening is where [the beholding of verse 14 reaches the church](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/09-architectural-implications/).

**The aorist.** ἐλάβομεν is a constative aorist. It sums up the community's receiving, which began in the reception of verse 12 and goes on. Harris has the aorist "viewing many successive receipts unitarily", and setting it against ἐθεασάμεθα, the beholding of verse 14. Morris: "John uses the same verb and the same tense as he has used of the single act of receiving Christ in verse 12." Michaels: "To receive the Giver is to receive and partake of his gifts."[^aorist] For Robertson the receiving is "a wider experience than beholding", one "that all believers may have". Bultmann sets the beholding within the Revealer's giving and the believer's receiving.[^beholding]

**One receiving, still going on.** Harris's many receipts and Morris's single act are joined here. The aorist gathers into one the receiving that began when "we" received him, at verse 12, and has gone on since. Its continuance is not in the tense but in its object, χάριν ἀντὶ χάριτος. The reception of verse 12 was found to be [believing](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/14-the-turn-children-of-god/), and to receive from his fullness is that reception bearing its fruit.

**Two readings are declined.**
- **The Baptist and the prophets.** Origen hears the "we" as the Baptist with the prophets before him, who received their gift "from the fullness of Christ". It falls with the reading that the Baptist speaks on.[^prophets]
- **All humanity.** Abbott allows that "we all" "may mean 'every human being from the creation of man.'" Calvin weighs it: "It is indeed uncertain whether he speaks generally of the whole human race". He finds "no absurdity" in the wider sense, but prefers the narrower: "it is more probable that here he especially recommends that rich abundance of blessings which Christ displayed at his coming".[^humanity]

The church's "we" is received at *all*, not read back into verse 14. If the eyewitness "we" of verse 14 were ever to move, this reading would move with it.

## Καί — introducing what was received

χάριν ἀντὶ χάριτος is the content of the receiving from his fullness. The scholars divide on the force of the καί, and agree on its function. That shared function is held with high confidence.
- **It defines.** Harris: "Καί is epex. … and defines what is received by believers". McHugh calls it "an epexegetical καί", "defining further the object of ἐλάβομεν". Schnackenburg reads it as explanatory, "(that is to say) grace upon grace".[^defines]
- **It heightens.** Abbott: the καί does not mean "namely" "but 'and, what is more,' 'and indeed,' or 'yea'", and Morris takes the same sense after him.[^heightens]
- **It opens a second clause.** Barth: "in a new beginning, in a second clause, we are told emphatically (this is the point of the kai) what we have received here: grace for grace".[^second]

Bultmann weighs two constructions of the verb. Either ἐλάβομεν takes a second object after a καί meaning "namely", or it stands absolutely and is supplied again before καὶ χάριν. "It is not possible to decide", he writes, but "As far as the meaning is concerned it makes no difference."[^constructions] On each of the three readings the clause names what was received. That is what the evidence settles, and it is all this commentary holds. The choice among the three is recorded and not forced.

## Χάριν ἀντὶ χάριτος — grace in place of grace

Grace is given in place of grace, unceasingly, from the one fullness of the Son. ἀντί keeps its own sense, *in place of*. The first grace is the grace before it in the believers' receiving, not the grace of an earlier covenant. The phrase unfolds the fullness: what "we all" received from him is grace upon grace in the only way ἀντί can say it, each gift giving way to the next, and the supply never failing. This is held with high confidence.

**Ἀντί as "in place of".** Bultmann: "Ἀντί means 'instead of'". Yet neither for the source nor for the evangelist can the Revealer's grace take the place of an earlier Old Testament grace: "It can only mean the χάρις of the Revealer, whose inexhaustibility is unfolded in its ever changing variety". Morris reads it so: "Probably also he means that as one piece of divine grace (so to speak) recedes it is replaced by another. God's grace to his people is continuous and is never exhausted." For Michaels, ἀντί, "which strictly means 'in place of,'" is used "to accent the boundless generosity of the Giver".[^antiplace]

**Succession, not a single exchange.** Harris: "Certainly more is implied than a single substitutionary exchange." Because the text sets grace against grace, and not grace against the law, he finds "a rapid and perpetual succession of blessings". Barrett: "as it proceeds one grace is exchanged only for another", with Philo for the usage. Robertson pictures "'grace' taking the place of 'grace' like the manna fresh each morning".[^succession] Barth reads the same gift given and received at different times, "not becoming another gift but retaining its material identity", though he offers that nuance with a "perhaps". Schnackenburg reports that "according to most modern commentators", ἀντί "indicates the ceaseless stream of graces which succeed one another."[^stream]

**Both graces from his fullness, with verse 17 as evidence.** Schnackenburg: "But all graces come from the incarnate Logos, and v. 17 also tells against this explanation. Only the law was given through Moses, grace only came through Jesus Christ." Barrett: "nor is the grace of God available in two grades." Lincoln, who holds the other view, concedes: "The next verse does not, of course, speak of grace in association with the Mosaic covenant". Ridderbos holds that John "does not understand the relation of the old and the new, Moses and Christ, in that way".[^bothgraces] Barth grants that verse 17 raises no objection to the other view, and declines it on John's usage instead: "John is not Paul, and we have no parallel for the Old Testament grace even if we take that Pauline usage into account."[^barth117]

**The argument from the sentence.** The sentence makes χάριν ἀντὶ χάριτος the content of what "we all" received from his fullness. A grace not received from that fullness cannot be one of the two terms unless the law is read into the second χάρις. So the reading that makes the law the first grace has to supply that grace from outside the clause, while the reading adopted finds it in the clause already. Carson's answer is real. He holds that "the law that was given through Moses, and the grace and truth that came through Jesus Christ (v. 17), alike sprang from the fulness of the Word (v. 16), whether in his pre-existent oneness with the Father, or in his status as the Word-made-flesh."[^carson134] On the first of these, the fullness of verse 16 reaches behind the incarnate Son whom verse 14 named, and this commentary reads the fullness as the incarnate Son's. The second is recorded, and is not answered here.

## Not "upon"

ἀντί means *in place of*, and accumulation would need ἐπί. This is held with high confidence. Brown: "Normally, however, accumulation would be expressed by epi, while anti implies opposition or substitution." Carson: "The normal preposition for such meaning is epi, not anti."[^upon]

The abundance that the rendering "upon" protects is received into the reading adopted, without its preposition. Keener: "Whether the phrase suggests ever-renewed or ever-increasing graces is hardly essential to the image; in either case, it would mean an inexhaustible supply of blessing." Lincoln allows that the phrase "could still be taken as referring to an abundance of grace which is always being renewed as one kind replaces another", before judging it more likely that the grace found in Jesus replaces the grace associated with the Mosaic covenant.[^abundance]

## The strongest alternative, heard in full

The strongest alternative is the reading that the grace of the law was replaced by the grace of the gospel. Its case is real.
- **McHugh:** "The strongest arguments in favour of this interpretation are that Jn 1:17, especially with its initial ὅτι, seems to point directly to it, and that it is the explanation favoured by the Greek Fathers."[^mchugh65]
- **Carson:** "The most convincing view takes anti in one of its most common uses (and by far the most common in the LXX) to mean 'instead of'".[^carson132]
- **Brown** finds that "Verse 17 seems to support this", and Theodore of Mopsuestia has the law named a grace and replaced: "instead of that grace … this grace is given."[^brownth]
- **Köstenberger** prefers "grace instead of grace", and Abbott hears in it, as in Philo, "the notion of 'exchange' rather than that of mere succession", which he "probably" finds intended.[^kostabbott]
- **McHugh** upholds it, with the proviso that the Old Law is a grace "superseded by a grace that is more attractive still", and his excursus speaks of "a grace which supersedes the very greatest blessings imparted in the Law". Yet on verse 17 he writes that the slight support for a reading with δέ "only confirms the interpretation of v. 16 as indicating not the replacement of one grace by another, but the continuity in God's bestowal of grace". The tension is his own. That continuity is the one he gives the reading of accumulation, the continuity of the gift "under the Old and the New Covenant", and it does not count for the reading adopted here.[^mchughtension]

The reading adopted shares this one's sense of ἀντί, and the case McHugh takes from de la Potterie: where the two terms are the same and one party receives, ἀντί means *instead of*, and the later grace is given in place of the earlier. What it does not take is the identification of the earlier grace with the law. That identification is where the clause is silent, and where verse 17, read as evidence, says otherwise. The confidence is high and not very high because of this alternative's strength: the commonest sense of ἀντί in the Greek Old Testament, the Greek Fathers, the ὅτι of verse 17, the case McHugh takes from de la Potterie, and the modern commentators who hold the reading, McHugh, Brown, Carson, Köstenberger and Lincoln.

## Philo's usage and John's purpose

Philo is the parallel the commentators cite. Carson objects that Philo speaks of graces in the plural, and that his point, that God is wise in dispensing his graces in small doses, is "quite unlike John's". McHugh reports de la Potterie's observation that John writes the singular, χάρις, "thus indicating that he is referring to the replacement of one specific and definite grace by another".[^philo] The objection holds against Philo's purpose, not against his preposition. Only the usage is borrowed: ἀντί for one grace given in place of another in an unending series. John's singular is answered by the grace's identity across its giving. The grace is one, given again and again, not a series of kinds.

## Three further readings, declined

- **Correspondence.** The reading that the believer's grace answers to Christ's has no footing in the word. Edwards states the ground at first hand: ἀντί "never actually means 'corresponding to', except possibly in certain compounds", and in most of the examples cited for the view it means "'in exchange'", so that "this obscure meaning … must fall on linguistic grounds". McHugh: ἀντί "is nowhere found with the meaning corresponding to in the LXX, in Hellenistic Greek or in the NT". Schnackenburg's tentative allowance of it, "Perhaps the preposition also indicates the correspondence between the grace possessed by the Logos and that of those who receive him", is recorded as an overtone the reading adopted can bear.[^correspondence] Calvin's own reading is close to it but is not it. He agrees with those who say "that we are watered with the graces which were poured out on Christ", and it is theologically true, but it is not what ἀντί says. Barth judges Calvin's reading "very fine and materially true", but "the anti … comes off much too badly to let us adopt this view."[^calvinbarth]
- **Exchange.** The reading that grace is given in return for faith is declined. Carson: such a quid pro quo "is alien to the New Testament in general and to John in particular". Of Augustine's form of it, Calvin: "This is piously and judiciously said, but has nothing to do with the present passage."[^exchange]
- **A higher grace for a lower.** Origen's form, "the more divine and greater prophetic grace from his fullness for the grace we received from him in relation to our free choice", is spoken in the person of the Baptist with the prophets before him, and rests on the "we" already declined. Others count Origen among the readers of the law's grace replaced: Brown and Edwards among the Greek Fathers, and Abbott with Chrysostom as appearing to discern it. Edwards finds Origen's views "complicated and perhaps not entirely consistent". The disagreement is recorded, and the decline rests on Origen's own text.[^origenform]

Westcott's growth, in which one measure of grace "realised and used" is exchanged "(as it were)" for a larger, is recorded as compatible with the reading adopted, and not adopted.[^growth]

## What the verse hands to verse 17

The verse hands on a constraint, not a verdict. On the reading adopted, verse 17's two members are not verse 16's two graces, and whether verse 17 is antithesis, progression or fulfilment is not the content of ἀντί. Whether the law was itself a grace, and how it stands to *the grace and the truth* that *came to be through Jesus Christ*, is verse 17's question. The reading adopted neither needs the law to be a grace nor denies it. Barth, Keener and Michaels allow the law its part in grace, Michaels most firmly: "The law is itself grace from God".[^lawgrace] The theology of grace in place of grace is gathered with the theology of the law and the truth in this commentary's account of what the passage teaches.

## The stages behind the text

Readings of a stage behind the text at verse 16 are received as questions about the Prologue's sources, and are declined at the text as it stands.
- **Schnackenburg's opening καί.** "But the original hymn may have had καί", he writes, and the evangelist the ὅτι.[^hymn]
- **Bultmann's.** He thinks it "unlikely that a conclusion can be reached" whether the verse first began with ὅτι or καί, though καί "would suit the style of the source better". If no line has been omitted, he thinks it reasonable to join the close of verse 14 to the opening of verse 16, "as does Burney". And he reads verse 17 as "an exegetical gloss on v. 16".[^bultmannsource]
- **Hirsch** takes the ὅτι for part of an editor's insertion.[^hirsch]
- **Black's Aramaic reading**, grace in place of shame, presupposes an Aramaic Prologue that is not shown.[^black]

Whether the Prologue grew from a hymn is a question for the volume's introduction, and it is not answered here.

How firmly each is held:
- **Ὅτι:** the ground of verse 14's confession, with the witness taken in: high.
- **The fullness:** the Son's fullness of grace and truth, received in share, the source undiminished: high.
- **Ἡμεῖς πάντες ἐλάβομεν:** the eyewitnesses, widened to the church; the aorist gathering one continuing receipt: high.
- **Καί:** introducing what was received: high on the shared function, the choice among its senses recorded and not forced.
- **Χάριν ἀντὶ χάριτος:** grace in place of grace, from the one fullness: high, not very high, because of the strength of the alternative heard in full.
