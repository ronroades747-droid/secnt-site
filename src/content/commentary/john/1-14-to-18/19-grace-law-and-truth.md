The confession's third claim is about what was received. [The fullness that verse 16 names](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/13-grace-in-place-of-grace/) and [the law and the grace and the truth that verse 17 sets side by side](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/14-law-grace-and-truth/) have been read for what they say: the fullness, who received, what grace in place of grace means, and the two members of verse 17 with their mediators and their verbs. This page does not weigh those words again. It asks what the church, on the strength of them, confesses about the grace received and its source.

The two verses give the confession its ground. Verse 16 grounds the confession of verse 14: the receiving is how "we" know him full, not what makes him full. Verse 17 grounds verse 16 in turn. The grace received is inexhaustible and his own, because the grace and the truth came to be through Jesus Christ. The claim has four parts, and each is held as firmly as the exegesis held it: a fullness never diminished; the grace received and the gift of the Spirit; the law, God's gift, and the grace and the truth, come in Christ; and Moses and Christ.

## A fullness never diminished

What "we have all received" is a share from the fullness of grace and truth that verse 14 named in the Father's only Son, [the glory beheld](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/18-dwelling-and-glory/) in the Word made flesh. The fullness is not lessened by the giving. The eyewitnesses who first said "we" are widened to the whole believing church, and the receiving that began when [*as many as received him*](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/14-the-turn-children-of-god/) has gone on since. Grace is given in place of grace, unceasingly, from the one fullness of the Son, and the supply does not fail. This is held with high confidence. That the first grace is the grace before it in the believers' own receiving, and not the grace of the law, was a close weighing, and the strongest alternative, that the law's grace is replaced by the gospel's, is [heard in full with the exegesis](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/13-grace-in-place-of-grace/) and declined there.

**The fountain.** Chrysostom and Cyril of Alexandria heard the verse so. For Chrysostom the Son is the fountain of every good, "and after the overflowing remaining full, in nothing diminished by supplying others".[^chrysostom] For Cyril, "from the fulness of the Son, as from a perennial fountain, the gift of the Divine graces springing forth comes to each soul that is found worthy to receive it", and the Son can bestow on all, "refusing diminution".[^cyril] What the Fathers set in the image of the fountain is what the verse says: the giving does not lessen the giver.

**The channel.** Calvin gives the fullness a Christological form. He joins those who read the verse of the graces poured on Christ: "For my own part, I agree with the opinion of those who say that we are watered with the *graces* which were poured out on Christ; for what we receive from Christ he does not bestow upon us as being God, but the Father communicated to him what would flow to us as through a channel."[^calvin] In his *Institutes* he sets the same thought beside the Spirit given to the Son without measure, at 3:34.[^institutes] The reading is recorded here as theologically true, and not as what the preposition says: ἀντί says *in place of*, and [the exegesis](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/13-grace-in-place-of-grace/) does not make Calvin's derivation its sense. It stands where it can stand, under the one fullness of the Son, from which the grace is given.

## The grace received, and the gift of the Spirit

What the grace is, beyond the grace of the Revealer, as Bultmann names it, the exegesis leaves open.[^revealer] A reading that hears the gift of the Spirit in it is recorded here, and not decided. Each who holds it is heard at his own strength.
- Michaels thinks the grace "probably refers to, or at least includes, the gift of the Spirit". He limits the reading himself: "the reality to which it points has more to do with revelation than with empowerment for ministry", and the focus of the text "is not on the Spirit as such".[^michaels89]
- Schnackenburg has believers receive their "superabundant gifts of salvation" from the incarnate Logos or the Lord returned to his glory "through the Holy Spirit". He holds that, to the evangelist's mind, χάρις "certainly does not differ essentially from πνεῦμα and ζωή", with the caution that the context allows no more to be affirmed.[^schnackenburg]
- Bavinck, without citing the verse, echoes it. The Holy Spirit "takes everything from Christ", and out of the Son's fullness "he communicates grace upon grace". The Spirit adds nothing to what Christ has done: his work, Bavinck writes, "is therefore nothing but an applicatory one".[^bavinck]

At verse 17 the reading meets the truth. Michaels reads Jesus' words to the Samaritan woman at 4:23–24 as identifying "Spirit" with "truth", "essentially with the 'grace and truth'" that came into being through Jesus Christ.[^michaels253]

The exegesis does not decide this, and this commentary does not either. The Gospel's later teaching on the Spirit may bear on what the grace is. It is recorded as a reading the Gospel's sequel may fill out, and nothing here is built on it.

## The law, God's gift; the grace and the truth, come in Christ

The law was a gift of God's grace. God gave it, and Moses mediated it. It was not *the* grace and *the* truth, which came to be, an event in history, through Jesus Christ. This is held with high confidence, and it is what verse 17 teaches: the law is God's gift, given in love, and it bore witness to the grace and the truth that came to be in the person of Jesus Christ.[^love]

**Two answers, to two questions.** The verse raises two questions, and the sources answer them differently. Whether the law was a gift of God's grace, nearly all say yes. Whether the law was the grace and the truth themselves, nearly all say no. [The exegesis](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/14-law-grace-and-truth/) hears both answers, and this commentary holds both. Two lines are set side by side.
- Among those who hold the law a gift of grace are Edwards, Carson, Michaels and Köstenberger. Michaels: "The law is itself grace from God, 'given through Moses' as a preparation for more and greater grace to come." Edwards: "the old covenant was still a gift of grace, that is a mark of God's gracious favour to his people."[^gift]
- Calvin holds that the law had neither grace nor truth, and still keeps its office. Of the evangelist's antithesis he writes, "his meaning is, that *the law* wanted both of them", and yet "it must not be supposed that anything was exhibited by *the Law* in a manner fitted to deceive". Augustine has the grace absent from the Old Testament, "because the law threatened but did not bring aid", and the law prepare "the way for that physician who was to come with grace and truth".[^office]

The two lines answer the two questions. That the law was a grace does not make it the first grace of verse 16, which came from the one fullness of the incarnate Son. Those who hold the law a gift of grace mostly take it so at verse 16 as well, and that conclusion is recorded as theirs. Carson reaches further still, and has the law and the grace and truth "alike" spring from the fullness of the Word; that is recorded, and not answered here.[^carson]

## Moses and Christ

The contrast is between the mediators and the verbs. The law "was given" through Moses; the grace and the truth "came to be" through Jesus Christ. The agency is the Son's, under the Father. What came through him is his own, because he is full of grace and truth. Moses passed on a gift received, and the Son is what he gives. This is held with high confidence.

**Real, and not polemic.** The contrast is real, and it is not polemic. Moses and his law are not set down as the enemies of grace. Barth: "In John Moses with his law is viewed favorably in his own place." He draws the result of the comparison with the same care: "The result is not that Christ is victorious in the competition but that there is no competition."[^barth] When grace and truth are set beside the law, and Jesus Christ beside Moses, what is seen is not a contest but two things of different orders.

**Not Paul's antithesis.** Nor is the contrast Paul's of law against grace, which the evangelist does not draw here. This is held with high confidence, on a weighing where the sources divide more sharply than anywhere in verse 16. Bultmann and Barrett read the verse in Paul's form, and [the exegesis](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/14-law-grace-and-truth/) records them and declines the form, while receiving the contrast they rightly see.

## What the confession hands on

The confession's third claim, then, is this. From the fullness of the only Son, never diminished, we have all received, grace in place of grace, and the supply does not fail. The grace received may be heard, by some, as including the gift of the Spirit; that is recorded, not decided. The law was God's gift, given through Moses, and it bore witness to the grace and the truth that came to be through Jesus Christ. Moses passed on what he received; the Son is what he gives. How firmly each is held:
- **The fullness**, the Son's, shared and never diminished: high. **Grace in place of grace**, from the one fullness, unceasingly: high, on a close weighing. **Calvin's channel:** recorded as theologically true, not as the sense of the preposition.
- **The grace and the gift of the Spirit:** recorded, not decided.
- **The law a gift of God's grace, and not the grace and the truth themselves:** high.
- **The mediators and the verbs**, the Son's agency under the Father, the grace and truth his own: high. **Not polemic:** high. **Not Paul's antithesis:** high, on a close weighing.
