The reader has now met every verb of these five verses, each in its own place: the Word who [became flesh](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/10-word-became-flesh/), the dwelling and the beholding of [his glory](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/11-glory-beheld/), [the witness who still speaks](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/12-witness-who-still-speaks/), [the fullness received](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/13-grace-in-place-of-grace/), [the law given and the grace and the truth come](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/14-law-grace-and-truth/), and [the only Son who has made God known](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/15-only-son-made-him-known/). [Before the verses were read one by one](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/04-temporal-payload/), this commentary set out what their tenses do together, as part of the passage's architecture. This page does the same work from the other end. With each verb now read where it stands, the verbs are gathered back onto one line, and the reading stated before the verses is stated again from the verbs up: **the incarnate life told in retrospect from its completion, broken twice — by a witness that still sounds and by a truth outside narrative time — and closed on the Son who abides with the Father.**

## One retrospect from the completed life

The passage marks time, and it marks it as one retrospect. Its aorists are spoken from this side of the incarnate life, and each gathers its span into one completed fact. The verse pages left the verbs this way:

- **The becoming.** The aorist of verse 14 names a single occurrence, and the dwelling and the beholding that follow it name prolonged action. Harris puts the three verbs side by side: "with ἐγένετο denoting a single occurrence (the incarnation = conception of Jesus Christ) and ἐσκήνωσεν and ἐθεασάμεθα referring to prolonged action."[^becoming]
- **The receiving.** The ἐλάβομεν of verse 16, *we have received*, is, in Harris's words, a constative aorist "viewing many successive receipts unitarily."[^receiving]
- **The making known.** The ἐξηγήσατο of verse 18, *has made him known*, is a constative aorist "encompassing in a single glance the whole span of Christ's earthly life including his death and resurrection."[^making-known]
- **The verb of becoming.** The verb that heads the passage occurs six times in the Prologue, and of all six Harris writes that "these uses of the aorist are all constative," whatever the action they name, whether momentary, prolonged or repeated.[^six]

The vantage is the witnesses'. Barth says it at verse 15, of the Baptist's "strangely retrospective" *this was he*: "We stand on this side of the epiphany."[^epiphany] Westcott titles the section that begins at verse 14 "The Incarnation as apprehended by personal experience," and at the break in verse 14 he has the evangelist pause, as he puts it, to look "as it were, from without upon the record and comments upon it."[^westcott-vantage] Michaels says of the beholding that it "is not a claim based on a single experience," but a testimony to the whole of Jesus' life on earth.[^michaels-whole] Keener, at verse 18, says that "the context confirms that this revelation coincides with his earthly life, while climaxing in the cross."[^keener] Schnackenburg agrees: those who speak in verse 14 are "the witnesses, in fact, of his work on earth."[^schnackenburg-witnesses] Meagher notes, as one step in an argument of his own, that by verse 14 the earthly ministry "has already been represented as a past event"; he does not himself adopt the reading of verse 14 as a retrospect.[^meagher]

No single verse settled the frame, but together the verses leave no other. The reading of verse 14 found the dwelling constative. The reading of verse 16 found the receiving constative. The reading of verse 17 found that the grace and the truth *came to be* as an event in history. And the reading of verse 18 found the making known constative. A frame is what those readings make when they are laid side by side, and it is held with very high confidence.

*The referent, not the frame.* The passage's movement into history is what the retrospect looks back on, not the place it looks from. Brown sees the contrast in the Prologue's opening and closing strophes: "Thus the eternal being of the Word in the opening strophe is contrasted to the temporal becoming of the Word in the last strophe." Carson finds that from verse 12 on "the rise in pace adds forward movement as the text with increasing explicitness drives deeper into the realm of history." Köstenberger finds that "the storyline seems to progress in linear fashion from 1:1 to 1:14 to 1:18."[^referent] They are right about what the passage refers to. But the later aorists do not step forward from verse 14, as though the story began there and moved on. They gather up what verse 14 began.

*The vantage widens, then yields.* The eyewitnesses of verse 14 widen at verse 16, where *we have all received*, to the church. At verse 18 they give way to declaration. Michaels marks the turn: the confessional "we" and "us" of verses 14 to 16 "now gives way to a tone of pure christological declaration not unlike that of the Gospel's opening verses."[^declaration]

*The denial of time, received and declined.* One serious reading holds that the Prologue marks no time at all. Barth, writing on verse 4, says: "Obviously no one time is here marked off from another." He writes it of verse 4, and this commentary extends it to the present passage, as it did [at verses 6 to 13](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/16-the-units-tenses/). Its observation, that the eternal and the historical are held together here, is the frame's own, and the two breaks carry it. Its conclusion, that no time is therefore marked, is declined on Barth's own pages. Of verse 14 he writes: "The Word was among us; it was where we are, in time. There is a place in time where its *skēnē* was erected, where he put up, where he found lodging."[^barth-time]

Dodd is sometimes counted with him, and he is not a denial. He reads the Prologue on "the philosophical conception of two orders of being, distinguished not by succession in time," and he sets that against eschatology, which "is cast in a temporal mould." On the same page the Christian revelation is affirmed in "a proposition which declares that in one single area of the universe of space and time" the eternal has been fully present, an area "co-extensive with the life, death and resurrection of Jesus Christ." And he makes Jesus' life the history of the incarnate Logos, lived "upon the stage of limited time."[^dodd]

## One becoming, its breadth explicated

The aorist of verse 14 names one becoming. Morris: "'Became' is in the aorist tense, and indicates action at a point of time."[^morris-point] Its breadth is what the first clause says together with the second, the dwelling explicating the becoming. Barth: "The egeneto signifies the epiphany, the concrete historical existence of the Word in all its breadth, just as the coming of the Baptist in v. 6 refers to his total appearance and not simply to the first moment of his activity."[^barth-breadth]

The breadth reading is Barth's. Barrett does not hold it. His suggestion that ἐγένετο is used "in the same sense as in v. 6," the Word coming on the human scene, is offered under "Perhaps," and it weighs what the verb means rather than how long its action spans. [The exegesis of verse 14](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/10-word-became-flesh/) declines it as a rendering. Barrett's own word for the aorist, at verse 1, is "punctiliar": he sets the punctiliar ἐγένετο of verses 3, 6 and 14 against the continuous tense of the Word who *was*.[^barrett]

This reading joins the one becoming and its breadth. Verse 6 has nothing like the second clause of verse 14, so its aorist carries the summary itself. Verse 14 has one, so its aorist need not. Here alone in the passage the shape of the text itself requires the reading. It is held with high confidence, the confidence of the reading of verse 14 on which it rests.

## The two breaks

Twice the passage steps out of its retrospect, and the two breaks are of different kinds.

**At verse 15: the witness in the reader's day.** The present μαρτυρεῖ, *bears witness*, and the perfect κέκραγεν, *has cried out*, set the Baptist's witness in the evangelist's own day, and so in the reader's, as [the reading of the witness](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/08-the-aperture/) set out. Ridderbos reads the two verbs as one expression: the present for the "enduring significance" of John's testimony, the perfect for "that word once for all spoken." Carson finds that the two tenses "combine to suggest" the evangelist is presenting the witness "both vividly, as if it were in progress, and comprehensively, summing it up as a set-piece." Calvin: by the present tense "he denotes a continued act."[^witness] Inside the witness, γέγονεν, *has come to rank*, names a rank that came to be and stands now. Westcott calls the saying "the argument which points to a present consequence (*is now come to be*) of an eternal truth (*He was before me*)."[^westcott-argument] This break is held with very high confidence.

**At verse 18: outside narrative time.** The perfect with πώποτε states that no one has ever seen God, and Harris notes that πώποτε "excludes any exceptions."[^ever] The participle states the Son's abiding with the Father. Harris: "the preceding ὁ ὤν probably depicts a supratemporal condition that had no beginning." Morris: "The only begotten is continually in the bosom of the Father."[^abiding] The passage's last verb, the aorist ἐξηγήσατο, closes on the completed revelation, set against that abiding. Harris: "Jesus Christ personally disclosed in time his own intimate, eternal knowledge of the Father."[^disclosed] This break is held with high confidence, the confidence of the reading of verse 18 on which it rests.

**Not one composed frame.** The two breaks do different work. The cry stands into the reader's day, and the seeing is denied for all time. Barth likens the two perfects in their significance, the perfect of verse 18 being "like the kekragen of v. 15" in that neither is part of a narrative, and he makes no claim that they were composed as a pair. Abbott's stage direction, "John is discovered testifying … and crying aloud," is said of verse 15 alone.[^not-composed] No source this commentary has read pairs the two breaks as one composed design.

One further observation is recorded here and left open. Together, the two breaks may hold the retrospect open at both ends of the reader's time: the witness now, the Son always. This commentary offers that as an argued observation, held tentatively, and does not build on it. Whether it holds across the Prologue is a question for the Prologue's introduction.

## The line of tenses at its close

The Prologue has a line of tenses that runs through all of it, set out [at its opening](/commentary/john/1-1-to-3/03-macro-tense-spine/): the Word who *was*, ἦν, set against what *came to be*, ἐγένετο. The passage brings that line to its close.

- **Verse 14's ἐγένετο against verse 1's ἦν.** This is the line's contrast at its height, and it confirms the reading of the line this commentary gave at the Prologue's opening. Barrett: "The continuous tense is to be contrasted with the punctiliar ἐγένετο of v. 3 (creation), v. 6 (the appearance of the Baptist), and v. 14 (the incarnation)." Wallace states the same mirror in the grammar: θεός was the Word's nature from eternity, "hence, εἰμί is used," and σάρξ was added at the incarnation, "hence, γίνομαι is used." Brown sets verse 1's "was" against verse 14's "became," and Schnackenburg too finds the historical event "brought out by the ἐγένετο which follows the series of ἦν," though he goes on to call it a different becoming from that of verse 6 and verse 3.[^line-contrast] This is held with very high confidence.
- **Verse 17's ἐγένετο.** The grace and the truth *came to be*. This commentary judges that the verb names the same historical event as verse 14, at its gift, and that judgment is held with high confidence. Barth: "the egeneto has this historical sense here as in v. 6, although naturally we have to think also of the great principial egeneto of v. 14." Edwards suggests that the word "may well have been chosen deliberately to echo v. 14." Michaels sets the verb beside its uses at verses 3, 6, 10 and 14, and ties it more specifically to verses 3 and 10, as Sosa Siliezar ties it to verse 3.[^seventeen]
- **Verse 18's ὁ ὤν.** It answers verse 1's ἦν in what it says. Harris: "1:18 makes the same three affirmations of Jesus Christ as 1:1 does of the Logos": eternal existence, intimate relationship with God, and deity.[^three] But it is not an instance of the line's contrast, because no ἐγένετο stands against it in its clause. Its correspondence with verse 1 belongs to [the frame the two verses close](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/05-frame-completed/). This is held with high confidence.
- **Verse 15's γέγονεν.** It is a perfect of the line's own verb, followed by ἦν. It names a resulting state within the Baptist's saying, which its ἦν grounds, and it is not on the line. That is this commentary's judgment. Its earlier description of the line called the perfect of verses 3 and 4 the line's unique bridge, and that description is scoped to the work the perfect does there; it moves nothing, and verse 15's perfect does not compete with it. Barth has the speaker treat the rank as already settled: "This saying points to a fact which the speaker recognized as already complete when he spoke." Zerwick reads the perfect as said of the Word as man.[^fifteen] None of the commentators read for this commentary calls the perfect of verses 3 and 4 unique, or reads the perfect of verse 15 as a bridge.
- **The questions left open about the line are answered.** Within these five verses the only ἦν are the two of verse 15, and the reading of that verse took up both. *He was before me* confirms the line at its place. Barth reads the retrospective *this was he* as "his historical appearance considered as a whole," and within this passage that construal is said of that ἦν alone, not of *he was before me*. Barth himself sets it beside the ἦν of verses 9 and 10, which [the reading of verses 6 to 13](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/16-the-units-tenses/) engaged.[^this-was] No question remains open among the commentators read directly about what the line's terms carry, and the line is untouched.

As the line ran through [verses 4 and 5](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/03-macro-tense-run/) and verses 6 to 13, it runs through these verses to its close.

## Where the passage ends

The Prologue's movement toward its end point within this volume ends at the beholding, as [the reading of verse 14](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/11-glory-beheld/) found. Gathered from the verbs, that end point has a tense-form: a single becoming that conditions a prolonged beholding, each gathered by the constative as one completed fact. McHugh suggests that the verb of beholding may be translated "we beheld, for a time, for ourselves." Ridderbos: "here as well we must think of the continuous glory in the earthly life of Jesus." Westcott and Lincoln hold that the glory was there throughout the life: Westcott, that the Gospel represents the whole human life of Christ "as a continuous and conscious manifestation of divine glory"; Lincoln, that "for those with the eyes of faith, there was glory all the way through Jesus' life."[^throughout]

The end point reaches further than its first witnesses, and what carries the reach is the present of verse 15 and the *we have all received* of verse 16. Bultmann has the beholding "renewed again and again," and Dodd has the seeing of the glory "just as possible, and just as necessary, for us as for those who saw Him in the flesh."[^reach] The reach is not verse 14's own tense-form, because this commentary reads the "we" of verse 14 as the eyewitnesses. Bultmann and Dodd read that "we" more widely, and that is where this commentary parts from them.

*A particular moment, declined.* The Transfiguration is not the beholding. The constative of prolonged action excludes it. Brown, who grants that there is much to recommend hearing an echo of it, judges in the end that "it remains no more than a possibility that the Johannine writers knew of the Transfiguration scene."[^transfiguration] The fullness of that perception comes at the hour, which is the Gospel's end point and not the Prologue's, and this commentary keeps the two end points distinct.

*Across the Prologue.* With this passage the account of the Prologue's time is complete. [Verses 4 and 5](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/03-macro-tense-run/) found the ground laid before time and the light that is shining now. [Verses 6 to 13](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/16-the-units-tenses/) found an advent told in retrospect, with its door left open at verse 12. These five verses find a beholding, once begun and gathered whole, of a life now past. That beholding is held open by a witness that still sounds and by a receiving the church still has. It is closed by a making known that is complete: of the God no one has ever seen, by the Son who is still in the bosom of the Father.

## The aspect of the dwelling, confirmed

The reading of the whole passage confirms [the reading of verse 14](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/11-glory-beheld/) that the dwelling is constative, and it neither deepens that reading nor moves it. Zerwick weighs the inceptive aorist, *took up his abode*, against the constative, *dwelt among us*, and favours the constative by the verb that follows: "The latter favoured by the following ἐ-θεασάμεθα." Harris reads it the same way. The verb "may be ingressive," he grants, but it "is more probably constative," like the becoming and the beholding.[^dwelling] Robertson reads it as ingressive, and that reading is recorded; the aspect of the clause is not reopened.

That is the time the passage is told in. What the church confesses on its strength, about the Word become flesh, the glory beheld, the grace received, and God made known, is the work of the sections that follow.
