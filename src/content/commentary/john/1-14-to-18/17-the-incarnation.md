The exegesis is finished, and the theology begins where the confession begins. [The clause *And the Word became flesh*](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/10-word-became-flesh/) has been read for what it says: what σάρξ names, what the becoming asserts and excludes, and how the church has received it. This page does not return to the clause to press it further. It asks what the church, on the strength of it, confesses about the one who became flesh.

The confession's first claim is about the subject of the becoming. The one who became flesh is the Word who was with God and [was God](/commentary/john/1-1-to-3/16-full-deity/). He remained the Word, and he came to be what he was not. Three things follow from that, and each is held as firmly as the exegesis held it.

## One subject, without change, disguise or mixture

The clause asserts one subject. The Word was not changed into flesh, he did not wear flesh as a disguise, and his deity and his humanity were not mixed into something that is neither. This is the clause's own assertion, and it is held with very high confidence.

**The doctrine of the two natures.** The church's doctrine of the two natures in one person is the historically specific articulation of that claim. It is engaged here at full weight, and it is not fixed as the clause's own assertion. Those who stand in the church's reception say so in their own measure:
- Schnackenburg grants that in John's own perspective the two natures "do not stand out". What the verse depicts is a sequence in the history of salvation. Yet the doctrine, he holds, "is comprised therein, in germ, and can be developed legitimately from 1:14 in the metaphysical thinking of the Greek Fathers."[^germ]
- Calvin draws the unity of the person from the verb itself: "When he tells us that *the Speech was made flesh*, we clearly infer from this the unity of his Person". And he holds with it that the unity of the person does not hinder the two natures from remaining distinct.[^calvin]
- Westcott sets out what "must be held as expressed in the words when they are fairly interpreted". Among those truths, the Lord's human and divine natures "remained without change, each fulfilling its part according to its proper laws", and they "were united in one Person". He adds at once that "the attempt to express the Truth with precision is beset with difficulty and even with peril."[^westcott]
- Bavinck states the doctrine from the whole of Scripture: "Scripture knows only one person, one subject, one Christ, yet ascribes to him two kinds of attributes, divine and human." A Christ formed by mixing the two natures would be, in his words, "a “third kind” of being", and could not be the mediator between God and humankind.[^bavinck]
- Cyril denies that the nature of the Word was changed and became flesh. In his own exposition of this verse he gives the next clause the same work. The Word dwelt among us so that, in Cyril's words, "you might not suppose that He is transformed into flesh, but rather that He dwelt in *Flesh* …"[^cyril]

**What the verse supplies, and what it does not.** Westcott holds those truths to be expressed in the clause's words, fairly interpreted. This commentary engages them at that weight, and does not make the doctrine of the two natures the verse's lexical content. The verse says of one subject that he was God and became flesh. The doctrine is the church's way of saying, in the terms of a later debate, what that one subject is. The ground the verse itself gives lies in its match with the first verse. Wallace states it in the grammar: "The grammatical structure of these two statements mirrors each other; both emphasize the nature of the Word, rather than his identity." *The Word was God*, and *the Word became flesh*: what he was from eternity, and what he came to be.[^mirror]

## Flesh, at its most exposed

The Word became σάρξ, the whole human being in creaturely weakness before God. This is not sinful flesh. It is held with very high confidence.

The weight of the word is theological. John could have said that the Word became a man. He used the harshest available term for humanity before God, and so the Word entered human existence at its most exposed. Westcott names that side of it: humanity "from the side of its weakness and dependence and mortality".[^exposed] 
That is not to say that he became a sinner. Bavinck draws the line exactly. The texts that some have pressed the other way, this one among them, "say only that the Son of God assumed a weak nature subject to suffering and death but not that he himself was a sinner in a subjective sense."[^sinless] The flesh he became is mortal and weak, and it is not sinful.

**The flesh of verse 13.** The flesh that cannot generate the life of God at verse 13 is the flesh the Word became at verse 14. Those who believe were [*begotten — not of bloods, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of a husband — but of God*](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/20-word-gives-god-begets/). Keener draws the two verses together. He asks, "How was it possible for humans to be “born from God”?", and answers: "The chasm was unbridgeable from the human side; but God’s divine Word became flesh in 1:14." On his reading, the incarnation is what makes that begetting possible.[^keener] The clause itself fixes the fact of the enfleshment, not the timing of the begetting. So the children of God of verse 12 are not confined to the time after the Word's arrival in the flesh.

## The fact, not the manner; the reality, against docetism

The clause states that the Word became flesh. It does not say how.

**The virginal conception — recorded, not fixed.** The church has heard the clause to presuppose the virginal conception, and that reading is recorded here. Westcott holds it in his own voice: "the fact of the miraculous Conception, though not stated, is necessarily implied by the Evangelist." Bultmann denies it of the Gospel as a whole. The Fourth Gospel, he writes, "not only does not contain the idea of the virgin birth, but (like Paul) excludes it", and the evangelist "knew or wished to know nothing of the legend of the Virgin Birth."[^conception] The text's own silence is kept. The commentary does not supply what the clause does not say.

**Docetism — excluded.** What the clause does exclude is any Christ who only seemed human. This is held with very high confidence. Lincoln says it of the Prologue: "It leaves no room for docetism, the notion that Jesus only appeared to be human or simply took on the outward appearance of a human."[^docetism]

**Written against docetists? — probable, not certain.** That the clause was written against docetists is a further claim. It is held as probable and not certain, with moderate confidence, on the grounds [the exegesis set out](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/10-word-became-flesh/).

## What the confession hands on

The confession's first claim, then, is this: one subject, the Word, who became flesh without ceasing to be what he was. He became flesh in its full mortal weakness and without sin. He became flesh in fact, and not in appearance. How firmly each is held:
- **One subject**, without change, disguise or mixture: very high. **The two natures in one person:** the church's articulation, engaged at full weight, not fixed as the clause's assertion.
- **Σάρξ** as the whole human being in creaturely weakness, not sinful flesh, and the same flesh as at verse 13: very high.
- **The virginal conception:** recorded. **Docetism:** excluded, very high. **An anti-docetic intent:** probable, not certain, held with moderate confidence.

The confession's second claim follows from the first. The Word who became flesh dwelt among us, and we beheld his glory.
