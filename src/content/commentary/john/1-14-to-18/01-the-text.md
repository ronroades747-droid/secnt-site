The exegesis of this unit proceeds on a fixed text, and this first section establishes it. The unit is read on the critical editions' common text, adopted without emendation, for verses 14 through 18 alike. Its agreement with the editions has been checked at first hand: the printed text of the standard critical edition agrees with it word for word.[^na28] What remains is to show how that text was secured, and the work falls at three levels, which are kept distinct throughout. Three points are **resolved** against a named rival: the Baptist's formula at verse 15, the connective at the head of verse 16, and the crux at verse 18, which is decided on two questions, the noun and the article. The rest is **secure**, and is asserted verse by verse. And the questions of meaning that the variants raise are **routed** to the verses where they belong, and are not decided here.

The evidence is gathered in layers, and each layer is counted once. The standard critical edition, the United Bible Societies' apparatus with its companion textual commentary, and Metzger's *Textual Commentary* stand in one tradition of editing, and they are weighed as one witness, not three. Gregory of Nyssa's text of John is read on an original collation of his writings (Roades, 1974), which is preferred to the later collation by Brooks (1991) wherever the two differ. Origen is read in English translation, and the Greek of his lemmata is reached only through those who cite it.[^layers]

## Verse 15: "This was he of whom I said"

The Baptist names Jesus as the one he had spoken of: οὗτος ἦν ὃν εἶπον, *this was he of whom I said*. The rival reading, οὗτος ἦν ὁ εἰπών, *this was he who said*, would have the evangelist reintroduce John by recalling a known saying of his. This commentary reads ὃν εἶπον and holds the rival to be secondary, with high confidence.

**The documents.** ὃν εἶπον has both Bodmer papyri, 𝔓⁶⁶ and 𝔓⁷⁵, with Codex Sinaiticus (א) and Codex Vaticanus (B) as corrected, Codex Bezae (D), Θ, the later manuscripts and the versions. ὁ εἰπών is ancient. Origen cites it twice in his *Commentary on John*, and the first hands of Vaticanus and of Codex Ephraemi (C) read it, as does an early corrector of Sinaiticus.[^documents15] But once both papyri stand on the other side, that antiquity no longer carries the Alexandrian tradition with it. The reading's own modern advocate grants the point. Ramsey Michaels, who prefers ὁ εἰπών, the text Westcott and Hort printed, concedes that the support for ὃν εἶπον is more widespread and diversified, and that it includes equally strong Alexandrian support, above all in the two papyri.[^michaels]

**The editions.** The United Bible Societies' text rated ὃν εἶπον at its highest level of certainty in its third edition, and removed the variation unit from its apparatus at the fourth; the sixth edition records none. Only Westcott and Hort printed ὁ εἰπών, and Westcott's own commentary renders ὃν εἶπον.[^editions15]

**The scribes.** Here the evidence is divided, and it is stated as divided. Schnackenburg names a motive for ὁ εἰπών: the desire to avoid the difficulty the ἦν creates. McHugh and Barrett read ὁ εἰπών as a correction of the harder text. Michaels names a motive in the other direction, for ὃν εἶπον: a scribe would have conformed the verse to the Baptist's saying at verse 30, οὗτός ἐστιν ὑπὲρ οὗ ἐγὼ εἶπον. His second point, that a Baptist speaking in the present would have said ἐστίν, *is*, and not ἦν, *was*, is answered by the same three: the imperfect is the right tense for a witness looking back to what he had said before. His first point stands unanswered, as a live motive running the other way.[^scribes15] The case for ὃν εἶπον is therefore not rested on scribal habit. It rests on the documents and the editions.

**The commentators.** Barrett, Schnackenburg, McHugh and Westcott adopt ὃν εἶπον. Morris, Carson, Harris and Zerwick print and construe it without a note.[^commentators15] Gregory of Nyssa does not reach the reading at all: he cites only the verse's last words, πρῶτός μου ἦν, *he was before me*. The collation's appendix of Gregory's distinctive readings lists one at "1.15"; it is a slip for 1:5, where the reading it records belongs.[^gregory15]

**Its force.** The reading is held with high confidence, and not with very high confidence, for three reasons:
- the evidence of scribal habit is divided;
- ὁ εἰπών has third-century attestation in Origen, as old as any manuscript that carries it;
- a major commentator with a published study of the variant prefers it.

That study is Michaels's of 1981, on Origen and the text of this verse. It is not consulted here directly; its argument is known through the notes of his commentary, which state his preference and refer to it.[^study] The option of locking a working text without deciding between the original and the secondary reading was considered and declined, because the papyri support a decision.

## The head of verse 16: "for"

Verse 16 opens with ὅτι, *for*, and the rival καί, *and*, is secondary. The reading is held with very high confidence.

**The documents.** ὅτι has both papyri with Sinaiticus, Vaticanus, the first hand of Ephraemi, Bezae, L, 33, the Old Latin and the Coptic: the earliest Alexandrian and Western witnesses together. καί's witnesses — Codex Alexandrinus (A), W, Θ, the later majority, the Latin Vulgate and the Syriac — are all later than both papyri.[^documents16] McHugh's verdict is that "no modern editor places καί in the text," and Morris judges that "the attestation of 'For' is superior."[^verdicts16]

**The scribes.** The case against ὅτι survives only as a hypothesis of early correction. It supposes that the evangelist wrote καί, and that it was corrected to ὅτι once the Baptist was heard as the speaker of verses 16 and following. Such a correction would have to stand before both papyri, and be carried into the Western text and the Old Latin, which share ὅτι. And its exegetical engine fails at its own source. Origen, who holds the Baptist to be the speaker, reads "Because" at verse 16, so that reading of the speaker does not need καί.[^origen16] In the other direction, Westcott and McHugh derive καί from ὅτι. For Westcott it is the scribe's smoothing, once the verse was read as the Baptist's. For McHugh, copyists who found ὅτι awkward preferred καί, which made it easy to read the verse as the Baptist's.[^smoothing]

**The dissent.** Raymond Brown leans to καί, and Bultmann withholds a verdict. What their dissent rests on is a claim about the Prologue's sources: καί, for Bultmann, would suit the style of the source, and Brown's lean belongs with his reading of verse 16 as part of the hymn. That claim belongs to the study of the Prologue's sources, and it is not weighed against the transmitted text.[^dissent16]

**Its force.** The difference from verse 15 is deliberate. There, scribal motives can be named in both directions; here, only one survives. The reading is held with very high confidence.

## Who speaks verses 16–18

The two readings settle less than they may seem to. ὃν εἶπον keeps the Baptist as the speaker of verse 15's formula, and that excludes Michaels's reading of verse 15 as the evangelist's own digression, which needed ὁ εἰπών. But ὅτι at verse 16 is compatible with either speaker. Origen reads it and has the Baptist speak on through verse 18; Westcott reads it and has the evangelist speak from verse 16.[^speaker] So the text does not decide where the Baptist's words end. The question is decided in the treatment of verse 15: his words end there, and verses 16 to 18 are the evangelist's. The working text's punctuation records that decision.

## The crux at verse 18: μονογενὴς θεός

This is the unit's crux. The clause names the one who has made God known, and the question is on two axes: whether the noun is θεός, *God*, or υἱός, *Son*; and whether the article stands before it. This commentary reads μονογενὴς θεός, without the article, and on each axis it declines to leave the question open.

### The noun

**The documents.** Both early papyri read θεός, 𝔓⁶⁶ without the article and 𝔓⁷⁵ with it, and so do the first hands of Sinaiticus and Ephraemi, Vaticanus and L. In Metzger's judgment the papyri's acquisition has much strengthened the reading's external support. υἱός, read by most manuscripts from the fourth century on, by the Latin and by the Curetonian Syriac, has no early Greek witness.[^documents18] Westcott put it plainly: "There is no ancient Greek authority for the reading, the only-begotten Son."[^westcott-authority] Gregory of Nyssa's citations reach the noun, and both modern collations of his text agree that he read θεός. Origen's lemmata, in translation, read "God" at the noun.[^fathers18] The Fathers as a whole divide, as the *Ancient Christian Commentary* notes, even within the writings of a single author.[^accs] The breadth of υἱός is real. It is later, and it is weighed as later.

**The scribes.** Three grounds converge for θεός.
1. **The easier reading has a named source.** υἱός is explained by assimilation to the Gospel's and the First Epistle's ὁ μονογενὴς υἱός (John 3:16, 18; 1 John 4:9), and the unfamiliar θεός is the reading a scribe would change. So Metzger, Houghton and Michaels.
2. **The papyri date any change early.** An error of the eye might run either way, but the papyri place any change from υἱός to θεός before the year 200, as Harris shows.
3. **The article falls out of the υἱός hypothesis.** Westcott: the substitution of God for Son "does not explain the omission of the article," while the substitution of Son for God would naturally carry the article with it.[^scribes18]

The mechanisms the advocates of υἱός offer are an error in dictation (Bultmann) and a slip between the abbreviations ΥϹ and ΘϹ (Wikgren). Each must operate before 200, and must also explain the missing article, and neither does both.[^mechanisms]

**The editions divide, and so does the reading of the context.** The Nestle-Aland and United Bible Societies' texts, the SBL Greek New Testament, and Westcott and Hort print θεός. The Tyndale House Greek New Testament prints υἱός. Within the tradition of the United Bible Societies' editions, Allen Wikgren's dissent is recorded in Metzger's *Commentary*: "At least a {D} decision would be preferable."[^editions18] The case against θεός is made from the context, and it depends on how the clause is read. Bultmann holds θεός indefensible, because it sits ill with the preceding Θεόν and cannot carry the apposition ὁ ὤν. Ridderbos follows him on his ground. Barrett finds the following clause calling for υἱός, while allowing that this may make it the easier reading. Harris states the division in terms: "the greater the weight given to considerations of intrinsic probability, the more evenly matched do the two main readings become."[^intrinsic] This commentary does not claim the argument from context. What divides it is how the clause is construed, and that belongs to the treatment of verse 18. The reading rests on the documents and the scribes.

**The commentators.** Westcott, Carson, Michaels, Köstenberger, Lincoln, Morris and Harris adopt θεός. Harris: "A strong preference may therefore be expressed for μονογενής θεός as the primitive text." Barrett leans to it, and Brown follows it in his translation and his comment without a stated verdict. Bultmann and Ridderbos hold υἱός, on one line of argument. McHugh prefers ὁ μονογενής alone, *the only one*. His case derives both rivals from the short reading, but it cannot derive θεός, and the short reading is too thinly attested to be the text. Schnackenburg leans both ways on one page, and is counted in neither column.[^commentators18]

**Its force.** The noun is held with high confidence, and four things keep it below very high confidence:
- the editions divide;
- the tradition of the United Bible Societies' editions carries Wikgren's dissent;
- the argument from context is divided among major commentators;
- a major commentator prefers a third reading.

It is not held lower, because the documents and the scribes converge, and every major commentator's case against θεός depends on how the clause is construed. The option of leaving the noun undecided was considered and declined: it presupposes that the editions agree, and they do not, and the evidence supports a decision.

### The article

Here the documents divide at the earliest stratum.
- *For the anarthrous reading,* μονογενὴς θεός: 𝔓⁶⁶, the first hand of Sinaiticus, Vaticanus, the first hand of Ephraemi, and L.
- *For the articular reading,* ὁ μονογενὴς θεός: 𝔓⁷⁵, with 33 and a corrector of Sinaiticus.
- *The Fathers lean to the article.* McHugh: ὁ μονογενὴς θεός, "with the article, is much better attested among the early Fathers." Gregory's citations of the clause carry the article throughout. Origen stands on both sides.[^article-documents]

The scribes favour the anarthrous reading. The article is easily added, and nothing explains its loss. Metzger: "There is no reason why the article should have been deleted, and when υἱός supplanted θεός it would certainly have been added." The articular reading is explicable as a combination of μονογενὴς θεός with ὁ μονογενὴς υἱός (Westcott; Harris). And the want of the article is what makes the reading hardest. Houghton: "The most difficult reading is μονογενὴς θεός by itself, both for the lack of the definite article and the unexpected juxtaposition of these terms." McHugh asks the converse question, why an article would be inserted into an anarthrous text, and he is answered by the same considerations: combination, and the phrase's use as a title.[^article-scribes]

What Gregory adds is that the articular text stood in a fourth-century Greek New Testament and was widespread in use. He does not show that it is earlier than 𝔓⁶⁶'s text, or explain a loss of the article. His witness strengthens the articular reading's external case without reversing the direction of scribal change. The article's case from context, that it would blur verse 1's distinction between ὁ θεός and θεός, depends on how verse 1 is read and on a claim about John's usage.

The anarthrous reading is held with high confidence, provisionally, below the noun. The two earliest papyri divide, and the weight of the Fathers, Gregory's included, runs to the article. The option of leaving the article undecided was considered here too, and declined. Its premise holds, since the editions agree on the anarthrous text, but the evidence still decides: the article's addition is explained, and its loss is not.

## The rest of the text

The rest of the text is held secure, verse by verse, with very high confidence. The critical edition, the United Bible Societies' apparatus and its companion commentary, the Evangelical Exegetical Commentary, the major commentaries read directly and the Nyssen witness all support it.[^sweep] The lesser readings are each recorded, and none is raised.
- **Verse 14.** πλήρη for πλήρης, in Bezae, the Old Latin b and c, and one manuscript of Gregory, is an early witness to reading the adjective with "glory". It bears on meaning and not on the text, and it is taken up in the treatment of verse 14, where the text's πλήρης is read. Meagher's conjecture of πνεῦμα, *spirit*, for σάρξ has no manuscript support, as he himself grants, and belongs to the study of the Prologue's sources.[^v14]
- **Verse 15.** The first hand of Sinaiticus rewrites the verse. It omits ὃν εἶπον, alone among the witnesses, and inserts the relative ὅς, with W's supplement and the Old Latin c. ὑμῖν, *to you*, is inserted after εἶπον in Bezae, W's supplement, X and others. Both are recorded, and neither is raised.[^v15]
- **Verse 17.** χάρις δέ, *but grace* (𝔓⁶⁶, the Old Latin, the Harklean Syriac, the Bohairic), is recorded, and δέ is not text. What weight it lends to reading the verse as a contrast or as a continuity is a question of meaning, and it is taken up in the treatment of verses 16 and 17.[^v17]
- **Verse 18.** These are recorded and not raised:
  - the omission of ὁ ὤν, *who is*, in Sinaiticus and the Old Latin a;
  - ἐκ τοῦ κόλπου, *from the bosom*, in the Curetonian Syriac, a reading of the versions;
  - Blass's conjecture ἐκ τῶν κόλπων, which no Greek manuscript carries;
  - ἡμῖν, *to us*, after ἐξηγήσατο, in W, the Old Latin c and the Curetonian.[^v18]

  Gregory's ἐν τοῖς κόλποις, *in the bosom*, is recorded on the 1974 collation as a reading peculiar to Gregory. Brooks dissents: he grants that Gregory writes it in every quotation, but judges it most unlikely that Gregory's New Testament did.[^kolpois]

## What the text leaves to the exegesis

The working text decides no question of meaning. At verse 18 the gloss stays neutral, "only God" or "only-begotten God", and the critical edition's punctuation within the clause is left standing. Whether μονογενὴς θεός is a noun in apposition or an adjective with its noun, and how the clause is to be punctuated, are questions for the treatment of verse 18. The sense of μονογενής belongs to verses 14 and 18. So do κόλπος and ἐξηγήσατο as matters of sense, and the readings that add εἰ μή, *except*, as evidence of how the verse was received. Claims about earlier stages behind the text at verses 16 and 18 belong to the study of the Prologue's sources.

With the working text fixed — ὃν εἶπον at verse 15, ὅτι at the head of verse 16, μονογενὴς θεός without the article at verse 18, and the rest secure — the exegesis can turn to what the evangelist has built on it: first the architecture of the unit, and then its five verses in turn.
