The unit is John 1:14–18, read on the working text this commentary has settled: the critical editions' common text, adopted without emendation, with the close of the Baptist's speech fixed at the end of verse 15 and the Nestle-Aland edition's punctuation within verse 18b left standing:[^text]

> ¹⁴ Καὶ ὁ λόγος σὰρξ ἐγένετο καὶ ἐσκήνωσεν ἐν ἡμῖν, καὶ ἐθεασάμεθα τὴν δόξαν αὐτοῦ, δόξαν ὡς μονογενοῦς παρὰ πατρός, πλήρης χάριτος καὶ ἀληθείας. ¹⁵ Ἰωάννης μαρτυρεῖ περὶ αὐτοῦ καὶ κέκραγεν λέγων· οὗτος ἦν ὃν εἶπον· ὁ ὀπίσω μου ἐρχόμενος ἔμπροσθέν μου γέγονεν, ὅτι πρῶτός μου ἦν. ¹⁶ ὅτι ἐκ τοῦ πληρώματος αὐτοῦ ἡμεῖς πάντες ἐλάβομεν καὶ χάριν ἀντὶ χάριτος· ¹⁷ ὅτι ὁ νόμος διὰ Μωϋσέως ἐδόθη, ἡ χάρις καὶ ἡ ἀλήθεια διὰ Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ ἐγένετο. ¹⁸ Θεὸν οὐδεὶς ἑώρακεν πώποτε· μονογενὴς θεὸς ὁ ὢν εἰς τὸν κόλπον τοῦ πατρὸς ἐκεῖνος ἐξηγήσατο.
>
> ¹⁴ And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we beheld his glory, glory as of the only one from the Father, full of grace and truth. ¹⁵ John bears witness about him, and has cried out, saying, "This was he of whom I said, 'He who comes after me has come to rank before me, because he was before me.'" ¹⁶ For from his fullness we have all received — grace in place of grace. ¹⁷ For the law was given through Moses; the grace and the truth came to be through Jesus Christ. ¹⁸ No one has ever seen God. The only Son, himself God, who is in the bosom of the Father — he has made him known.

The English for verses 16 and 18 is the rendering the commentary settled at those verses, taken verbatim. The English for verses 14, 15 and 17 is composed from the readings settled there, and each choice is placed here so the reader knows where it is carried. "Became" renders ἐγένετο at its plain sense with a predicate: the Word, remaining the Word, came to be what he was not, and neither changed into flesh nor only appeared in it (see the treatment of verse 14a). "Dwelt among us," and not "tabernacled," keeps the verb's evocation of God's dwelling with his people in the exegesis, where its range is argued, rather than in a typology the English would fix. "Glory as of the only one from the Father" takes ὡς as defining, not comparing: the glory is the only Son's own, not like his. "Full of grace and truth" describes the one named in the genitive, the Father's only Son, and not the glory, nor the Word as the sentence's subject (see the treatment of verse 14b–d). In verse 15 the present "bears witness" and the perfect "has cried out" keep the tenses that set the Baptist's witness in the reader's own day; "has come to rank before me" renders ἔμπροσθέν μου γέγονεν as rank that came to be, and "because he was before me" renders πρῶτός μου ἦν as priority in time, the Word's being before the Baptist (see the treatment of verse 15). The closing quotation mark after verse 15 records how the commentary settled a question the treatment of the text held open, the extent of the Baptist's speech: his words end there, and verses 16–18 are the evangelist's. In verse 17, "for" carries the causal ὅτι, the articles of "the grace and the truth" take up verse 14's pair, and "came to be" keeps ἐγένετο as an event in history set against the law's ἐδόθη (see the treatment of verse 17). The semicolon joins two balanced members without the "but" the Greek does not have. In verse 18, the edition's unpunctuated μονογενὴς θεός stands, and the apposition the rendering carries, *the only Son, himself God*, is the construal set out in the treatment of verse 18 and in the text-critical notes.

The text is the editions' common text, and the text-critical notes set out how it was secured. In verses 14–17 two readings are resolved: ὃν εἶπον at 1:15, held with high confidence, against the ὁ εἰπών of Origen and the first hands of Codex Vaticanus and Codex Ephraemi; and ὅτι at the head of verse 16, held with very high confidence, against καί. The rest is secure, with πλήρη at verse 14, δέ at verse 17 and two singular readings at verse 15 recorded at their weight and none raised. At 1:18, the unit's crux, two questions are resolved, and in each the case for leaving them evenly balanced is declined: the noun, θεός against υἱός, held with high confidence; and the article, the reading without it against the reading with it, held with high but provisional confidence.

The unit opens where the Word is named again and closes where the Prologue closes, and the evangelist built both edges. The front edge is the seam the treatment of [John 1:6–13](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/) set after verse 13, and the text marks it four times over: a καί after asyndeton; ὁ λόγος, named for the first time since verse 1; μονογενής and πατήρ, set at verse 14 and set again at verse 18, terms which in Barth's observation "seem to combine the last five verses of the prologue into a material unity"; and the first-person plural, for "In vv. 14 and 16 there is a switch to the first person plural," as Lincoln puts it. The back edge is the end of the Prologue. Between the two stands one section, in the sources' own voice as in Ridderbos's: "In vs. 14 the third and last segment of the three-part cycle begins, and at the end (vs. 18) everything returns to the beginning."[^edges]

Inside its edges the unit is one movement in three parts. The first is the confession of verse 14: the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we beheld his glory. The second is the witness of verse 15, which attests the confession. The third is the ground and the declaration of verses 16–18: two ὅτι give the ground, and verse 18, asyndetic, declares what they ground and crowns the movement. Westcott drew the same three parts: "St John gives first the substance of the apostolic witness (v. 14); and then the witness of prophecy, represented by the Baptist (v. 15); and thirdly, a general account of the nature of the revelation (vv. 16–18)."[^parts] The catchwords span the witness. What verse 14 says the Son is full of, verses 16–17 say was received and came, so that the thought runs across verse 15 from verse 14 to verse 16; and the chain the exegesis closes one link at a time, verse 17 grounding verse 16 and verse 16 grounding verse 14, is the movement's spine. Read for its content, the three-part movement is the spine of this commentary's exegesis and of its lectures: *confession → witness → ground and declaration*. One discrimination governs throughout and is stated here so it need not be re-argued at each verse: the unit is **deliberate composition, not deliberate verse**. The seams that source-critical readings draw after verse 14 and after verse 16 are received as real, and they fall where the three parts divide; the source-critical explanation of them, insertion into an earlier hymn, is declined under the discipline this commentary keeps of receiving an observation while declining the explanation offered for it.

## Why This Passage Matters

This is the Prologue's summit and its close. The first movement said who the Word is: with God and God, the one through whom all things came to be, the life that was the light of men. The second told of his coming to the world and to his own, and of those who received him. These five verses say what the first two withheld: that the Word became flesh. The verb of becoming, which the Prologue has used of the creation (verse 3) and of the man sent from God (verse 6), is applied to the Word himself, and the subject does not change. He dwelt among us; and those who saw him beheld his glory, God's own presence and character made manifest in him. The passage matters, first, because the Christian confession of the incarnation has its classic text here. The clause asserts one subject who remains the Word and came to be flesh, without change, disguise or mixture, and the church's two-natures doctrine is engaged as that claim's historically specific articulation (see the treatment of verse 14a, and the first of the theological loci).

It matters, second, because here the Prologue names what it has described. The Father and the Son are named for the first time at verse 14. Jesus Christ is named for the first time at verse 17, with Moses beside him: the law given through the one, and the grace and the truth come to be through the other. And at verse 18 the Word who was God in the beginning is called God again at the end, μονογενὴς θεός, so that the Prologue's last verse answers its first. Carson calls the parallels between verse 1 and verse 18 "a kind of literary envelope that subtly clasps all of 1:1–18 in its embrace."[^envelope] The frame [the foyer-bracket architecture](/commentary/john/1-1-to-3/02-foyer-bracket/) set at its near end closes here, and with it the Prologue's design is complete.

It matters, third, because of the one place in it where the time of the telling reaches the reader. The unit narrates the incarnate life from its completion, in retrospect: he became flesh, dwelt, was beheld, and from his fullness we have all received. Into that retrospect the evangelist sets the Baptist's witness in the present, John *bears witness* and *has cried out*, so that his witness still sounds in the reader's day. And the movement closes on what holds outside narrative time: no one has ever seen God; the only Son, who is at the Father's side, has made him known.

For the reader and the teacher alike the unit sets before both a single composed movement: the confession that the Word became flesh and was beheld; the witness to the one who came after him and was before him, testifying still; and the ground of all that has been received, which is the grace and the truth that came through Jesus Christ, crowned by the declaration that he alone has made the unseen God known. What the passage does not do is also part of what it is. It does not tell how the Word became flesh: the clause states the fact of the enfleshment and not its manner, and the text's silence on the virginal conception is kept. It does not take the reader into the Gospel's story; verse 19 does that. And it does not decide what the Prologue as a whole is, beyond the frame and the framework the commentary has settled. That is left to the review of the whole Prologue that follows this unit and to the volume's introduction, and the commentary's close names what stays open.

## Framework Inheritances

This commentary inherits the project's standing framework for the Johannine Prologue, the settled readings of John 1:1–13 that hand directly into these five verses, and one calibration, settled at verse 9 and carried through the second movement, whose end point this unit was given to settle. Naming the inheritance is part of the commentary's scholarly disclosure. The reader is given the framework under which the unit has been read; the points at which 1:14–18 stands on work already done; and the points at which the readings are held at a stated force, so that the force is not raised in the reading.

### Linear architecture, and the unit's place

The Prologue is read under the Ridderbos/Morris three-movement linear architecture: 1:1–5 (the Word in the beginning), 1:6–13 (the Word coming as light), 1:14–18 (the Word's glory in the flesh). John 1:14–18 is the third movement and the last. It is one composed movement, bounded at Καὶ ὁ λόγος σὰρξ ἐγένετο and ἐκεῖνος ἐξηγήσατο (see the treatment of the bounded movement). With its seating, all three movements stand by the commentary's own adjudication. The framework itself was left open when the Prologue's frame was first settled, and it is now decided. The treatment of John 1:6–13 engaged the chiastic alternative (Culpepper, Boismard, Staley) at its centre in verse 12b, and it left the schemes centred on 1:14 to this unit. This unit decides between linear and chiastic for the Prologue as a whole (see the treatment of the framework). **A chiasm of the whole is declined**, with very high confidence, on seven traditions read in their own voice.[^seven] The Prologue is three movements in progression, the third returning to the first. It is not a concentric structure around a centre, at 1:14 or anywhere else. **The positive framework is a linear movement carrying concentric figures,** held with high confidence. It stands below the decline because its second half, the concentric figures, rests on a narrower base than the decline does. What the chiastic tradition saw is received, and what it built on that is not. Brown grants Boismard "some valid observations (the concluding vs. 18 picks up the theme of the opening vs. 1; and vs. 15 matches vss. 6–8)", and finds the parallels between verses 3 and 17 and between verses 4–5 and 16 "highly imaginative."[^brown-chiasm] The outer pairs are received: verses 1–2 with verse 18, which is the Prologue's frame, and verses 6–8 with verse 15, the two Baptist passages. The concentric design of the whole is declined, and so are the inner pairings. Brown's own verdict stands with the decline: "We remain in doubt on the applicability of a chiasm pattern to the Prologue." One figure is left with verse 14: its correspondence with verse 1, the Word who was God and the Word become flesh, which would make verse 14 the frame's hinge. It is recorded as an architectural thesis and not settled (see the treatment of the frame completed). It is the volume introduction's to seat, and the commentary's close names it as open.

### The Foyer frame

The Prologue is read under Carson's *foyer* image: the entrance hall through which the reader passes before stepping into the Gospel proper. The strong form of the claim, that the Prologue originates the Gospel's themes rather than summarizing them, is held provisionally, as it was for John 1:4–5 and 1:6–13, and nothing in this unit moves it. The unit's place in the frame has changed with its position. John 1:4–5 stood at the foyer's far pole, and John 1:6–13 stood enclosed. **John 1:14–18 is the frame-closing movement.** Its last verse is the far pole of both brackets settled in [the foyer-bracket architecture](/commentary/john/1-1-to-3/02-foyer-bracket/), with very high confidence. The first is the verse 1 / verse 18 pair: the Word πρὸς τὸν θεόν, and the only Son εἰς τὸν κόλπον τοῦ πατρός. The second is the verse 3 / verse 18 pair: the creator, and the one who has made the Father known. The treatment of verse 18 received the content of that pole as it had been settled, and nothing needed reopening. The κόλπος of verse 18 renders the πρὸς τὸν θεόν of verse 1 at another register: it is the same relation, named now as the Son's to the Father. ὁ ὤν characterizes the Son as being in the bosom of the Father without temporal restriction, his incarnate life included (see the treatment of verse 18). This unit adds the brackets' seating: the third movement is where the frame closes, held with high confidence, and the brackets themselves are not settled again. Inside the closed frame two further figures are placed, each at its own force; a third, verse 1 / verse 14, is set out above. The θεός of verse 1c and the μονογενὴς θεός of verse 18 form an inclusio of the received text, held with high confidence. It is settled as a figure of the text the commentary secured, and not as evidence for that text, because its carriers argue it as evidence for the reading and Ezra Abbot argued it as grounds for a gloss. The creation of verse 3 and the law of verse 17 are an echo inside the frame, not a third bracket of it.

The foyer's inner pair, verse 4 / verse 14, is the one the earlier units held open, and this unit assessed it. The content is grounded: the glory beheld at verse 14 is the same reality as the life-and-light of verse 4, now manifest in the flesh. The placement supplements it, since each pole is followed by a witness that confirms it. On those data the two verses are held as the foyer's inner correspondence, with high but provisional confidence, on the editor's ruling (see the treatment of the foyer's inner pair). The claim that the evangelist composed them as a bracket is the project's reading, and it is disclosed as such. No primary-text source states the bracket, and the placement datum is one argument with two carriers. **The pair is assessed, not raised.** The very high confidence the earlier units anticipated for it is not reached, and the frame's settled pairs do not move. One consequence runs back into John 1:6–13. That unit held its second movement's Prologue-wide seating, as what the inner pair encloses, provisionally on this assessment. That seating now rests at high but provisional confidence on the ground named here, and not on a settled bracket. This provisionality is stated here once and is not repeated at each layer of the architecture.

### What John 1:6–13 hands forward

The second movement handed forward a settled body of work. Six of its deposits are taken up here.

1. **The seam after verse 13.** It was settled with very high confidence and is received as this movement's front edge. The marks carried across it are the marks set out above: verse 14's καί, and ὁ λόγος named for the first time since verse 1.
2. **The μονογενής relation, with Cyril's bound.** The begetting of the children of verse 13 is creative and not procreative. It is bounded above by participation in God's gift and not in his being. The treatment of verse 14b–d receives the bound where the Gospel first names the Father and the Son: the Son's sonship is his own, and the children's is given.
3. **The status of God's children and the enfleshment.** The treatment of verse 12 found that status not confined to the enfleshed arrival. Two data pressed on that finding at verse 14, and both were assessed. One was Brown's frame from 7:39, which places the realization of sonship at the glorification. The other was Keener's "transferral," by which the Word became flesh so that others could be born of God.[^transferral] Verse 14a states the *fact* of the enfleshment, not the *timing* of the begetting, so the finding is not shifted. Keener's coupling is received at its proper register. The σάρξ that cannot generate the life of God at verse 13 is the σάρξ the Word became at verse 14 (see the treatment of verse 14a).
4. **The spine.** The Prologue's run of tenses, ἦν / ἐγένετο / ὃ γέγονεν, runs here on two of its terms: the ἐγένετο of verse 14 and of verse 17, and the ἦν of verse 15. Where voices at verse 14 deny that ἐγένετο there means coming into being, the divergence is received as a matter of the term's content at this locus, and the spine's register is untouched. No ὃ γέγονεν stands in this movement either. What that absence means for the Prologue's design is the volume introduction's question.
5. **The series of unit readings.** This unit's temporal reference stands to those of John 1:4–5 and 1:6–13 as theirs stood to each other, and its reading of the unit as a whole stands to theirs in the same way. With it the series is complete, one synthesis for each movement (see the treatment of the temporal payload).
6. **The present-apertures.** The treatment of each movement recorded its opening onto the reader's present, and John 1:6–13 handed forward the third, at verse 15. It is settled as this movement's one aperture, held with high confidence: the Baptist's witness set in the evangelist's own day (see the treatment of the aperture). The break at verse 18 is of another kind. It concerns what holds outside narrative time, not an opening onto the reader's present. That the two breaks together hold the retrospect open at both ends of the reader's time is recorded with moderate and provisional confidence, as an argued observation that originated in this commentary's drafting, not in the sources. It is left to the volume's introduction.

### The working calibration — presence-as-advent, and its end point

The working calibration was settled at verse 9 and governed how John 1:6–13 read its imperfects. It reads the light's presence as *presence-as-advent*: the light was always present to the world it made, its coming is one continuous advent, and fullness of perception is reached at the glorification. The terminus is perceptual and confessional, and never ontological. Each of its instances in John 1:6–13 was held with high confidence and capped. The cap was the calibration's own ground, which was left open to deepening, not reversal, by the treatment owning the modality at 1:14. That is the treatment of verse 14b–d, and **its ruling is that the end point is deepened, not moved.** The hour stays the terminus of the light's advent toward perception. Verse 14 is now settled as what the modality is: the glory perceived in the flesh, through the signs, by those given to see, during the ministry. The fullness of that perception, and of the life it opens, comes at the glorification. The articulation is the commentary's own synthesis, on components each attested on its own. Schnackenburg sets the glory revealed in the signs in the time of the incarnation, and the saving power of bestowing life after the hour. Dodd makes the death the glory's final and decisive showing.[^endpoint] Brown's frame from 7:39 is received as it was recorded, not re-read. The deepening stays within the calibration's own condition for change. The finding that verse 14a fixes the fact and not the timing stands beside it.

**Two termini, kept distinct.** The one-life arc traced through the Prologue runs on the light's revelatory reach, 1:4 → 1:5 → 1:9 → 1:14, and it has a terminus within the Prologue that is not the working calibration's. That terminus is the beholding, and the enfleshment is its condition. The arc arrives where the Word arrives, σὰρξ ἐγένετο, and ends where the light is seen, ἐθεασάμεθα τὴν δόξαν αὐτοῦ, on the editor's ruling. The unit's temporal reference gives it its tense-form, and the unit's architecture seats it across the movement (see the treatment of the temporal payload). The calibration's terminus lies in the Gospel, at the hour; the arc's lies in the Prologue. This commentary keeps them apart wherever both are in view. The instances in John 1:6–13 held with high confidence and capped stand as they were settled. The ground under the cap was reassessed and holds, and nothing in this unit re-rates the instances. This commentary does not re-rate them either.

### Intra-Johannine restraint, with the permitted forward gestures

The exegesis treats the unit on its own terms. It permits forward gestures into the Gospel only where a settled reading made one, and those are named here with their locus. They fall into five groups.

**The narrative's threshold.** The Gospel's narrative begins at 1:19, so no narrative stands around verse 15 for a historic present to enliven. The Baptist's "of whom I said" points to a proclamation the narrative gives at 1:27 and 1:30, cited here by anticipation (see the treatment of verse 15).

**The beholding and its time.** The glory is seen in the signs (2:11; 11:40, through Schnackenburg). It is seen through the whole ministry, and conclusively at the death. Its fullness comes at the hour, which is the working calibration's terminus (above). The "we" who beheld are those who saw him in the flesh, in the sense of 1 John 1:1. The widening to every believer is the text's own, at verse 16 (see the treatments of verses 14b–d and 16).

**The Son and the Father.** μονογενής is read with 1:18 and 3:16, so that the relation precedes the sending. That the Son came from the Father is the Gospel's constant thought, but it is taken from the context and not made the preposition's sense. At verse 18 the Johannine use of an attributive μονογενής with an articular noun (3:16, 18; 1 John 4:9) bears on the construal. The perfect of 3:13 bears against reading ἐξηγήσατο as "led the way" (see the treatment of verse 18).

**God unseen.** The Gospel's own 12:41, where Isaiah saw "his glory," bears the reading of the theophanies the summit adopts: what was seen was not God in his being. The beloved disciple ἐν τῷ κόλπῳ τοῦ Ἰησοῦ (13:23) is a probable echo of 1:18 at the level of the image. It bears on the disciple's witness and not on the verse 1 / verse 18 bracket.

**Registered and not engaged.** These are named so that they are not mistaken for gestures made:
- the temple-replacement theme (2:19–22), recorded and not settled;
- McHugh's inclusio of 17:3 and 20:31 on the full name "Jesus Christ", recorded for the unit's architecture;
- the title λόγος not recurring after 1:14, recorded and not adjudicated;
- the θεός inclusio's Gospel-wide extension to Thomas's confession (20:28), which is the volume introduction's.

### Inter-canonical permission, governed by substantive illumination

Canonical material beyond the Gospel enters only where a settled reading brought it in, and each permission is bounded where it was given.

**The dwelling.** ἐσκήνωσεν evokes God's dwelling with his people, as Israel knew it in the tabernacle (Exod 25) and in the idiom in which Sirach sang of Wisdom tenting in Jacob (Sir 24:8, 10). It is a connotation, not a direct reference or a continuous Exodus typology. The Shekinah is a possible echo, not asserted (see the treatment of verse 14b–d). Nor does the verb carry transience: the usage Westcott cites (Rev 7:15; 21:3) runs against it.[^westcott-dwelling]

**Sinai.** The pair χάρις καὶ ἀλήθεια renders the חֶסֶד וֶאֱמֶת God proclaimed to Moses (Exod 34:6), now the character of the incarnate Son. That Exodus 34 frame of verses 14–18 makes the law, given through Moses, a gift of the God whose covenant love and faithfulness Exod 34:6 proclaims. Verse 17 sets the law beside the grace and the truth as the good and the better, fulfilment and not antithesis. It is not Paul's law against grace (see the treatment of verse 17). At verse 18 Moses at Sinai, who could not see God's face and live (Exod 33:20), is the denial's occasioning instance. He is its highest case, not its only target. What was seen in the theophanies was a form, a manifestation or the glory (above). ἐξηγήσατο answers the question of Sir 43:31, and the sentence, not the word, carries the making known of God himself (see the treatment of verse 18).

**Permissions withheld.**
- πλήρωμα carries no Pauline or Gnostic technical weight.
- The law is not read into verse 16's first grace. Philo is borrowed for the usage of ἀντί and not for his purpose.
- The Transfiguration is not the moment of the beholding.

### Patristic engagement at primary-text register

The Prologue's Greek reception bears weight at the registers this unit engaged it, and at the text above all.

**The supersession principle** (described under the sources below) operates twice in this unit. At 1:14–17 it is confined to the sweep of the verses, because the witness of Gregory of Nyssa does not reach the reading the treatment of the text resolved. At 1:18 it is the first instance in this unit in which Gregory's witness reaches the crux itself, on both of its questions. The principle settles whose account of Gregory's text the commentary carries, and that account is the editor's own collation (see the text-critical notes). Elsewhere it is asserted not to operate, reading by reading. At the κόλπος singular it works on data already received and adds no instance.

**The text.** At 1:15, Origen's ὁ εἰπών, read twice in his commentary and by the first hands of Codex Vaticanus and Codex Ephraemi, is ancient and is declined for ὃν εἶπον. At 1:18 the patristic layer divides on the article, and Origen stands on both sides of it. The Fathers lean to the reading with the article, Gregory included, at the grade of a citation. Gregory strengthens that reading's external case without reversing the direction in which the copyists' changes ran, and the reading without the article stands (see the text-critical notes).

**The speaker.** The early record is divided on where the Baptist's words end. Origen has him speak on through verse 18. Chrysostom and Cyril of Alexandria give verse 16 to the evangelist, and Origen himself reports unnamed others who did the same.[^speaker] The commentary closes the question with the evangelist (see the treatment of verse 15).

**Christological reception.** Chrysostom's denial that the Word's essence changed into flesh, and Cyril's refusal of a change in the Word's nature, are the patristic voice of the guard against change, disguise and mixture (see the treatment of verse 14a and the first theological locus). Eternal generation (Cyril of Jerusalem; after Irenaeus, the term used of the pre-incarnate and the incarnate Word) is engaged as the doctrine's historically specific articulation and not settled. At verse 16 the Greek Fathers' "instead of," the grace of the law replaced by the grace of the gospel (Theodore), is the strongest alternative, heard in full and declined (see the treatment of verse 16). Cyril and Augustine on the law as grace are engaged at verse 17. At verse 18 two readings of the theophanies were weighed. The patristic one, that the Word was the one seen, is divided within the tradition, Origen against Augustine, and is recorded and not settled. Gregory against Eunomius is recorded as the Nicene reception of ὁ ὤν. Origen is engaged in translation throughout, and the *Ancient Christian Commentary* at the register of the clauses' reception, as in the earlier units.

### Held open to revision

This commentary is a permanent scholarly artifact, held open to retroactive refinement under the project's standing procedure for it. It composes settled readings, each under its own governance. The live pressure points are named here, so that the reader knows where refinement is expected and in which direction.

1. **The foyer's inner pair** (above). Upward, a primary-text source that states the verse 4 / verse 14 bracket would move it. Downward, a condition was set when it was settled: a primary-text source of the first rank setting verse 4 and verse 14 against each other as *non*-corresponding would withdraw the correspondence, on the record.
2. **The Prologue-wide readings this unit records and does not settle.** Among them are the pattern of three present-apertures, one for each movement; the observation that the two breaks hold the retrospect open at both ends of the reader's time; and what the absence of ὃ γέγονεν from the second and third movements means for the Prologue's design. They are the volume introduction's to seat. The commentary's close names them as open, and nothing in the architecture, the exegesis or the theology states them as settled.
3. **The text.** Three named studies and editions would bear on it, and none is yet in the corpus: Michaels's 1981 study of Origen and 1:15, the Tyndale House edition's apparatus and argument at 1:18, and the *Editio Critica Maior* of John. Each would reopen the reading it bears on only under the conditions the text-critical notes set.
4. **Verses 16–17.** Philo and Edwards at 1:16, and Pancaro and Jeremias at 1:17, are still to be read in full. The treatments of those verses name what they could move.
5. **The apparatus.** Corrections owed to the project's record of what each source treats at 1:14–18 are deferred to their own pass. Every silence this commentary asserts is verified against the source itself and not taken from that record alone.
6. **A door assessed and shut.** The treatment of verse 14b–d left a door open at μονογενής, to be opened if μονογενὴς θεός pressed origin into the word. The treatment of verse 18 assessed it and found that it does not, so the reading at verse 14 stands unrefined.

## Sources and Methodological Discipline

The apparatus is layered, and at every conclusion the layer at which each source is brought to bear is disclosed. Five layers operate, as in the earlier units:
- **Full substantive engagement**, with the source read directly at the load-bearing locus.
- **Direct engagement at a supporting register**, such as an apparatus, a reference grammar or a standard reference work.
- **Engagement with a summary articulation**, which carries the author's authority but not the underlying analysis.
- **Engagement through another scholar's citation**, not the source read directly.
- **Engagement at an adjacent locus**, substantive there and at apparatus level on the present question.

This unit sharpened the layers at three points. First, a patristic commentary read in translation is engaged at the highest register for the reading's sense. Its Greek form is disclosed as reached through another's citation, because the English carries the sense and not the wording (Origen, through Heine). Second, claims about stages behind the transmitted text are recorded and routed to a source- and redaction-critical register, and not adjudicated with the text. Third, the Nicene and later reception of a reading is recorded at a register of its own, distinct from the exegesis it receives. The reader can therefore see, at every conclusion, which sources bear on the result and what kind of evidence each supplies.

### The commentators read directly

A fixed core of seven carried the adjudication at the highest register in every one of the unit's ten settled readings: Schnackenburg, McHugh, Bultmann, Brown, Westcott, Michaels and Köstenberger. At the text-critical base Köstenberger was engaged for a verified silence and for nothing more. Barth stands at the highest register in nine of the ten, and Calvin's commentary in seven. Barrett, Carson, Ridderbos, Morris, Lincoln and Keener carry the readings of the clauses. Harris is the grammatical carrier in his volume of the *Exegetical Guide to the Greek New Testament* and the fullest treatment of 1:18 in *Jesus as God*. Dodd's *Interpretation* carries the glory.

- **Schnackenburg.** He is the load-bearing modern source, as in the earlier units, and was read from the page images. His argument that the Logos stays the subject of the sentence, and so was neither turned into flesh nor only seemed to be flesh, grounds the guard at verse 14a. His excursus on the Johannine signs was read for this unit. It is engaged at a supporting register at verse 14b–d, where its sentence on the glory revealed in the time of the incarnation grounds the working calibration's end point. At verse 14a it is engaged only as an analogy, at an adjacent locus, and never as his exegesis of the verse. At 1:18 he wavers between the readings, and the treatment of the text assigns him to neither side.[^schnack]
- **McHugh.** He supplies the unit's fullest dossiers and its sharpest single sentences. σάρξ is a synecdoche for the whole human person, bones, blood and soul together. It is, he judges, scarcely possible that ἡμεῖς πάντες ἐλάβομεν stood on the Baptist's lips, so the Baptist's speech ends at verse 15.[^mchugh] He is engaged against at three points: his preference for ὁ μονογενής at 1:18, his gloss of μονογενής at 1:14, and the perpetuity he reads into ἔμπροσθέν μου γέγονεν, whose sense of rank the commentary takes.
- **Barth, *Witness to the Word*.** He is more often adopted here than in John 1:6–13, and at full force either way. *Adopted:* ginesthai as the new and specific content the Logos now adds; the beholding as the perception of his deity; the backward look, spoken from this side of the epiphany, said at verse 15 and extended to verse 16; and at verse 17, contrast with no trace of polemic. *Engaged against:* his reading of σάρξ after 1:13, and his revival of the old view that verses 16–18 continue the Baptist's speech.[^barth] The composition fence this commentary keeps on Barth — that he is not consulted on the Prologue's composition — held at every settled reading but one. At the reading of the unit's architecture alone it was lifted, bounded to seven named pages.
- **Bultmann.** He was read from the scans. He reads the present μαρτυρεῖ as the Baptist's witness still in force, not a report of the past, and ἀντί as "instead of" within the Revealer's inexhaustible grace.[^bultmann] At 1:18 he is the chief voice engaged against: his case that θεός cannot be defended is answered on its grounds, at the crux and at the construal. His hymn-and-source explanations are surveyed and set aside (see the methodological discipline below).
- **Brown, in the Anchor Bible.** He carries the whole person as the sense of σάρξ, the case against rendering μονογενής by "only begotten", and the verdict on the chiasm (above). His lean to καί at verse 16 and his judgment that verse 15 betrays editing are routed to the source-critical register and declined on the final form.[^brown]
- **Westcott.** His three parts of verses 14–18 are the articulation's oldest statement (above). He grounds verse 16 as the evangelist's by the "we all", and holds μονογενὴς θεός at 1:18. He is engaged against where he makes verse 17 parallel with verse 16 and not its ground.
- **Michaels.** He is the counter-case at 1:15. He prefers ὁ εἰπών, and the commentary's reading rests in part on his own concession. He gives the argument from the copyists' habits for μονογενὴς θεός at 1:18, and reads the beholding as spanning Jesus' whole life on earth, not one experience.
- **Köstenberger, in the *Evangelical Exegetical Commentary*.** He gives the reasons for θεός at 1:18. He names identifying Jesus with the Word as the purpose of verses 14–18, and he reads the narrative line as running straight from 1:1 through 1:14 to 1:18, a voice for the framework (above).
- **The commentators on the clauses.**
  - Ridderbos's reading of "became" as identification, not transformation, grounds the guard beside Schnackenburg. His tabernacle motif is engaged at verse 14b, and his three-part cycle carries the architecture (above). His choice of υἱός at 1:18 is declined on its ground.[^ridderbos]
  - Carson reads verse 15 as a parenthesis the evangelist planned, the contrast of verse 17 as plain in the text, and θεός at 1:18 in apposition.[^carson]
  - Barrett carries σάρξ at its starkest and the Father's only Son, and is engaged against at "became" and at υἱός.
  - Morris carries the one grace replacing another, and the theophanies' limit.
  - Lincoln carries the guard against docetism, and the denial at verse 18 extended to Moses.
  - Keener is engaged at full weight for the "transferral" of verses 13–14 (above), and at verse 17 for a contrast that is one of degree rather than of kind.
  - Calvin's commentary is treated under the Reformed tradition below.
- **Harris.** In his *Exegetical Guide* he is the grammatical carrier of a unit-wide reading. He calls the Prologue's six ἐγένετο all constative, verse 17's among them. At their verses he labels ἐλάβομεν and ἐξηγήσατο constative, and ἐσκήνωσεν "more probably constative", like ἐθεασάμεθα; ἐδόθη he leaves unlabelled for aspect. He also anchors the grammar at verses 14, 15, 16, 17 and 18. In *Jesus as God* he is the fullest single treatment of 1:18. He states the preference for μονογενὴς θεός as the primitive text and the epexegetic apposition of θεός to μονογενής, and he reads the collocation as the Prologue's climax.[^harris]
- **Dodd.** He carries the enfleshment as becoming, not descent upon or indwelling, and the glory as God's being and presence made available to human experience. He makes Cana's words a deliberate recall of 1:14, and holds that the Torah did not fully bring grace and truth. His chapters on the First Episode and on the titles Messiah, Son of Man and Son of God were read at the source; the second of these, read after the treatment of verse 18 was settled, was assessed against the condition that treatment set, which it did not meet.[^dodd]
- **The narrow-scope works.** Edwards's study of verse 16 was read after the treatment of that verse was settled, and met none of the conditions it set. At verse 17 it stands at the highest register for the balanced halves without an adversative.[^edwards] Sosa Siliezar and Boyarin stand at the highest register at the question of the framework, and Waetjen at verse 14a for his divergence on what ἐγένετο carries. Gordley, Nolland, Pagels, Evans, Koester, Meagher and Tobin are engaged at a supporting register, at an adjacent locus or at the source-critical register, and not at the highest.

### Patristic apparatus

The editor's 1974 collation of Gregory of Nyssa's text of John is engaged as primary-text work — original collation — at the text of verses 14–17, at the crux of verse 18, at verse 15, at the unit's temporal reference and at its architecture. The supersession principle operates at its bounds, twice (above). At 1:14–17 the principle is confined to the sweep, verse by verse: at 1:14 a full citation, at 1:15 a single citation of πρῶτός μου ἦν, at 1:16 ὅτι supplied as context, and at 1:17 an adaptation only. At 1:18 Gregory reaches the noun at the grade of a full citation, where both collators agree on θεός, and the article at the grade of a citation. Brooks's study of Gregory's New Testament text stands at published-secondary register as the supersession's foil, with one independent datum, πλήρη at verse 14.[^roades]

- **Origen** is engaged in translation, through Heine, at the highest register for sense, with his Greek lemmata disclosed as reached through the translation. He is at once a text-critical witness (ὁ εἰπών at 1:15; both sides of the article at 1:18) and a reader. He has the Baptist speak on through verse 18, reads διά at verse 17 as agency, and holds that the theophanies' seers saw the image of the invisible God.[^origen]
- **Chrysostom, Cyril of Alexandria, Augustine, Theodore and Cyril of Jerusalem** enter at their loci (above), chiefly through the *Ancient Christian Commentary* at the register of the clauses' reception.
- **Gregory against Eunomius** is the Nicene reception of ὁ ὤν, recorded at the register of reception.
- **Through others' citation.** Irenaeus, Athanasius and Chrysostom stand there for πλήρη as an early witness to the construal with δόξαν (through Brown). So do the Greek Fathers' "instead of" at verse 16 (through McHugh) and the absence among them of the reading that has ὁ ὤν speak of a return (through McHugh).

The patristic Greek of verse 15 on pre-existence in Gregory's works is a question of intake and is not engaged here.

### Text-critical and grammatical apparatus

The editions were checked at first hand. The Nestle-Aland edition, text and apparatus, serves in two roles kept apart: as the reading text, and as documentary data; with the United Bible Societies' text, Houghton and Metzger it counts as one witness of the editions' tradition, not four. Metzger's *Textual Commentary* is engaged at first hand at the crux, within that witness. Houghton is engaged at 1:15 and at 1:18. At 1:15 the datum is his negative and his note on where the Baptist's speech ends. At 1:18 it is his report that the Tyndale House edition adopts ὁ μονογενὴς υἱός while the SBL edition agrees with the United Bible Societies' text. The Tyndale House edition is reached through Houghton and Köstenberger, and the *Editio Critica Maior* of John and its companion volume through Houghton. All three are named among the studies that would bear on the text (above).[^editions]

**The grammatical apparatus.** It supplies the parsings the commentary composed. At every verse a reference grammar was raised to the highest register as the grammatical anchor, and at the unit's temporal reference, for the first time, a single grammarian anchors a unit-wide reading (Harris, above). Beside Harris stand:
- Zerwick, at verse 15 and verse 18;
- Wallace, for the predicate of verse 14a and the construal at verse 18;
- Abbott's *Johannine Grammar*, for the three ὅτι and the connectives of verses 16–18;
- Robertson, at the highest register at verse 16 and at the reference register elsewhere, and Harner, at the reference register.

Moulton stays reached through others' citation, not yet in the corpus, and his reports are recorded where they conflict.[^grammar]

**The recorded silences.** They are load-bearing, and they were tested at the source before they were asserted. Two classes are distinct.
- **Silences that hold.** These are confirmed at the source. Among them: Metzger and Houghton carry no unit at 1:14, 1:15, 1:16 or 1:17; the Nestle-Aland edition prints no punctuation unit at 1:15–16; Colwell, Menken, Dyer and Walker are silent at the unit's loci; Harner is silent at 1:17 and 1:18, having treated 1:14; and Nolland is silent by citation, his allusion to verse 14 engaged at the unit's architecture. Among the summit's load-bearing negatives, no source in the sweep presses origin into μονογενής at 1:18 except Robertson and Gordley's unargued translation.[^silences]
- **Silences recorded in the project's own source record and contradicted at the source.** These were found at verses 16, 17 and 18, where the full sweeps read the sources rather than the record. Calvin's *Institutes*, Bavinck's second and third volumes, Wallace, Westcott, Origen and several commentators were recorded as silent where they are not. Each correction has been passed back to the record, and none is asserted here as a silence.

The lesson of John 1:6–13 is applied as a rule: no claim is routed to a source as silent on the record's word alone (see the methodological discipline below).

### Reformed-tradition systematic register

**Calvin.** His commentary is read directly at every verse and stands at the highest register in seven of the unit's settled readings. σάρξ names mortal humanity and not corrupt nature, the part standing for the whole. Christ ranks above the Baptist by his excellence. At verse 17 the evangelist forestalls an objection. At 1:18 his text reads "Son", and the reading is recorded. His *Institutes*, recorded as silent in John 1:6–13, are now engaged directly at verse 17 (2.7.16) and at verse 18 (1.13.17, 2.9.1, 4.17.30), where the record's silences failed. At 1.13.17 he asks who would dare to say the Son first entered the Father's bosom when he came down to assume human nature, which is ὁ ὤν read as characterization (see the treatment of verse 18).[^calvin]

**Bavinck.** He was not engaged in John 1:6–13, and the ground was stated there. He is engaged in this unit directly, at the systematic register:
- at verse 14a, for the Logos become flesh rather than dwelling in a man, and for the two natures at the register of reception;
- at verse 14d, for the Father's relation to the Son as eternal and not born in time, the doctrine of eternal generation engaged and not settled;
- at verse 17, for the law fulfilled and not abolished;
- at verse 18, for the Son who remained in the Father's bosom during his life on earth, received again at the unit's temporal reference.

At verse 16 he is engaged at an adjacent locus.[^bavinck]

**Torrance.** He stands at an adjacent locus at verse 14b (*Calvin's Doctrine of Man*). *The Trinitarian Faith* is engaged provisionally, because the copy in the corpus is incomplete, and this is disclosed as such.

### Methodological discipline

Seven disciplines govern the composition. Each is carried from the earlier units and exercised here at the unit's own loci.

1. **Final-form composition.** The unit is read as the evangelist's deliberate final-form work. The readings that posit a hymn behind the text, a source, a redactor or an interpolation are recorded at the source- and redaction-critical register and not weighed against the text. These are Bultmann's source and gloss, Schnackenburg's hymn-καί, Brown's strophes, Gordley's redactor and Tobin's additions. The seams after verse 14 and after verse 16 are received as the movement's own joints (above; see the treatment of the articulation).
2. **Receive the observation, decline the explanation.** It is exercised at every settled reading. The chiastic tradition's outer pairs are received and its concentric design declined (above). The source-critics' seams are received and their insertion-explanation declined (the articulation). The abruptness of the name at verse 17 is received and the explanation of it as a gloss declined.
3. **The co-articulation of the synchronic and the diachronic.** It was settled at the close of John 1:6–13 and is confirmed here at its third instance. The articulation and the temporal reference lie over one another tread for tread, and they coincide at the witness and at the crown (see the treatment of the temporal payload).
4. **The source record as the consuming interface, tested at the source.** Each source's routing and silences are taken from the project's per-volume record of what each source treats. No silence is asserted until the source itself has been read (above), and the corrections owed are passed back to the record (above).
5. **The word does not carry what the passage carries.** A lexeme is not made to bear a sense its clause, context or object supplies. σκηνόω does not carry transience (verse 14b–d). μονογενής does not carry origin (verses 14b–d and 18). ἀντί denotes "in place of", and the unceasing supply is the fullness's (verse 16). ἐξηγήσατο means to make known in full, and the making known of God himself is the sentence's (verse 18).
6. **The dual posture.** A source of the first rank is received at one pole of a resolution and declined at another, in the same breath and with the register named. Examples are McHugh on the rank of verse 15 and on ἀντί, and Keener at verse 17 and at verse 18.
7. **Structural necessity, carried and re-grounded.** It is exercised at the unit's two synthesis readings, the temporal reference and the architecture. Conclusions reached to answer one question are carried into another and re-grounded on its own evidence, never assumed (see the treatment of the articulation).

Above all, the governing discrimination stated at the outset holds throughout: the unit is *deliberate composition, not deliberate verse*.
