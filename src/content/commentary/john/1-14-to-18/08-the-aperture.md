The previous section ended at the witness that follows verse 14. This section takes that witness on its own. Verse 15 is the one place in the movement where the telling opens onto the reader's present. The question here is what kind of opening it is, how it differs from the movement's other break in time at verse 18, and what the two together might suggest.

## The one opening

The movement tells a completed life. He became flesh, he dwelt among us, we beheld his glory, from his fullness we have all received: each is told from its completion, looking back. Into that retrospect the evangelist sets one voice in the present. *John bears witness about him, and has cried out*: Ἰωάννης μαρτυρεῖ περὶ αὐτοῦ καὶ κέκραγεν. The present and the perfect, as [the commentary on the movement's tenses](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/04-temporal-payload/) set out, place the Baptist's witness in the evangelist's own day. He bears witness, and his cry, once raised, still sounds. McHugh writes that the two tenses "affirm the perduring nature of John's witness up to the time of the writing of the Gospel", and he prefers that reading to taking the present as a vivid historical present.[^mchugh]

What this section adds is the witness's place in the whole. The unit narrates a completed life and leaves one voice sounding into the reader's day, and the voice is a witness's. The witness of verse 15 is the movement's one opening onto the reader's present. McHugh's horizon is the time the Gospel was written; that the voice reaches the reader's own day is this commentary's reading of what his sentence implies for those who read the Gospel after it.

The opening stands where two things meet. Verse 15 is the second part of the movement's articulation, the witness between the confession of verse 14 and the ground and declaration of verses 16–18, as [the commentary on the articulation](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/03-the-articulation/) set out. And it is the first of the two places where the narration breaks from its retrospect. The joint and the break fall at the same verse. Nothing requires them to fall together, but here they do.

## A third station?

The commentary on verses 6–13 found the second movement's one opening onto the reader's present at the believing, at the end of its turn. It then looked ahead to this verse, on McHugh's sentence, and asked whether the Prologue's third movement has a present of its own, [a third station](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/07-the-aperture/).

It has. If each of the Prologue's movements opens onto the reader's present at one station of its own, the light's shining at verse 5, the believing at verse 12, and the witness here, then this is the third. That would describe the Prologue as a whole, and it is not stated here. This section finds the third movement's one opening. Whether the three openings make a pattern of the whole Prologue is a question for the volume's introduction, where the Prologue is read as a whole.

## The break at verse 18

The movement's time breaks a second time at verse 18: *No one has ever seen God. The only Son, himself God, who is in the bosom of the Father — he has made him known.* The perfect with *ever* says that no one has seen God, without exception and for all time, and the participle says where the Son always is. This break is of another kind. It does not open the telling onto the reader's present. It states what holds outside narrative time. That is this commentary's reading of the two breaks, and it keeps them distinct.

Barth comes nearest to joining them. He likens the two perfects: the perfect of verse 18, "like the kekragen of v. 15", has "present or supratemporal significance, so that it is not part of a narrative but is presenting a universal fact."[^barth] He ties them by their grammar, in that neither belongs to the narrative, and he leaves the present open beside the supratemporal. In his next sentence he applies verse 18 to those who hear and believe and proclaim the gospel. He does not say that the evangelist composed the two breaks as a pair.

Michaels does not say so either. He reads John's testimony as running past verse 15, but he argues it on the other reading of verse 15, ὁ εἰπών, *he it was who said*, which his own translation follows. On the text this commentary adopts, ὃν εἶπον, *this was he of whom I said*, he keeps John at verse 15 as a present witness and leaves open whether John is still the speaker at verse 16. At verse 18 he hears a new tone, "pure christological declaration", and he observes that "there is no ‘now’ in the sentence".[^michaels]

Neither of them states a composed pairing of the two breaks.

## An observation held open

One further thing can be said, and it is said as this commentary's own observation, not as a finding. The two breaks, taken together, hold the retrospect open at both ends of the reader's time: the witness now, the Son always. The witness sounds into the reader's present, and the Son is where he always is, in the bosom of the Father.

No source this commentary has read states it. The commentators read when the movement's architecture was studied do not, and neither do Lund or Boismard, read since. Lund sets verse 15 aside as "extraneous material". Boismard pairs verse 15 with verses 6–8, and verse 18 with verses 1–2. Neither relates verse 15 to verse 18.[^silence] The observation was first made in this commentary's study of the movement's tenses. It is held with moderate confidence and provisionally, and it is not taught here as settled. With the question of the three stations, it is left for the introduction to the volume.

## How firmly this is held

- **The witness in the reader's day**: held with very high confidence, on the present and the perfect of verse 15.
- **The witness as the movement's one opening onto the reader's present**: held with high confidence.
- **The break at verse 18**: of another kind, what holds outside narrative time, not an opening onto the reader's present.
- **The two breaks together, holding the retrospect open at both ends of the reader's time**: this commentary's own observation, held with moderate confidence and provisionally.
- **A third station**: a question for the volume's introduction, not answered here.

The movement has now been read at seven layers: its edges, its parts, its tenses, its frame, its framework, its inner pair, and its one opening. The next section asks what the architecture, taken whole, carries for theology.
