The architecture of this movement has been read whole, and what it carries has been stated. The exposition begins here, at the sentence that opens the third movement: Καὶ ὁ λόγος σὰρξ ἐγένετο, *and the Word became flesh*. It is the high point within the Prologue, where the Word who was with God becomes flesh. The movement it opens is crowned at verse 18, where the only Son makes the unseen God known.

The Word, of whom the first verse said *was* three times, is named here for the first time since that verse. The verb the Prologue used of the creation at verse 3 and of the man sent from God at verse 6 is now used of the Word himself, and its predicate is σάρξ, *flesh*. Five things are fixed on this page, in order: what σάρξ names; what the becoming asserts and what it excludes; what the one verb for the Baptist and the Word carries; what the opening καί does; and what the returning name binds. Then the church's reception of the clause is set out, each part held as firmly as the evidence allows: the two natures, the virginal conception and the docetic question.

## Σάρξ — the whole human being, in creaturely weakness

Σάρξ names the whole human person, not the body alone, and it names that person from the side of creatureliness: earth-bound, transient, mortal and weak, and set over against God. McHugh writes that here it stands, by a figure of part for whole, "for the whole human being, not merely for the flesh, but for the bones and blood and soul as well." Brown: "“Flesh” stands for the whole man." Calvin says the same, that the lower part includes the whole man.[^whole] Schnackenburg and Ridderbos give the side from which the person is named. For Schnackenburg the word expresses what is bound to earth and passes away, the human way of being, "as it were", over against all that is divine and spiritual. For Ridderbos it is the whole person in creaturely existence, distinct from God.[^creature]

**Why σάρξ and not ἄνθρωπος.** John could have written that the Word became a man, as verse 6 said of the Baptist. Barrett notes what the stronger word does: σάρξ represents human nature as distinct from God, "but expresses this in the harshest available terms, harsher than those of v. 6, where John the Baptist is described as ἄνθρωπος."[^harshest] The Word entered human existence at its most exposed.

**Not sinful flesh.** σάρξ here is not flesh as sinful, or fettered by sin, or inclined to it. Schnackenburg sets that sense aside at this verse; it is not the sense of 1 John 2:16, and it is not the Pauline contrast of flesh and spirit.[^notsin] McHugh denies that John's σάρξ stands for flesh "as corrupted by sin (frequent in Paul)", and reads it as "flesh as mortal and physically weak". Calvin takes the word for mortal man and not for corrupt nature, and Ridderbos for what is human as such, apart from sin.[^mortal] Bavinck draws the dogmatic bound. The texts some have pressed the other way, this one among them, say only that the Son of God took a weak nature subject to suffering and death, and not that he himself was a sinner.[^bavinck]

**The same sense as at verse 13.** The word means here what it meant one verse earlier, where [the birth *nor of the will of the flesh*](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/15-begotten-of-god/) was read with σάρξ neutral in itself and set against God by its clause. Barrett holds that only if verse 13 were an interpolation by another writer would it be reasonable to give the word a different meaning in the two verses. Harris says it has no negative overtones at either.[^same] Barth agrees that the sense is the same, but not that it is neutral. He reads σάρξ at 1:13 as human nature in its hostile opposition to God, and "can hardly suppose" that after so pregnant a use it has its neutral sense here.[^barthsarx] His ground is the reading of verse 13 that this commentary declined, so his conclusion is declined with it. But his own bound is received, inside that guard: the Word's flesh may be called the likeness of sinful flesh, and not sinful flesh.

Keener's coupling of the two verses is received at full weight. The flesh that cannot generate the life of God at verse 13 is the flesh the Word became at verse 14, and Keener names the movement between them: "The narrative’s logic implies a transferral".[^transferral] The Word became flesh, so that those born of flesh could be born of God.

## Ἐγένετο — the Word, remaining the Word, came to be flesh

The subject of the becoming is the Word of verse 1, and he remains the subject. He was not changed into flesh, he did not merely appear in it, and he did not cease to be God. He came to be what he was not, in a new mode of existence, his humanity added to what he eternally was. This is held with very high confidence, and what the clause excludes is fixed as firmly as what it asserts.

**Not transformation.** Schnackenburg argues that the clause "cannot mean: “The Logos was changed into flesh”, since the Logos remains the subject in the following affirmation". Ridderbos: "“became” does not mean “changed into.” It denotes an identification." Chrysostom says the same in the Greek reception: "that essence did not change to flesh", but, continuing what it is, took the form of a servant.[^transform]

**Not disguise.** Schnackenburg denies that the Logos "merely appeared in a fleshly disguise; this would not do justice to the verb". Dodd denies that the Logos only came upon Jesus or dwelt in him: "The Logos did not merely descend upon Jesus, enter into Him, or abide in Him. The Logos became the σάρξ or human nature which He bore." Keener: "the verb indicates the enfleshment of his whole being, not a temporary or partial adoption of it as an envelope or covering".[^disguise]

**Not mixture.** Barth: "No change takes place, no transubstantiation, no replacing of the Word’s mode of being by another, no dissolution of the logos in sarx, and also no development of a mixture of both, but a full union".[^mixture]

What remains is the positive claim. Harris: "The Logos became what he was not (σάρξ) without ceasing to be what he was (θεός, v. 1)." McHugh says it in nearly the same terms: "the Logos became a human being without ceasing to be God." Gregory the Great says that the Word was made flesh "not by losing what he was but by taking what he was not."[^positive] Schnackenburg calls the event a change in the Logos's mode of being: hitherto he was in glory with his Father, "now he takes on the lowliness of human, earthly existence."[^mode] What he names is a taking on, and not the replacing of one mode of being by another, which Barth denies. Schnackenburg also quotes Jerome, in the patristic idiom that later grew from this verse, that the Word became flesh and "did not cease to be what he was before".[^jerome]

**The rendering "became."** This rendering is held with high confidence. "Became" is the verb's plain sense with a predicate, and the alternative, "came on the (human) scene", is declined. Barrett states it, and his ground: "It cannot mean ‘became’, since the Word continues to be the subject of further statements—it was the Word who ‘dwelt among us’, and whose glory ‘we beheld’; the Word continued to be the Word." He offers his own sense only with a "Perhaps".[^barrett] Others decline "became" each in his own words:
- Michaels renders "So the Word came in human flesh".
- Waetjen renders "And the Logos happened (as) flesh", and holds that ἐγένετο "should not be translated “become.”"
- Harris, whose own sense is "took on" or "assumed", names Barrett's as "another possible sense".[^others]

All four hold that the Word remains the Word. Michaels, Waetjen and Harris say outright that he is not changed into flesh, and Barrett's refusal of "became" rests on the same ground. That continuity is the very ground Barrett protects. But it is also the premise from which Schnackenburg and Ridderbos exclude "changed into", and so it cannot then be turned against "became".

Two of those who read the verb otherwise are answered in print:
- Ridderbos answers Nicol, who adopts Barrett's rendering: "In my opinion ἐγένετο says more than that."[^nicol]
- Morris reads "became", and reports C. E. B. Cranfield's judgment, against Barrett, that it "is surely the natural meaning of egeneto here".[^cranfield]

**Two accents, received.** Two accents are received as true of the clause, and both are already honoured by what it asserts.

Barth's is that the becoming takes nothing from the Word who was: "Without discarding the einai of v. 1 and all its specific content, the Logos now adds the ginesthai with its express and different specific content." A little later he says that "one must not put the main accent on the idea of coming into being", and that the stress lies on "the coincidence of Word and flesh".[^barthaccent]

Bultmann's accent is restraint. To see the becoming "as a physiological miracle", he writes, "is to do violence to the main theme of the Gospel, that the Revealer is a man." That restraint is received. But Bultmann goes further. Because the Revealer is a man, he holds, "all questions about the way in which the ἐγένετο took place" are superfluous, and that refusal of the question itself is declined. Against Zahn he presses the very identity the clause asserts: "If the Incarnate is the Logos, then he is also θεός." But for Bultmann the Logos is God "in so far as he reveals himself", and he will not think divinity and humanity "according to the category of substance".[^bultmann]

**Eternity and time.** The clause mirrors verse 1. *The Word was God* and *the Word became flesh* are matched statements of the Word's nature: what he was from eternity, and what he came to be. Wallace states the mirror in the grammar: "θεός was his nature from eternity (hence, εἰμί is used), while σάρξ was added at the incarnation (hence, γίνομαι is used)." Westcott states it in its theology: "eternity and time, the divine and the human, are reconciled in Him."[^mirror] The qualitative sense of [θεός at verse 1](/commentary/john/1-1-to-3/08-theos-en-ho-logos/), the Word sharing the very nature of God, is the incarnation's precondition: the one who became flesh is the one who was God.

Some voices at the verse set the sense of coming into being aside, or below other senses. Barth withholds from it "the main accent", and lays the stress on the coincidence of Word and flesh. Michaels holds that for the Word, "who always existed", coming into being "is hardly an option".[^comingtobe] That divergence is received as a matter of what the word means at this verse. It leaves untouched the contrast between *was* and *came to be* that runs through the Prologue.

## One verb, two subjects — verse 6 and verse 14

John uses one verb for the Baptist and for the Word, and the sameness is deliberate. This is held with high confidence.

**The sameness.** Barrett: "It is part of the paradox of this statement that the same word should be used of the eternal Word as of the Baptist." Morris draws from it the reality of the incarnation: "The verb is identical with that used of the Baptist in v. 6, which underlines the reality of the incarnation." Waetjen sees the same verb, which first expressed John's appearance in history, used to unite subject and predicate.[^sameverb] The sameness makes the Word's becoming as real as the Baptist's coming.

**The difference.** The difference of subject carries the distance between creature and Creator. [At verse 6](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/10-witness-sent/), *there came a man*: the verb was the creation's, and, in Brown's words, "John the Baptist is a creature." The Word, of whom verse 1 said *was*, came to be flesh. Schnackenburg marks the difference at the verb itself. The becoming of verse 14 is not the Baptist's appearing, nor creation's coming to be, and "the context alone provides the key." Barth sees the verb's use of the Word at verse 14 as "a further and steep step on the way", which the reader must not suppose already plain at verse 6.[^difference]

That the two subjects carry the distance between creature and Creator is this commentary's own formulation, drawn from these sources. It extends what verse 6 first showed, the Prologue's distinction between *was* and *came to be* applied to a person, and it completes the pairing of the man who came to be on mission with the Word who came to be flesh. The firmness is held at high and no higher, because Barrett's word "paradox" reads the sameness more strongly than this commentary does.

## Καί — the confirmation

The καί confirms. What verses 9 to 13 told in retrospect, the advent and its reception, is here stated in its full reality. Verse 14 adds no new step to the narrative's sequence: it says what the coming was. This is held with high confidence, and no higher, because it rests on reading verses 6 to 13 as an advent told in retrospect.

And what verse 14 says is new. Schnackenburg calls the event "something new (καί...) and unique, which took place only once, a real event". Barth finds the paradox not in the coming but in its manner: "The paradox of the statement in v. 14 is not that he came into the world, for this was said already in v. 9, but that he came in this way, in the flesh."[^new]

**The ground.** Schnackenburg reads the special coming of the Logos as "probably presupposed since v. 9", in the Prologue as the evangelist shaped it. "On this supposition", he writes, "the καί must be understood as confirmative".[^ground] McHugh names the condition. He holds the two readings of verses 10–13, of the Word before the incarnation and after it, to be "perhaps not mutually exclusive alternatives, but complementary". And he gives the confirmatory καί to "those (e.g. Schnackenburg) who think that vv. 10–13 refer to the activity of the Word after the Incarnation".[^condition] This commentary holds that condition: verses 6 to 13 tell the advent in retrospect, and that reading is the ground of the confirmation.

**The restatement, received.** Michaels holds that "So the Word came" is the best rendering of the conjunction, "because the verse is not asserting anything new." Lincoln finds that what the Prologue has so far left implicit is now said outright: "the claim, implicit until now in the prologue, is made explicit that the Word actually became this one person, Jesus of Nazareth."[^restatement]

**The tie to verse 11, declined.** Michaels ties the restatement to verse 11. "The Word came in human flesh", he writes, "simply recalls and reaffirms" *he came to what was his own*. He holds the tie "even though “came” is ἐγένετο here and ἦλθεν in verse 11." The tie is declined on Westcott's ground. The verse is bound to verse 1 by the repeated title, and "This connexion is far more natural than that which has been supposed to exist between v. 14 and v. 9 or v. 11."[^v11]

**The climax.** The clause is the Prologue's climax. Dodd: at verse 14 "we reach the climax of the Prologue", and the clause "takes us beyond the range of Jewish ideas". McHugh puts the climax in the conjunction itself. On the reading he takes up, the most fitting rendering is "and then, to crown all—the Word became flesh", and each καί of the verse is a step "always rising from one climax to another".[^climax] This commentary receives the climax as the clause's, not as the conjunction's own force.

**A parenthesis, declined.** The reading that makes verses 6 to 13 a parenthesis is declined, because they are one composed movement, the Prologue's second. Two writers come near it, each at his own hedge. Westcott: "All that has intervened is in one sense parenthetical." Köstenberger: "It is almost as if all of 1:6–13 forms one large parenthesis."[^parenthesis]

The resumption of verse 1's subject is real, and it is carried by the return of the name, not by the conjunction.

## Ὁ λόγος — the name returns

The return of the name binds the clause to verse 1, [at the movement's front edge](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/02-bounded-movement/), and by that binding it identifies the subject of the enfleshment with the Word who was with God and was God. This is held with very high confidence.

Barth notes that "here for the first time since v. 1 the term ho logos reappears". Schnackenburg makes the name's return "already a link with v. 1", and Brown observes that "Verses 14 and 1 are the only two verses in the hymn that mention “the Word” specifically."[^return] Ridderbos names what the link does: "now the entire focus is on the self-identification of the Word with the man Jesus of Nazareth." Köstenberger makes it the purpose of the whole movement: "To identify Jesus explicitly with the Word is the major purpose of 1:14–18."[^identify]

McHugh observes that here "ὁ λόγος reappears, just once, and for the last time in the Gospel, as a proper name."[^last] The Greek bears him out: the word comes many more times in John, but never again as the name of the Word. The observation is recorded here, and what McHugh draws from it is not ruled on. The verse that names the Word for the last time also [closes the frame](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/05-frame-completed/) that verse 1 opened, and how verse 1 and verse 14 stand to each other within that frame is left open.

## The church's reception, at its registers

The church has read this clause since its first centuries. Its reading is set out here at the registers this commentary holds it, and it is taken up again where the commentary gathers the passage's theology.

**The two natures — engaged, not fixed as the clause's own assertion.** The clause asserts one subject, who remains the Word and came to be flesh, without change, disguise or mixture. The church's doctrine of the two natures is that claim's historically specific articulation, and it is engaged at full weight:
- Schnackenburg finds the doctrine in the verse in germ. In John's own perspective, he says, the two natures "do not stand out", but the doctrine "is comprised therein, in germ, and can be developed legitimately from 1:14 in the metaphysical thinking of the Greek Fathers."
- Calvin holds one person, and "the unity of person does not hinder the two natures from remaining distinct".
- Cyril: "We do not say that the nature of the Word was changed and became flesh".[^natures]

The doctrine is not, however, made the clause's own assertion.

**The virginal conception — recorded, not fixed.** The clause states the fact of the enfleshment, not its manner. Westcott judges that "the fact of the miraculous Conception, though not stated, is necessarily implied by the Evangelist." Bultmann holds that the Fourth Gospel "not only does not contain the idea of the virgin birth, but (like Paul) excludes it".[^conception] The text's own silence is kept.

**Docetism — excluded.** The clause leaves no room for a Christ who only seemed human. This is held with very high confidence. Lincoln says it of the Prologue: "It leaves no room for docetism, the notion that Jesus only appeared to be human or simply took on the outward appearance of a human." Ridderbos and Keener agree. For Ridderbos one cannot "(in “docetic” fashion) hide or even dissolve the reality of the flesh". Keener: "Käsemann is certainly wrong to regard John as docetic".[^docetism]

**Written against docetists? — probable, not certain.** That the clause was *written against* docetists is held as probable and not certain. That judgment is held with moderate confidence.
- Brown asks of this clause whether its intent is polemical, and answers that "we cannot be certain that it was written against such views". Of the Gospel as a whole he judges an anti-docetic motif "possible and even probable … but it has no great prominence."
- Morris thinks it "probable that he was confronted by opponents of a docetic type".
- Schnackenburg is unhedged on the hymn's anti-Gnostic tone: it "is unmistakable". But on the docetic shape of the error he hedges on the same page: "We cannot say for certain whether the false doctrine attributed only an apparent body to the Redeemer". And earlier he leaves open whether the hymn's author meant to counter Gnostic teaching at all: "But we cannot be certain."[^intent]

**Meagher's conjecture — declined.** John C. Meagher conjectured an earlier reading, πνεῦμα, *spirit*, behind σάρξ. On his account it was altered either as source material was adapted for the prologue or soon after the text was joined to the Gospel, probably in a late redaction. The tensions he builds on are real, and they are answered where they arise. The conjecture is declined because this commentary reads [the transmitted text](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/01-the-text/), which Meagher himself holds "unshakably established". He grants that his arguments lack "the compelling evidence of textual variants", and that this limits their strength. But he holds that it is "not a damaging objection".[^meagher]

## What the verse hands on

*The Word became flesh* is the point in the Prologue's account of the Word's one life where the Word who was arrives in the flesh. The goal of that account, the beholding for which the enfleshment is the condition, belongs to the clause that follows: *and dwelt among us, and we beheld his glory*. And the clause fixes the fact of the enfleshment, not the timing of the birth from God that it makes possible. So what verse 12 said of the children of God is not shifted: their status is not confined to the time after the Word's arrival in the flesh.

How firmly each is held:
- **Σάρξ** as the whole human being in creaturely weakness, not sinful flesh, and in the same sense as at verse 13: very high.
- **The becoming.** The Word, remaining the Word, came to be flesh, without change, disguise or mixture: very high. The rendering "became": high.
- **The one verb** for the Baptist and the Word, its sameness and its difference of subject: high.
- **The καί** as confirming what verses 9 to 13 told: high, resting on the reading of the advent told in retrospect.
- **The returning name** binding the clause to verse 1: very high.
- **The two natures:** engaged, not fixed as the clause's assertion. **The virginal conception:** recorded. **Docetism:** excluded, very high. **An anti-docetic intent:** probable, not certain, held with moderate confidence. **Meagher's conjecture:** declined.
