After the ground, its reason: ὅτι ὁ νόμος διὰ Μωϋσέως ἐδόθη, ἡ χάρις καὶ ἡ ἀλήθεια διὰ Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ ἐγένετο, *for the law was given through Moses; the grace and the truth came to be through Jesus Christ.* The grace "we have all received" from his fullness, [grace in place of grace](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/13-grace-in-place-of-grace/), is inexhaustible and his own, *because* the grace and the truth came through Jesus Christ. With this verse the chain of grounds is closed, and the one the Prologue has described is named.

Read in summary, the verse says this. It sets the law, God's gift given through his servant Moses, beside the grace and the truth, which came to be in history through Jesus Christ. They stand as the good and the better, the gift and the reality it bore witness to, not as enemies. The law was a gift of God's grace, but it was not *the* grace and *the* truth.

Six things are fixed on the verse: the second ὅτι; how its two members stand to each other; whether the law was a grace; the two verbs and the preposition with each; the name; and the articles.

## Ὅτι — the chain's second link

The ὅτι is causal, and the verse explains verse 16. This is held with high confidence. Wallace lists the verse among his instances of causal ὅτι. McHugh: "here it is explanatory of v. 16". Michaels has the connective introduce "an explanation of the cryptic 'grace upon grace'", Köstenberger has the clause add "the explanation", and Ridderbos reads verse 17 as "again … a further explanation".[^hoti]

**The weight on the second member.** The stress falls on what came through Jesus Christ. Barth: "Hence the underlying hoti has reference, not to the first statements nor to the antithesis as such, but to the second statements." For Barth, though, the ὅτι does not ground verse 16. The second statements are "too parallel to that of v. 16 to be a basis for it", and he points the ὅτι past verse 16 to the Baptist's words: "An answer or basis is given to v. 15 by the simple means of quietly putting what Christ is and does side by side with what Moses is and does."[^barth120] Harris finds the contrast between the two parts of the verse drawn "with the emphasis falling on the second part", and in his study of the deity of Christ he takes the ὅτι as showing that the verse "is giving proof of the constancy of God's grace". Schnackenburg confines the one to Moses and the other to Christ: "Only the law was given through Moses, grace only came through Jesus Christ."[^weight]

**The chain closed.** Verse 17 explains verse 16, as McHugh, Michaels, Köstenberger, Ridderbos and Harris read it. Verse 16 in turn gives the ground of verse 14's confession, as [the commentary on verse 16](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/13-grace-in-place-of-grace/) found. So the chain of grounds is closed one verse at a time, and the passage's [articulation](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/03-the-articulation/) has its spine.

**The first member is the foil, not verse 16's first grace.** The reading that makes the verse's two members the two graces of verse 16 is Brown's. Verse 17 "merely spells out what has been said in 16 by naming the two occasions of God's demonstration of covenant love", he writes, on a translation he states conditionally.[^brown35] Edwards, Carson and Michaels read the verse the same way.[^samway] The sentence permits that reading, but nothing in it requires it.
- Westcott, Barth and Schnackenburg read the verse without the identification, and so does Harris in his grammar of John. Schnackenburg turns verse 17 against it: "v. 17 also tells against this explanation."[^without]
- Westcott and Barth do not make verse 17 the ground of verse 16 at all. Westcott sets it parallel with verse 16, and Barth sets its ὅτι alongside that of verse 16.
- In his study of the deity of Christ, Harris has verse 17 "specifying the two occasions on which God preeminently displayed his grace". That is the shape of Brown's sentence, though Harris does not call the law the first grace of verse 16.[^harris102]

The grace "we have all received" was received from the one fullness of the incarnate Son, which the law was not. The question verse 16 left open was whether verse 17's two members *must be* its two graces. They need not be, so the reading of verse 16 stands.

**Two other readings are placed.** Calvin reads an anticipation: "This is an anticipation, by which he meets an objection that was likely to arise". That is recorded as this reading's pastoral form.[^calvin52] Westcott makes the clause "parallel with v. 16, and not the ground of it". Barth holds the same, grounding verse 15 instead.[^parallel] That reading is declined.

## The law, and the grace and the truth — fulfilment, not antithesis

The verse sets the law beside the grace and the truth as the good and the better, the gift and the reality to which it bore witness. This is held with high confidence, and not very high.

**Fulfilment.**
- **Carson** finds the law-covenant given by grace and anticipating the incarnate Word, and now "necessarily superseded by that which it 'prophesied' would come": "It is this prophecy/fulfillment motif that explains why the two displays of grace are not precisely identical."[^carson133]
- **Keener:** "The contrast is one of intensity more than of quality", and "the contrast of John 1:17 is between something good and something better, which are not mutually exclusive."[^keener421]
- **Harris:** "John is not contrasting truth and falsehood, but complete and incomplete revelation."[^harris37]
- **Dodd:** "The evangelist affirms that the Torah did not, in the full sense, bring grace and truth, but Christ does."[^dodd]
- **Edwards** finds John seeing the Law "as pointing to Christ, and as fulfilled in him". **Boyarin:** "Jesus comes to fulfill the mission of Moses, not to displace it."[^fulfil]
- **Bavinck**, of the old dispensation at the coming of Christ: "Better still, nothing was abolished, but the fruit was ripe and broke through the husk."[^bavinck]

**The contrast is real.** It is carried by the mediators and the verbs. Carson: "This cannot mean there is no contrast between law and Jesus Christ: that contrast is explicit, on the surface of the text." Ridderbos: "Apart from this contrast the mention of Moses here is hard to make intelligible." Schnackenburg has the verse aim "at showing the previous legal system has been surpassed by the reality of the grace of Jesus Christ". Keener holds that the missing adversative "does not eliminate the contrast", but that it "also does not permit us to exaggerate the force of the contrast".[^real]

**Not polemic, and not Paul's antithesis.**
- **Barth:** "Certainly there is contrast, but with no trace of polemic". "The result is not that Christ is victorious in the competition but that there is no competition." "In John Moses with his law is viewed favorably in his own place."[^barthpolemic]
- **Ridderbos**, reading the passage against Exodus 33–34: "in vs. 17 we are not dealing with a Pauline contrast between law and grace."[^ridderbos57]
- **Schnackenburg:** "Thus John sees no absolute contradiction between Moses who gave the law (at God's command), and Jesus Christ who brought grace and truth."[^schnack277]
- **Edwards** asks that John be let be himself, and finds that he "invariably alludes to Moses and the Law in a neutral or clearly affirmative manner".[^edwards78]
- **Barth** sets aside the law's shortcomings that have been read into the verse "From the days of Augustine until now". "It might play a big role in Paul or Hebrews, but where does it figure in the train of thought of this Gospel in such a way as to justify our introducing it without further ado here?"[^barth123]

**The form.** The verse is a synthetic parallelism: two balanced members without an adversative, the thought moving beyond the first member to the second. Edwards observes that the two halves are "exactly balanced", with "no adversative—no ἀλλά or δέ in the Greek", and that there is still "a progression of thought in the second part of the verse which could not readily have been predicted from its first half". Schnackenburg finds Jeremias "correct in seeing in Jn 1:17 a synthetic, not an antithetic parallelism". Carson: "there is nothing in the Greek text that requires antithesis." Zerwick allows that the two parallel statements, coordinate in form, "do not exclude" subordination in idea.[^form]

**The asyndeton decides nothing.** The absence of δέ settles neither antithesis nor continuity. Schnackenburg compares two places in the first letter of John where the missing δέ marks growing emphasis in one and contrast in the other. A δέ would not have settled it either, since δέ can mark either. Morris: "We should have expected the contrasting μέν and δέ, but John simply puts the two statements side by side." The δέ the Old Latin and a few other witnesses supply is [recorded with the text](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/01-the-text/), and it is not the text. McHugh takes its slight support to confirm continuity at verse 16. That inference is recorded as his, with the tension in his own pages that [the commentary on verse 16](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/13-grace-in-place-of-grace/) noted.[^asyndeton]

**Antithesis declined, its contrast received.** The sources that hold antithesis read the mediators and the verbs rightly. What is declined is the Pauline form of the contrast.
- **Bultmann** holds that form. At verse 17 "the contrast between νόμος and χάρις is introduced", and it "is otherwise foreign to John and comes from the Pauline school". Its foreignness elsewhere, with the abrupt naming of Jesus Christ, is his mark of the verse as the evangelist's own gloss.[^bultmann79]
- **Pancaro** holds the antithesis as John's own. He first finds that on verse 17 alone there is no way of deciding between antithetic and synthetic parallelism, "although the text speaks in favour of an antithetical parallelism". When he returns to the point he writes that "the matter cannot be decided on the basis of v. 17 alone", and at once qualifies it: to call the parallelism synthetic is to disregard that the law was given through Moses but the grace and the truth came to be through Jesus. He grants that John "would probably not deny" that the Law "was authentic divine revelation to the Israel of old". The antithesis he holds is aimed at the claim that grace and truth "are now to be found in the Law".[^pancaro]
- **Barrett** reads grace "as by Paul, in contrast with law" at verse 17. At verse 16, against a reading of grace in place of shame, he writes that "it cannot be said that John regards it", the Old Testament, "as a matter of shame or reproach".[^barrett169]
- **Lincoln** speaks of "an antithetically structured parallelism", and still writes: "This is not a denial that before the coming of the Logos the law was previously an expression of Yahweh's grace and truth."[^lincoln]

Pure synthesis is received in its form and not its substance, because it would make the law and the grace and the truth two of a kind.

The confidence is high and not very high. The sources divide here more sharply than they do on verse 16. Antithesis is held by, among others, Bultmann, Barrett, Lincoln, Morris, Pancaro, Robertson, and the line of Augustine and Calvin. Bultmann, Barrett and Robertson tie the contrast to Paul, and Westcott ties it to "the other apostolic writings".[^antithesis]

## Whether the law was a grace — a gift of grace, not *the* grace and *the* truth

The law was a gift of God's grace: God gave it, and Moses mediated it. It was not *the* grace and *the* truth, which came to be through Jesus Christ. This is held with high confidence. The sources divide into "yes" and "no" along this line, and both answers are held.

**Yes.**
- **Edwards:** "the old covenant was still a gift of grace, that is a mark of God's gracious favour to his people."
- **Carson:** "On the face of it, then, it appears that the grace and truth that came through Jesus Christ is what replaces the law; the law itself is understood to be an earlier display of grace."
- **Michaels:** "The law is itself grace from God, 'given through Moses' as a preparation for more and greater grace to come."
- **Köstenberger** reads ἐδόθη as a divine passive, the giving of the law "a gracious gift". Barrett notes that the Law "is regularly regarded in Jewish sources as a gift of God to Israel".
- **Cyril** grants that "the law too used to give grace to people, calling them to the knowledge of God", in a passage that also has the law condemn the world.
- **Ridderbos** will not put Moses, and the dispensation he represented, outside "the grace and truth of which Jesus Christ was the personification".
- **McHugh:** "for the most basic fact about the Law is that it is the Word of God."[^yes]

**No.**
- **Barth:** "The law is not what will later be said to come through Jesus Christ. It is not grace and truth. Naturally not; nor is it the witness of the Baptist nor John's Gospel!"
- **Barrett:** "the point of the present passage is that grace did not come by Moses."
- **Augustine:** "This grace was not in the Old Testament, because the law threatened but did not bring aid".
- **Calvin:** "his meaning is, that *the law* wanted both of them."[^no]

**Two questions, two answers.** The answers are compatible because they answer two questions. ἐδόθη makes the law God's gift. The frame of Exodus 34 behind verses 14 to 18 makes that gift an act of the God whose covenant love and faithfulness Moses heard proclaimed, the pair the Son was [full of](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/11-glory-beheld/). The articles take that pair up, and ἐγένετο says that it came to be through Jesus Christ. So the grace and the truth themselves are what the law bore witness to, and not what Moses mediated. Barth, who says "no" to the law's being grace and truth, holds elsewhere that the verse does "not deny to the Old Testament, to the law, participation in grace and truth". Augustine and Calvin, also on the "no" side, still keep the law's office. Augustine has the law prepare "the way for that physician who was to come with grace and truth". Calvin: "if you separate *the Law* from *Christ*, there remains nothing in it but empty figures."[^office]

**Not verse 16's first grace.** That the law was a grace does not make it the first grace of verse 16. The grace "we have all received" came from the one fullness of the incarnate Son, and the law did not. The conclusions Edwards, Carson, Michaels and Köstenberger draw at verse 16 are recorded as theirs. Carson's own statement of the fullness reaches further, and it is recorded, not answered here: "the law that was given through Moses, and the grace and truth that came through Jesus Christ (v. 17), alike sprang from the fulness of the Word (v. 16)".[^carson134]

## Ἐδόθη and ἐγένετο, and διά with each

The law was given, and the grace and the truth came to be. Each came through a mediator, and the difference lies in the mediators. This is held with high confidence.

**Ἐδόθη.** The law was God's gift, given through Moses as mediator. Michaels: "the passive 'was given' points to God as the Giver, even though it was given 'through Moses.'" Keener: John "accepts the divine origin of Torah ('it was given' is presumably a divine passive) and the Mosaic agency". McHugh: John writes ἐδόθη "because the Law was a gift received by a servant to pass on to those for whom it was intended". Abbott finds "subordinate agency" "clearly attributed to Moses".[^edothe]

**Ἐγένετο.** The grace and the truth came to be, an event in history, through Jesus Christ. Westcott: the Gospel "came", "as if, according to the orderly and due course of the divine plan, this was the natural issue of all that had gone before". Schnackenburg: the evangelist "ponders how the reality of divine grace only came upon earth with the incarnate Logos". Barth: "the egeneto has this historical sense here as in v. 6, although naturally we have to think also of the great principial egeneto of v. 14". Lincoln: "The language of 'coming into being' certainly makes this a strong claim." The ἐγένετο of verse 14 and the ἐγένετο here name [one historical event](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/04-temporal-payload/), at its head and at its gift.[^egeneto]

**Διά — agent, and source.**
- Robertson reads Jesus Christ as "the intermediate agent of God the Father", the term he also uses of Moses.
- Harris: ἐγένετο διά "implies that grace and truth were Christ's own intrinsic possession, not extraneous virtues that he simply mediated". And: "He is not only the mediator but also the source of grace and truth."
- Ridderbos: "he not only proclaimed grace and truth but … he himself represented them".
- Origen: the law "has been given 'through Moses,' not 'by Moses,'" and the Father "has both given the Law through Moses and has produced through Jesus Christ the grace and truth which have come to men".[^dia]

**Each at its own level.** The two are not in conflict once each is read at its own level. διά names agency, and the agency is the Son's under the Father. Wallace classes the διά of verse 3 as intermediate agency, "with the implication that God is the ultimate agent", and lists verse 17 with it. Origen reserves "by" for the Father and gives the Word the "through whom". What came through the Son is his own, as Harris has it, because he is full of grace and truth. The difference from Moses lies there: Moses passed on a gift received, and the Son is what he gives.[^levels]

Bultmann thinks the two verbs carry no distinction: "It is hard to think that any significance attaches to the alternation of δοθῆναι and γενέσθαι." Beside that he sets two observations, that διδόναι "is elsewhere used of the revelation", and that it "in v. 16 is presupposed in correlation with λαμβάνειν". The judgment is declined. Barth's "innocent" ἐδόθη denies that the verb disparages Moses' mission, and that "a significant contrast" can be inferred from it. It does not deny the difference between a gift given and a reality come.[^verbs]

**The echoes.** Sosa Siliezar: "There is a striking parallel between 'everything came into being through the Word' (διʼ αὐτοῦ ἐγένετο) in 1:3 and 'grace and truth came through Jesus Christ' (διὰ Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ ἐγένετο) in 1:17." Moses "is not portrayed as bringing something into existence". Michaels draws the same line: "'grace and truth' came into being 'through Jesus Christ' just as all things came into being 'through him'". Morris allows that the verb "may be meant to associate grace and truth with the work of Christ", the Word's work in creation. The echo of [verse 3](/commentary/john/1-1-to-3/11-panta-di-autou/), *all things came to be through him*, is received in this form: an echo inside the passage's frame, not a further bracket of it. Edwards's suggestion that ἐγένετο echoes verse 14 is recorded.[^echoes]

## Ἰησοῦς Χριστός — the first naming

The Word of verses 1 to 14, made flesh, is named here for the first time, with the compound name and its messianic title, beside the name of Moses. The naming is deliberate, the end of a restraint, not the abruptness of a gloss. This is held with high confidence.
- **Westcott:** "The Person who has been present to the Evangelist throughout is now at last fully named." The name declares "the true humanity of the Saviour (Jesus), and … His relation to the earlier dispensation (Christ)".
- **Barth** finds the name brought forward "in deliberate fashion and after long restraint": "For Jesus the Messiah stands where Yahweh stood."
- **Carson:** "here named for the first time as the human being who is nothing other than the Word-made-flesh."
- **Ridderbos:** "This is the first and—apart from 17:3—the only time that the full name 'Jesus Christ' occurs in this Gospel. Thus the approaching story announces itself."
- **Morris:** "There is point in using the full name Jesus Christ with all solemnity in this passage where his superiority to Moses is being brought out."
- **Michaels:** "the Word through whom all things came to be finally has a name—'Jesus Christ.'"[^name]

Bultmann finds the name brought in "abruptly and without any introduction", and Schnackenburg "without previous introduction". The abruptness is received as a restraint ended. The explanation that the verse is therefore a gloss is declined. McHugh sets aside the name's only other occurrence, at 17:3, as "almost certainly a late addition to the text", and suggests that the formula of 20:31, "Jesus is the Christ", taken with this verse, "may well represent an inclusio embracing the entire Gospel". That suggestion is recorded and not taken up here.[^abrupt]

## Ἡ χάρις καὶ ἡ ἀλήθεια — the articles take up verse 14's pair

The articles take up verse 14's pair, the covenant pair of Exodus 34:6, and set it member for member against ὁ νόμος. This is held with high confidence.
- **Brown:** "The articles before the nouns indicate a reference to the 'enduring love' already spoken of in vs. 14".
- **Michaels** allows either: "Possibly the articles are markers recalling verse 14 … Or they may simply have definite articles in order to stand parallel to 'the law' (ho nomos)."
- **Barrett** notes the recurrence: "The same words (with articles) recur in v. 17." **Barth** notes its weight: "note the solemnity which the use of the article gives these terms here".[^articles]

ἀλήθεια keeps the double sense it had at verse 14, faithfulness and divine reality, and it does not set the law down as falsehood. Harris: "John is not contrasting truth and falsehood, but complete and incomplete revelation." He also notes that the singular verb conceives the two nouns "as forming a unit, not as separate entities". Morris's alternative, "the well-known, outstanding grace and truth", is recorded: he allows both readings, and supports the second from the "preliminary manifestations of grace and truth" in the Old Testament.[^aletheia]

## What the verse hands on

- The chain of grounds is closed one verse at a time, and it is the spine of the passage's [articulation](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/03-the-articulation/).
- The law as God's gift, and Moses and Christ, are drawn out in this commentary's account of the theology of the law and the grace.
- At verse 18 Moses returns, as the instance behind the denial that anyone has ever seen God.
- The two ἐγένετο, at verse 14 and here, name [one historical event](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/04-temporal-payload/), at its head and at its gift.

## The stages behind the text

Readings of a stage behind the text at verse 17 are received as questions about the Prologue's sources, and are declined at the text as it stands.
- **Bultmann** reads the verse as "an exegetical gloss on v. 16" by the evangelist, and the hymn as reaching "its proper conclusion" with verse 16.[^bultmannstages]
- **Schnackenburg:** "The two ὅτι at the start of v. 16 and v. 17 do not prove that v. 17 must belong to the hymn as well as v. 16. On the contrary, they are a pointer to the work of the evangelist."[^schnackstages]
- **Gordley** sets verse 17 inside the hymn he reconstructs, with a hedge of his own, and **Tobin**, following Brown, takes verses 17–18 for "explanatory additions to the hymn".[^gordleytobin]
- **Edwards** offers a conditional: the Jewish way of speaking of the Law would be particularly fitting if verse 16 continued the Baptist's words, "v. 17 then being his own comment".[^edwardsn39]

Whether the Prologue grew from a hymn is a question for the volume's introduction, and it is not answered here.

Gregory of Nyssa's witness to the verse is an adaptation of its first member only, and it has no bearing on the text the commentary reads.[^gregory] Pancaro's study of the law in the Fourth Gospel has been read in full, and nothing here moved. Jeremias's article on Moses, reported by Schnackenburg, Harris, Carson and Edwards, has not been read for this commentary.[^jeremias]

How firmly each is held:
- **Ὅτι:** causal, the verse explaining verse 16, the weight on the second member: high.
- **The law, and the grace and the truth:** fulfilment, not antithesis, the contrast real and not Paul's: high, not very high, because the sources divide more sharply here than anywhere in verse 16.
- **Whether the law was a grace:** a gift of God's grace, not *the* grace and *the* truth: high.
- **Ἐδόθη and ἐγένετο, and διά:** the law given through a servant, the grace and the truth come to be through the Son, who is their source: high.
- **Ἰησοῦς Χριστός:** the first naming, deliberate: high.
- **The articles:** verse 14's pair taken up: high.
