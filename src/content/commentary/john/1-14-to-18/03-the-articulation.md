The previous section set the movement's edges. This one goes inside them. A reader who has settled where verses 14 to 18 begin and end can still read the five verses as one undifferentiated stretch, and miss how they are jointed. They are jointed in three parts, the joints are marked in the Greek, and a chain of grounds runs back through them to the confession with which the movement opens. This section sets out the three parts, the words that tie them across the middle one, the particles that mark their joints, and why the three-part reading is not optional.

## Confession, witness, ground and declaration

Inside its edges the movement divides in three.

The first part is **the confession of verse 14**. The Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we beheld his glory, full of grace and truth.

The second is **the witness of verse 15**. John's testimony attests the confession: the Baptist is set at verse 14 to bear witness to what is confessed there.

The third is **the ground and the declaration of verses 16 to 18**. Verses 16 and 17 each open with ὅτι, *for*, and together they give the ground. Verse 18 opens without a connective and declares what they ground, and it crowns the movement.

The division is not this commentary's alone. Westcott drew the same three parts: "St John gives first the substance of the apostolic witness (v. 14); and then the witness of prophecy, represented by the Baptist (v. 15); and thirdly, a general account of the nature of the revelation (vv. 16–18)."[^westcott] The names for the parts differ. The joints between them do not.

## The catchwords span the witness

The strongest evidence for the three parts is a set of words that reach across the middle one. What verse 14 says the Son is full of, verses 16 and 17 say was received and came. Verse 14 calls him πλήρης χάριτος καὶ ἀληθείας, *full of grace and truth*. Verse 16 says that from his πλήρωμα, his *fullness*, we have all received grace in place of grace. Verse 17 says that the grace and the truth came to be through Jesus Christ. So the thought runs across verse 15, from verse 14 to verse 16, and the witness stands inside it.

Ridderbos states it directly: "Verse 16 no longer forms a part of John's testimony but (as is evident from the word 'fullness') continues vs. 14."[^ridderbos] Brown says it more briefly still: "(16 expands 14; 15 is an interruption)."[^brown]

Ten traditions carry the tie. Beside Ridderbos and Brown stand Schnackenburg, Barrett, Carson, Lincoln, Westcott, Bultmann, Barth and Gordley, each at their own page.[^ten] They differ on how verse 15 came to stand where it does. Some take it as an insertion into an earlier text, others as a parenthesis the evangelist planned. They agree that verse 16 takes up verse 14 across it. Barth is a witness of a particular kind. He grants the connection and names the words that carry it, and he holds in his own voice that "we are right to connect v. 16 with v. 14." He also reads verses 16 to 18 as the Baptist's own words, a reading taken up below.[^ten]

## The joints are marked in the particles

The Greek marks where the parts meet, in its small connecting words.

- **The καί at verse 14.** It opens the confession, and with it the movement. In a Prologue whose sentences have mostly stood without a connective, it is the mark the previous section set at the front edge.
- **The two ὅτι of verses 16 and 17.** Each introduces a ground. Abbott counts three ὅτι in the passage and finds that each "seems to base a new statement on some preceding similar one."[^hoti] The third, in verse 15, stands inside the Baptist's words, *because he was before me*, and marks no joint between the parts. The two that mark the articulation are those of verses 16 and 17.
- **The asyndeton at verse 18.** Verse 18 opens with no connective at all, and the absence is itself a tie. Harris reads it so: "Being asyndetic, verse 18 must be closely connected with verse 17."[^asyndeton]

## The chain, and the crown

**The chain is the articulation's spine.** It was closed one link at a time, in the exegesis of the verses: verse 17 grounds verse 16, and verse 16 grounds verse 14. Read backward, the ground of the third part runs to the confession of the first, across the witness that attests it.

The seams of the articulation are held with very high confidence. The crown is held with high confidence. The crown is verse 18 read as the movement's close inside the third part, and not as a fourth part of its own. It declares what verses 16 and 17 ground, and with it the movement ends where the Prologue ends.

## Two dissents

Two readers dissent at a link of the chain. Both are recorded, and each is answered where the exegesis of its verse answers it.

- **Harris**, in his exegetical guide, ties verse 16 to the Baptist's testimony. On his reading the evangelist, in verse 16, "confirms the accuracy of the Baptist's testimony to the superiority of Jesus as the incarnate Logos by appealing to the uniform experience of believers."[^harris] Verse 16 then grounds verse 15 rather than verse 14. The catchword *fullness* is what that reading leaves unexplained, and it is declined.
- **Westcott** makes verse 17 parallel with verse 16, and not its ground: "The clause is parallel with v. 16, and not the ground of it."[^westcott17] He ties verse 16 to verse 14 with the others, and he declines the second link.

## The source-critical seams

Readers who reconstruct an earlier hymn behind the Prologue draw seams in this passage. They draw one after verse 14, where the Baptist's testimony breaks in, and one after verse 16, where on their account the hymn ended and the evangelist's additions began.[^seams] The seams after verse 14 and after verse 16 are received here as real, and they fall at the articulation's own joints: the first between the confession and the witness, the second at the ὅτι of verse 17, inside the third part. Their explanation, insertion into an earlier hymn, is declined, as the commentary declines it throughout. The observation is received and the theory of composition is not adopted.

A second reading is recorded and declined. Some have heard verses 15 to 18 as one speech, the Baptist's, so that verses 16 to 18 are his words and not the evangelist's. Barth revives that reading and claims the early church for it: "the whole of the early church also had no doubt that the Baptist's statement included vv. 15–18."[^speaker] The early record is in fact divided, and the exegesis of verse 15 closes the question with the evangelist. The Baptist's words end with verse 15, and verses 16 to 18 are the evangelist's.

## Why the three parts are not optional

The articulation is held, in part, by structural necessity. Three readings were each settled to answer one verse's question:

- the witness of verse 15 as integral to the passage, set at verse 14 to attest it;
- the ὅτι of verse 16 as giving a ground, fixed to verse 14 by the catchword;
- the chain of grounds, closed at verse 17.

None of the three was settled to answer the question of the unit's articulation. Carried into that question, each is grounded afresh on the division and on the convergence of the catchwords. Remove the three-part reading, and the three settled readings stand unrelated. Keep it, and they are three joints of one movement.

That is how the movement is jointed. What the movement's verbs say about time, the incarnate life told from beyond it, is the next section's question.
