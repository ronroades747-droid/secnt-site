The previous section set out the time this movement is told in, and ended on a question: how its last verse closes not only the movement but the Prologue. This section answers it. The Prologue opens with two brackets, figures that join its first verses to a verse at the far end, and the commentary settled both of them where the Prologue begins. This movement is where they reach that far end. What it adds is their place: the third movement is the one that closes the Prologue's frame, and its last verse is the far pole of both brackets.

## The two brackets close

The first bracket joins verse 1 to verse 18. *The Word was with God*, at the beginning; *the only Son, himself God, who is in the bosom of the Father*, at the end. The bosom of the Father renders, at the close, the *with God* of the opening. The second joins verse 3 to verse 18: the one through whom all things were made is the one who *has made him known*. The creator is the exegete. Both pairs were settled, each as a pair, with very high confidence in the commentary on [John 1:1–3](/commentary/john/1-1-to-3/02-foyer-bracket/), and what verse 18 says at their far pole is received here as that commentary settled it. They are not settled again.

What is new is where they close. With very high confidence in the pairs and high confidence in their seating, this commentary holds that the movement of verses 14 to 18 is the Prologue's frame-closing movement, and that verse 18 is where the frame completes. From the second movement the same frame was seen [between the foyer and the summit](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/05-the-frame/). From here it is seen closing.

The commentators say it in their own words. Carson calls the parallels between verse 1 and verse 18 an inclusio, "a kind of literary envelope that subtly clasps all of 1:1–18 in its embrace."[^carson] Ridderbos: "And thus the circle is completed."[^ridderbos] Lincoln: "The end of the prologue, therefore, brings us back to its beginning."[^lincoln] Bultmann, Keener and McHugh say the same, each in his own form.[^three] Carson and Lincoln speak of verse 1 and verse 18 alone, and so do the others. None of the six names the bracket from verse 3. That one rests on the commentary's reading of the Prologue's opening, not on these six.

## Inside the closed frame

Within the frame two further figures are placed, each held at its own strength, and a third is recorded and left open.

**The θεός inclusio.** The Prologue's first verse ends *and the Word was God*, θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος. Its last verse names *the only Son, himself God*, μονογενὴς θεός. The θεός of verse 1 and the θεός of verse 18 form an inclusio, a figure the text itself makes, closing where it opened. This commentary holds it with high confidence.

Harris states it as part of a larger figure: "Μονογενὴς θεός forms a suitable climax to the Prologue, catching up (by *inclusion*) the two crucial designations of the Logos in the two principal preceding verses (viz., vv. 1 and 14)." The two designations he means are θεός, from verse 1, and μονογενής, *only*, from verse 14. In the same sentence he goes on to say that the phrase attributes deity to the Son, "as earlier it had been predicated of the Logos (v. 1)."[^harris] Westcott says much the same, that the reading combines "the two great predicates of the Word".[^westcott] Waetjen adds that verse 18 draws God and the Logos, the two of verse 1, into the Prologue's conclusion, and sets them in reverse order.[^waetjen] So for Harris, Westcott and Waetjen alike, verse 18 gathers up two words, from verse 1 and from verse 14. The inclusio this commentary holds is the θεός strand of that figure: θεός in the first verse and in the last. Each of the three affirms that strand in his own words, and Keener states it alone.[^three]

The inclusio is held at high confidence, not higher, for a reason internal to its witnesses. They argue it as evidence for the reading of verse 18, μονογενὴς θεός, over the alternatives. And an older argument, Ezra Abbot's, as Harris reports it, turned the same observation the other way: a closing θεός answering the opening one was just what a copyist might have added in the margin.[^abbot] An argument that serves both sides of the text question cannot be leaned on to settle the figure. So the commentary does not use the inclusio as evidence for the text. The text was settled first, on its own evidence, in the commentary's [text of these verses](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/01-the-text/), and the inclusio is held as a figure of the text so settled. The question earlier left open, whether the θεός of the two verses was composed as a pair, is answered here.

**An echo, verse 3 and verse 17.** Verse 3 says, *Through Him all things were made*: πάντα δι' αὐτοῦ ἐγένετο. Verse 17 says that *the grace and the truth came to be through Jesus Christ*: ἡ χάρις καὶ ἡ ἀλήθεια διὰ Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ ἐγένετο. The same verb stands in both, ἐγένετο, the verb of what *came to be*, and in both what came to be came through him. This is an echo inside the frame, on the Prologue's spine of tenses, at its ἐγένετο. It is not a third bracket. The two brackets reach to verse 18, and verse 17 is not the far pole.

As a composed pair, matched point for point, the echo is Boismard's, one of the matching members of the chiasm he finds in the Prologue. Brown grants that Boismard's arrangement is "supported by some valid observations", but the parallels he finds between verses 3 and 17, and between verses 4–5 and 16, Brown judges "highly imaginative".[^brown] The commentary receives the echo of the verb and declines the composed pair.

**The hinge, verse 1 and verse 14.** One more relation is recorded and not settled. Verse 14 opens *And the Word became flesh*, and it answers verse 1: the Word who was God, and the Word become flesh. Harris states it as antithesis: "Verse 14 stands in antithetical parallelism to v. 1."[^harrisjohn] Schnackenburg and Ridderbos register the link too.[^hinge] Read so, verse 14 is the frame's hinge, the point at which the Word of the beginning enters the world.

The commentary records it and does not add it to the two brackets. Whether the hinge is a figure of the Prologue's design or a link the reader rightly feels is a question for the Prologue as a whole, and it is left for the commentary's introduction to the whole Prologue to settle.

## How firmly this is held

- **The two brackets**, verse 1 to verse 18 and verse 3 to verse 18, were settled with very high confidence where the Prologue begins. They are received here and not settled again.
- **The frame's closing in verse 18**, this movement as the one that closes it: high confidence.
- **The θεός inclusio**: high confidence, as a figure of the text and not as evidence for it.
- **The echo of verse 3 at verse 17**: received as an echo inside the frame, at the spine's ἐγένετο, and not as a third bracket.
- **The hinge, verse 1 and verse 14**: recorded, and left open.

The frame has no centre and no fold. The Prologue's last verse answers its first, and nothing in this section turns the Prologue around a midpoint. Whether its parts are folded around one is the next section's question.
