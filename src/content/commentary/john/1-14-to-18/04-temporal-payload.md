The previous section set out how the movement is jointed. This one asks what time its verbs are told in. Tenses are easy to read past, and in writing this compressed a good deal of the argument sits in them: whether the Word's coming is announced or remembered, whether the Baptist's witness is a report of something over or a voice still heard, and where the Son stands when the movement ends.

The answer can be stated in one sentence, and the rest of the section unfolds it. The movement tells **the incarnate life in retrospect from its completion, broken twice, by a witness that still sounds and by a truth outside narrative time, and closed on the Son who abides with the Father.**

## The frame

The movement's aorists set its time as one retrospect, spoken from this side of the incarnate life. They run from σὰρξ ἐγένετο, *became flesh*, to ἐξηγήσατο, *has made him known*, and each of the evangelist's aorists gathers its span into one completed fact. The dwelling, the beholding, the receiving, the grace and the truth come: none of them is told as a sequence of moments. Each is looked back on whole.

Barth says it at verse 15: "There is undoubtedly a backward look. The pass from the Old Testament to the New has been crossed. We stand on this side of the epiphany." He carries the same reading to verse 16, where the past tense of *we have all received* is, he says, not to be pressed and not to be ignored.[^vantage]

The grammar carries it verb by verb. Of the Prologue's six ἐγένετο, Harris writes that "these uses of the aorist are all constative, denoting action, whether momentary, prolonged, or repeated, that is conceived of as a unit." At their verses he names ἐλάβομεν, *we have received*, and ἐξηγήσατο constative, and ἐσκήνωσεν, *dwelt*, "more probably constative", like ἐγένετο and ἐθεασάμεθα, *we beheld*. The ἐγένετο of verse 17 is one of his six. The ἐδόθη of the same verse, *was given*, he leaves without a label for its aspect.[^harris]

No single verse settles the frame, but taken together the verses leave no other. The exegesis reads the dwelling of verse 14 as constative, the receiving of verse 16 as constative, the *came to be* of verse 17 as an event in history, and the making known of verse 18 as constative. A frame is what those readings make when they are laid side by side.

Two things follow. The movement into history is what the retrospect looks back on, not the vantage it looks from. And the later aorists do not step forward from verse 14, as though the story began there and moved on. They gather up what verse 14 began.

## The head

The aorist of verse 14 names one becoming: the Word became flesh. Its breadth, the epiphany in all its breadth, is what the first clause says together with the second. The dwelling among us explicates the becoming. The single occurrence is the span of the verb, and the breadth is what the two clauses refer to together.

This is the one point at which the temporal reading is held in part by structural necessity. The relation between the becoming and the dwelling was settled in the exegesis of verse 14, to answer that verse's own question. Carried into the question of the movement's time, it is grounded afresh. The head is held with high confidence, the confidence of the reading of verse 14 on which it rests.

## The two breaks

Twice the movement steps out of its retrospect.

**At verse 15** the present μαρτυρεῖ, *bears witness*, and the perfect κέκραγεν, *has cried out*, set the Baptist's witness in the reader's own day. John does not merely once bear witness. He bears witness, and his cry, once raised, still sounds. McHugh writes that the two tenses "affirm the perduring nature of John's witness up to the time of the writing of the Gospel."[^mchugh]

**At verse 18** the perfect with πώποτε states, without exception and for all time, that *no one has ever seen God*, and the participle ὁ ὤν, *who is*, states where the Son always is: *in the bosom of the Father*. Neither is narrated. In Barth's words, the perfect "has here present or supratemporal significance, so that it is not part of a narrative but is presenting a universal fact."[^supratemporal]

The two breaks do different work. The cry stands into the reader's day. The seeing is denied for all time. Both stand outside the aorist retrospect, but they are not two ends of one design, and this commentary composes no frame across them. The witness of verse 15 is the movement's one aperture, a voice sounding into the reader's present. The break at verse 18 is of another kind: what holds outside narrative time altogether. The movement's last verb, the aorist ἐξηγήσατο, then closes on the completed revelation, set against the Son's abiding with the Father.

The witness of verse 15 is held as a break with very high confidence. The break at verse 18 is held with high confidence, the confidence of the reading of verse 18 on which it rests.

## The spine at its close

The Prologue has a spine of tenses, set in [John 1:1–3](/commentary/john/1-1-to-3/03-macro-tense-spine/): the Word who *was*, ἦν, set against what *came to be*, ἐγένετο. This movement brings the spine to its close.

- **Two ἐγένετο, one event.** The ἐγένετο of verse 14 and the ἐγένετο of verse 17 name one historical event, at its head and at its gift. The Word became flesh; the grace and the truth came to be through Jesus Christ.
- **The interior climax.** The ἐγένετο of verse 14, set against the ἦν of verse 1, is the spine's contrast at its height: the Word who was with God became flesh. It confirms the reading of the spine this commentary settled at the Prologue's opening.
- **The participle of verse 18.** ὁ ὤν answers the ἦν of verse 1 in what it says. The Son who is in the bosom of the Father is the Word who was with God. But it is not an instance of the spine's contrast, because no ἐγένετο stands against it in its clause.
- **The perfect of verse 15.** The γέγονεν of verse 15, *has come to rank*, is a perfect of the spine's own verb, but it names a resulting state within the Baptist's saying, and it is not on the spine.

The commentary's earlier description of the spine called the perfect of verses 3 and 4 its unique bridge. That description is scoped to the bridge's work at those verses. It moves nothing, and the γέγονεν of verse 15 does not compete with it.

As the spine ran through the movements of [verses 4 and 5](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/03-macro-tense-run/) and verses 6 to 13, it runs through this one.

## The parts and the tenses lie over one another

The articulation set out in the previous section and the plan of tenses set out here lie over one another tread for tread:

| The articulation | The tenses |
| :---- | :---- |
| the confession, verse 14 | the aorist retrospect of verse 14 |
| the witness, verse 15 | the break of verse 15 |
| the ground and the declaration, verses 16–18 | the aorists of verses 16 and 17; the break and the closing aorist of verse 18 |

The two readings rest on different witnesses. The articulation rests on the tradition of division and catchword. The plan of tenses rests on the grammarians and on the commentators' reading of the verbs. Each is brought to bear at its own question, and the two meet without either borrowing from the other.

This is the third time in the Prologue that the parts of a movement and its tenses have been found to lie over one another, after [verses 4 and 5](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/03-macro-tense-run/) and [verses 6 to 13](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/08-co-articulation/). Here it shows two things the earlier movements did not:

- **The joint and the break coincide at the witness.** Verse 15 is at once the articulation's second part and the plan's first break. So at verses 4 and 5 the present fell with the turn of the passage. At verses 6 to 13 the two parted.
- **The crown and the second break coincide at verse 18.** The verse that crowns the articulation is the verse at which the narration steps outside time.

At verses 6 to 13 the commentary found that the parts and the tenses need not meet at the aperture for the pattern to hold. That stands. It is not required here either, but here it happens.

The plan of tenses is held with very high confidence at its frame and at the witness of verse 15. It is held with high confidence at its head, where it rests on the reading of verse 14, and at verse 18, where it rests on the reading of verse 18.

That is the time the movement is told in. How verse 18 closes not only this movement but the Prologue's frame, answering its first verse, is the next section's question.
