Between the confession of verse 14 and its ground in verse 16 the evangelist sets a witness: Ἰωάννης μαρτυρεῖ περὶ αὐτοῦ καὶ κέκραγεν λέγων· οὗτος ἦν ὃν εἶπον· ὁ ὀπίσω μου ἐρχόμενος ἔμπροσθέν μου γέγονεν, ὅτι πρῶτός μου ἦν, *John bears witness about him, and has cried out, saying, "This was he of whom I said, 'He who comes after me has come to rank before me, because he was before me.'"* The man who [in verses 6 to 8 was spoken of](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/10-witness-sent/) now speaks, and he speaks in the reader's own day.

Five things are fixed on this verse: the tense of the witness; the riddle of rank and priority; the formula that introduces the riddle; where the Baptist's words end; and what the verse is doing in the movement.

Read in summary, the verse says this. The evangelist sets the Baptist's witness in the present of his readers: John *bears witness*, and his cry, once made, stands. He quotes the Baptist's known saying as a riddle in three terms. The one who comes after John in time has come to rank before him, a rank that came to be in the incarnate one and stands now, because he was before John in time: he existed before him. The Baptist's words end with this verse, and the verses that follow are the evangelist's.

## Μαρτυρεῖ — a witness that still speaks

μαρτυρεῖ is not a historic present narrating a past act. It sets the Baptist's witness in the evangelist's own day, and κέκραγεν has the force of a present: the cry, once made, stands. This is held with very high confidence.

The commentators hear it across their traditions. McHugh: "The present tense μαρτυρεῖ and the perfect κέκραγεν affirm the perduring nature of John's witness up to the time of the writing of the Gospel." Schnackenburg hears the two verbs make the forerunner's voice audible now, and Bultmann reads the present as a witness still in force, not a historical report.[^present] Barrett: "John (like the Old Testament) remains as a permanent witness to Christ." For Westcott the witness is "present and complete; present because his mission was divine, complete because it was directed to a special end which was reached." Zerwick takes the present to mark the testimony as valid for every time.[^permanent] Barth carries it to the reader: "Face to face with readers and hearers of the Gospel the Baptist still has to say what he says."[^barthreader]

**Against verses 6 to 8.** Set beside the Baptist's first appearance, the contrast is exact. There the aorists tell his coming as finished work. Morris reads the aorist of verse 7 as meaning not that John witnessed continually "but that he accomplished a finished work". Here the same witness continues.[^aorist]

**The historic present, declined.** Brown reads the tense as a historic present: "Literally, historical present". Yet he grants, on the perfect beside it, that the Baptist's witness "is looked on as still in effect against the claims of the sectarians".[^historic] And no narrative stands around this verse for a historic present to enliven. The Gospel's narrative begins at 1:19, after the Prologue has closed.

This is the movement's one opening onto the reader's present, [the aperture](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/08-the-aperture/) through which the Prologue's third movement, told otherwise in retrospect, speaks into the reader's day.

## The riddle in three terms

The saying sets three terms in a row: one who comes after, who has come to rank before, because he was before. Each term is fixed on its own axis. The first is time, the second rank, and the third time again, now the time before all time.

## Ὁ ὀπίσω μου ἐρχόμενος — after, in time

The one who comes after John comes later, in time and in ministry. This is held with high confidence.

Zerwick and Harris read ὀπίσω temporally, "after me (in time)".[^opiso] McHugh grants, against his own reading here, that the Synoptic form of the Baptist's saying carries a purely temporal sense. He finds more than this in John, and renders the phrase "There is one of my followers".[^mchughopiso] The sense of discipleship is recorded and not fixed. Barrett allows it, guardedly: he who first appeared as a successor, "perhaps as a disciple (this may be suggested by ὀπίσω)", of John the Baptist has now taken rank before him. Michaels reads both senses as intended: "Very likely the reader is intended to notice both contrasts", and at the parallel in verse 30 the Gospel writer is "content to let both implications stand".[^discipleship]

The riddle's logic needs ὀπίσω on the axis of time, because πρῶτος answers it there. What comes after in time is before in time.

## Ἔμπροσθέν μου γέγονεν — a rank that came to be

The second clause speaks of rank, not time. This is held with very high confidence. The rank came to be in the incarnate one, from his existence in time as man; it was shown at his manifestation, and it stands now, when the Baptist speaks. That is held with high confidence.

**Rank, not time.** McHugh carries the rank. If ἔμπροσθεν referred to precedence in time, the clause would only repeat the one that follows, and he endorses the lexicon's judgment that the words would then be a tautology. His own rendering: "he has always been (γέγονεν is perfect) one who takes precedence, by rank, before me".[^rank]

**A rank that came to be.** His "always" is declined. It would put eternity into the second clause and fuse it with the third, and that is the fusion the tautology argument he adopts forbids.
- Zerwick ties γέγονεν to the incarnate existence: "v. 3, i.e. as man".
- Harris gives that sense as one of two. If the perfect is present in sense, the clause states a standing rank. If it is a regular perfect, he "has become before me", as a human being.[^gegonen]
- Westcott: "As soon as He was manifested He took up a position in advance of His Forerunner."
- Barth: "This saying points to a fact which the speaker recognized as already complete when he spoke. The gegonen shows this."[^complete]

γέγονεν follows on [the ἐγένετο of verse 14](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/10-word-became-flesh/): the Word *became* flesh, and a rank that *has come to be* belongs to the one who came to be.

## Ὅτι πρῶτός μου ἦν — before him, and so before all

The third clause gives the ground of the second. The one who came after ranks before because he was before, and the "before" is the Word's being before the Baptist, not a higher historical rank. It is priority in time, and so pre-existence, grounding the rank. This is held with very high confidence.
- Harris: "The preexistence of Jesus Christ the Logos was the ground and evidence (ὅτι) of his surpassing of John in rank".
- Schnackenburg reads the clause of pre-existence: the evangelist cites John "to prove that Jesus, though coming later, ranks higher than him … because in reality he existed before him". Bultmann reads it the same way, and finds in it the ground of the rank: "the πρῶτός μου refers to his pre-existence".[^preexistence]
- Westcott reads the being-before as of his essence, with 8:58: "He was in His essence (8:58) before John, and therefore at His revelation He took the place which corresponded with His nature."
- Carson finds in it "not only temporal priority … but also absolute primacy".[^primacy]
- Barth sees that the clause, if it is not to repeat the second pointlessly, cannot denote a higher historical rank again.
- Origen reads it as a preference by being before.[^barthorigen]

**The rank reading, engaged and declined.** Calvin takes the third clause of rank: Christ was preferred to John "because he was more excellent". Abbott gives the rank reading to the Baptist's intention, "my superior, my elder brother". But he holds that the words, as the Baptist spoke them, "might be interpreted as meaning 'He was, from the beginning, my First,'" the Firstborn of God, and at that level he stands with the reading of priority.[^rankreading] Brown allows "my superior" as a rendering, "but such a translation ruins the contrast". Morris thinks the rank sense "seems to be an unnatural meaning for the expression".[^ruins] The tautology argument closes the alternative: a third clause of rank would only say the second again.

**Bultmann's riddle.** Bultmann calls the contradiction between πρῶτός μου and ὀπίσω μου "a riddle which remains unsolved". That is received as a description of the figure's surface, not as its outcome, since he goes straight on: "And yet it is precisely this which gives the grounds for the ἔμπρ. μου, because the πρῶτός μου refers to his pre-existence."[^riddle]

**The verb of the Word's being.** The ἦν of πρῶτός μου ἦν is the ἦν of the Word in the Prologue's spine of tenses, the Word who *was*, set against what *came to be*: the Word *was* before the Baptist. Here the spine is confirmed. The verse's other ἦν, in οὗτος ἦν, and its γέγονεν are read at their own clauses and are not added to it. How the tenses of the whole movement carry the life they tell is set out with [the movement's temporal reference](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/04-temporal-payload/).

## Οὗτος ἦν ὃν εἶπον — the known saying, cited by anticipation

The formula cites the Baptist's known saying ahead of its place in the narrative. This is held with high confidence.

**A retrospective imperfect.** The ἦν is spoken from the witness's present, looking back. It identifies the one the Baptist had spoken of; it does not predicate the Word's being. McHugh holds that "the imperfect is the correct tense to refer back to a chronologically anterior witness by the Baptist". Barrett gives the witness at the time of writing as "This Jesus was the person I spoke of". Barth reads the ἦν of his historical appearance considered as a whole: "Hence we should not press the imperfect." Westcott: "The Baptist throws himself backward in thought."[^retrospect]

**Cited by anticipation.** εἶπον points to the Baptist's known proclamation, which the evangelist cites here by anticipation and which the narrative gives at 1:27 and 1:30, as [the commentary's opening](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/) set out.
- McHugh names the saying "yet to be related, in 1:27, 30".
- Barth: "Formally we have still to say that the verse is naturally an anticipation of v. 30."
- Schnackenburg places the saying at 1:30, where it too is supposed to be known, "probably referring to 1:27". Keener names the tradition behind it at 1:27 and 1:30.[^anticipation]
- Carson's "by anticipation" is said of the identifying: the evangelist "by anticipation is identifying Jesus with the Word-made-flesh".
- Bultmann holds the saying known from the Synoptic tradition. He reads verse 30 as pointing to a scene the evangelist "has not described", and the saying at verse 27 as one an editor has inserted.[^bultmann97]
- For Michaels, on the text the commentary follows, "The double time perspective is confusing". He reads another text, on which the retrospect falls away.[^michaels84]

The readings differ in their account of the ἦν, whether a looking back, an attraction to εἶπον, or the appearance taken as a whole, but they converge on its function.

**Two readings declined.**
- **The being-predication.** Morris thinks that "More probably it is a way of referring to the continuing existence of the Word." It is declined, because the clause's subject is οὗτος, the one the Baptist identifies, and its verb answers εἶπον, not the Prologue's ἦν of the Word.[^being]
- **Editing.** Brown finds the reference to the earlier saying awkward even at 1:30, and "here it is illogical and the sure sign of editing". That judgment is received as a question about the sources and is declined at the text as it stands.[^editing]

The reading ὃν εἶπον itself, against ὁ εἰπών, was [secured with the text](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/01-the-text/).

## Where the Baptist's words end

The speech introduced by λέγων ends with this verse, and verses 16 to 18 are the evangelist's. This is held with high confidence. The quotation closes after πρῶτός μου ἦν, and the full stop of the standard critical edition stands.

**The internal datum.** Westcott: "These words and those which follow are certainly words of the Evangelist and not of the Baptist. This is shewn not only by their general character, but by the phrase *we all*." McHugh: "It is difficult, if not impossible, to conceive of the words ἡμεῖς πάντες ἐλάβομεν (in the past tense) on the lips of the Baptist". Bultmann holds that the ἡμεῖς of verse 16 must be those of verse 14.[^internal] The ἡμεῖς of verse 16 takes up the ἡμεῖς of verse 14, [who beheld the glory](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/11-glory-beheld/), and widens at *all* to the believing church. And the past ἐλάβομεν names a receiving the Baptist, speaking before the ministry, could not report.

**The consensus.** Ridderbos holds that verse 16 is no longer part of John's testimony but continues verse 14, and Schnackenburg agrees. Keener takes it as "clear" that the Baptist's voice ends at verse 15, and finds it "most natural" to read the fullness of verse 16 from verse 14. Morris finds that the view that the Baptist still speaks in verse 16 "does not seem to be correct". Houghton notes that "the customary practice in translations is to restrict it to 1:15 only".[^consensus]

**The early record is divided.** Chrysostom gives verse 16 to the disciple and not to the forerunner. With Cyril of Alexandria he reads the clause as the evangelist's. Origen reports unnamed others who gave verses 16 to 18 to the apostle.[^early] The landing page of this commentary [sets the early record out](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/).

## The extended speech, engaged and declined

The reading that the Baptist speaks on past verse 15 is engaged in full and declined. Its arguments are about connection, not about the speaker.

- **Origen** holds the Baptist speaks through verse 18. It is "very forced", he writes, "to suppose that the word of the Baptist is suddenly and unseasonably, as it were, broken off by the word of the disciple."[^origen]
- **Barth** reads verses 16 to 18 as the Baptist's. "The modern view seems to have against it the connecting and explanatory hoti of v. 15", he writes, and then, of the Baptist: "It is hard to see why, having been allowed to speak in v. 15 … he should not be continuing to speak in their name." He takes it, by his own account, "with a pinch of salt", since even verse 15 is "a typical saying" and not a reported address. In his opening survey of verses 1 to 18 he had asked whether it is not significant that the early church read verses 16 to 18 as the Baptist's "with a claim to no little probability", conceding "even if exegetically mistaken".[^barth]
- **Ridderbos** meets the point about ὅτι directly. The insertion of verse 15 does not disturb the connection: "Rather, it incorporates vs. 15 into what is explained."[^incorporates]
- **Calvin** gives the Baptist verse 16 alone, and verse 17 to the evangelist.[^calvin16]
- **Waetjen and Barth** are the reading's modern carriers through verse 18. Houghton notes that the speech could extend to the end of 1:18.[^carriers]
- **Michaels** extends the Baptist's testimony into verse 16 only on a reading of the formula, ὁ εἰπών, which [the commentary's text](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/01-the-text/) does not follow.[^michaelsspeech]
- **Heracleon's** division, which gives verse 18 to the disciple, is declined. It is reported only by its opponent, Origen.[^heracleon]

The confidence is high and not very high. The text marks no close of the quotation, and weighty voices stand on the other side with a real early tradition: Origen and Barth, who carry the speech through verse 18, and Calvin, who gives the Baptist verse 16. The extent of the Prologue as a whole is a question for the volume's introduction, and it is not answered here.

## The witness's place — set at verse 14 to attest the glory

Verse 15 is a composed witness, integral to the passage, the second of the Prologue's two passages on the Baptist. It is set at verse 14 to attest the glory there beheld, and the text is not bracketed. This is held with high confidence.

**What it is set to do.** Robertson: "The witness of John is adduced in proof of the glory full of grace and truth already claimed for the Incarnate Logos." Carson: "It would be fairer to conclude that v. 15 is a planned parenthetical remark", one that grounds the glory of the incarnate Word in a concrete individual, attested by another.[^set] Westcott: "The intercalated witness of the Baptist, pointing to the true nature of Christ, marks the source of this spiritual wealth." Bultmann finds the saying in its rightful place here, "because v. 14 has already given the essential characterisation of the Incarnate", though for him the verse is the evangelist's own note set into an earlier text.[^home] Ridderbos sets the Baptist's witness at [two strategic points](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/07-foyers-inner-pair/) in the Prologue, the second after the pronouncements of verse 14.[^strategic]

**Against the bracket.** Barrett finds it unnecessary, on the ground that verse 16 reads easily after verse 14, "to suppose that v. 15 is an interpolation". McHugh notes the parentheses of Westcott and Hort, the Revised Standard Version and the New Revised Standard Version, and says of the rest: "all others, wisely, do not".[^bracket]

**Its relation to verses 6 to 8.** There the Baptist is spoken of, and here he speaks. His witness moves from the light coming into the world to the concrete one in whom the glory was seen. The two passages on the Baptist stand at matching points, and they are [the outer pair the framework receives](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/06-the-framework/).

**The interpolation reading, received and declined.** Schnackenburg: "V. 15 stands out from the context even more sharply than vv. 6–8." Brown has the verse "awkwardly breaking up vss. 14 and 16". Lincoln: "the flow is interrupted by a further statement about the witness of John."[^interrupted] The bracket of the editions that use one is the typographic form of that explanation. The break they mark is received, as the break at verse 6 was received, and it is read as composed. Barth puts the choice as a condition: "If in spite of this we insist on taking the modern view, we are compelled to view v. 15 as a parenthesis." He does not take the modern view.[^parenthesis] This commentary keeps the word in another sense: a witness set integrally into the line of thought, and not a bracket.

## What the verse hands on

The witness hands on what later verses take up:
- The witness in the present is the movement's one opening onto the reader's day.
- The extent of the speech fixes the ὅτι of verse 16 as the evangelist's.
- The rank that came to be follows on the becoming of verse 14, and the being-before confirms the Prologue's spine of tenses at this verse.
- The two passages on the Baptist at matching points are the outer pair the framework receives.

How firmly each is held:
- **The witness in the present:** not a historic present, the cry standing: very high.
- **Ὀπίσω:** after, in time: high. The sense of discipleship: recorded, not fixed.
- **Ἔμπροσθέν μου γέγονεν:** rank, not time: very high. A rank that came to be in the incarnate one and stands now: high.
- **Πρῶτός μου ἦν:** priority in time, and so pre-existence, grounding the rank: very high.
- **Οὗτος ἦν ὃν εἶπον:** the known saying, cited by anticipation: high.
- **Where the words end:** with verse 15: high, not very high.
- **The witness's place:** integral, set at verse 14 to attest the glory: high.
