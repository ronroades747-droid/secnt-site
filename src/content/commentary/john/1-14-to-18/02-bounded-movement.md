The unit opens where the Word is named again and closes where the Prologue closes, and the evangelist composed both edges. Where a movement begins and ends is not a matter of layout. It settles what the movement says and what lies outside it: whether the confession of verse 14 opens something new or completes what came before, and whether verse 18 is a sentence within the unit or its last word. This section sets the two edges, shows what carries them, and records the edges that have been proposed in their place.

## The front edge

The front edge is not drawn here for the first time. The treatment of [John 1:6–13](/commentary/john/1-6-to-13/) settled a seam after verse 13 with very high confidence, and this unit receives that seam as its own beginning. What the earlier unit saw as its back edge is, seen from this side, the threshold of the third movement. The text marks it four times over, and the four marks are independent of one another.

The first is a connective. Every new sentence of the Prologue up to this point has stood without one except verse 12, which opens ὅσοι δέ, *but as many as*. The καί that opens verse 14 is therefore not a routine *and*. It stands out against the asyndeton around it, and McHugh draws the inference: "the καί here is therefore significant."[^connective]

The second is a name. Ὁ λόγος, *the Word*, is named three times in verse 1 and then not again until verse 14. Through everything between, the Word is carried by pronouns and by the figure of the light. Barth makes this the first formal sign that verse 14 begins anew: "here for the first time since v. 1 the term *ho logos* reappears."[^name]

The third is a pair of terms that bracket the unit. Μονογενής, *only*, and πατήρ, *Father*, appear together at verse 14, "glory as of the only one from the Father," and together again at verse 18, which closes on the one "who is in the bosom of the Father." Neither word occurs anywhere else in the Prologue. Barth observes that these two terms "seem to combine the last five verses of the prologue into a material unity," and his hedge is kept.[^name]

The fourth is a change of voice. At verses 14 and 16 the first-person plural enters: the Word dwelt among *us*, *we* beheld his glory, from his fullness *we* have all received. In Lincoln's words, "In vv. 14 and 16 there is a switch to the first person plural," as the narrator places himself among those who have believed.[^voice]

## The back edge

The back edge is the volume's terminus. Verse 18 ends the Prologue, and with verse 19 the narrative of the Gospel begins: the testimony of John before the delegation from Jerusalem. Michaels puts it plainly: the narrative proper "will resume in earnest only at verse 19."[^back] Where the front edge has to be established by the marks just set out, the back edge is the end of the composition itself. What this unit closes is not only its own movement but the Prologue.

## One section, from both sides of the hymn question

Between the two edges stands one section, and it is not this commentary's division alone. The sources set it in their own voice, and they come from both sides of the question whether the Prologue reworks an earlier hymn, so the agreement does not depend on a shared theory of how the text was composed.

Ridderbos, who reads the Prologue as it stands, writes: "In vs. 14 the third and last segment of the three-part cycle begins, and at the end (vs. 18) everything returns to the beginning." Schnackenburg, working inside his source-critical treatment of the hymn, still holds that on the Prologue's present structure one should rather distinguish three sections, the third of them verses 14 to 16 or 18. He leaves the third section's close open between those two verses, and that is kept.[^section]

Others draw the same section in their headings. Barrett heads verses 14 to 18 "The Economy of Salvation." McHugh heads them "The Word Become Flesh," and Lincoln "the community's confession about the Word." Westcott makes them the third part of his analysis, "The Incarnation as apprehended by personal experience," and Michaels reads them as the believing community's response to the summary of the Gospel in verses 6 to 13.[^headings]

The Evangelical Exegetical Commentary is a witness of a particular kind. Köstenberger dissents at the Prologue's head: he follows the ancient evidence in taking verses 1 to 5 alone as the prologue. Yet at verse 14 he marks the turn. For him verses 6 to 13 are almost one large parenthesis, and here the evangelist closes it and resumes speaking of the Word. And he draws this unit in his own voice: "To identify Jesus explicitly with the Word is the major purpose of 1:14–18."[^eec] A reader who places the Prologue's first edge elsewhere still finds this one.

Thirteen sources set verses 14 to 18 as one section. Twelve do so in their own voice and were read at first hand: Ridderbos, Schnackenburg, Barrett, McHugh, Westcott, Michaels, Lincoln, the Evangelical Exegetical Commentary, Barth, Koester, Evans and Harris. The thirteenth, Beutler, is known here through Köstenberger's report of him, which gives the third of Beutler's three literary subunits as "the Word's incarnation and reception in the community (1:14–18)."[^thirteen]

## The rival edges

Three other edges have been proposed. Each is recorded here and none is adopted.

The first joins verse 14 to what precedes, so that the Word's becoming flesh closes the second movement instead of opening the third. McHugh notes that the New English Bible and the Jerusalem Bible, with their revisions, do this, and that they are the exception, "for no editor of the Greek text has this arrangement." Morris's headings draw the same line: his section on the Word incarnate runs from verse 9 to verse 14, and a separate section on the Word's surpassing excellence opens at verse 15.[^joined] Against the four marks at verse 14, and against the convergence just described, the arrangement is recorded and not followed.

The second makes verse 14 the climax of the whole Prologue. That is a claim about the movement's shape within, not about where it begins, and a climax is not a boundary. It is taken up where the unit's articulation is set out, in the next section.

The third divides the Prologue in two, verses 1 to 5 and verses 6 to 18. It is received here as the treatment of John 1:6–13 received it, and it is not decided at this unit. The extent of the Prologue and its division as a whole belong to the Volume Introduction.

## Why the edges hold

The bounded movement is held with very high confidence. It rests on convergence: four marks in the text at the front edge, the end of the composition at the back, and thirteen sources who set the same section from opposite sides of the hymn question and by different methods.

It does not rest on structural necessity. At other layers of this unit's architecture a reading is held because the settled readings around it cannot be understood without it. That argument is not made here: no settled reading of the unit is carried at this layer and grounded afresh. The edges hold on what the text marks and on what the readers of the text have found in it.

That is the unit's outer shape. How the movement is jointed within, confession, witness, and ground and declaration, is the next section's question.
