The Prologue closes on a declaration. [The verse that closes it](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/15-only-son-made-him-known/) has been read for what it says: the denial, the only Son who is himself God, the participle and its preposition, the bosom of the Father, and the verb that says he has made God known. This page does not weigh those words again. It asks what the church, on the strength of them, confesses about God and about the Son.

Verse 18 crowns the movement, and it is a declaration about God and about the Son: *No one has ever seen God. The only Son, himself God, who is in the bosom of the Father — he has made him known.* What the church confesses from it has three parts, and each is held as firmly as the exegesis held it: God unseen; the Son who alone makes him known; and the Son who is God, in the bosom of the Father. A word on the Baptist's witness, which the verse completes, closes the page.

## God unseen

No one, ever, has seen God in his being. The denial admits no exception, and it is not about the eye alone. [The exegesis](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/15-only-son-made-him-known/) reads the perfect as a universal statement, not as one moment in a narrative, and so the denial stands for all time. What the life to come will give is not the question here. Barth sets aside the lack "which can be made good only eschatologically" as "a matter apart which is not the true issue here". This is held with very high confidence for the denial's sense and its scope.[^denial]

The confession is first a confession about God. He is not the object of any creature's sight. What is known of him is known because he makes himself known, and the verse goes on to say through whom.

**The theophanies.** The God who has never been seen did let himself be seen in Israel's history, in a form, a manifestation, the glory. The denial does not contradict this, because what was seen was not God in his being. The Gospel gives its own case: "Isaiah said these things because he saw Jesus’ glory and spoke about Him" (12:41). This is held with high confidence.[^theophanies]

A patristic reading that the Word was the one seen in the theophanies is recorded here, and not decided. The tradition is divided on it. Origen holds it with a "perhaps": the saints before Christ's coming "have been recorded to have seen God" because they saw "the image of the invisible God". Eusebius meets the denial conditionally: if the text before him is understood "of the Word of God, who was seen by the fathers", no contradiction is involved. Augustine denies that the Son was seen before he took flesh. Yet he holds that it was Christ, the Lord, who revealed himself to Moses, and that what Moses saw, a cloud, an angel, a fire, "bore the type of its Lord".[^wordseen] The reading is the historically specific form the church's early centuries gave the matter. It is engaged at full weight, and the confession does not build on it.

**Moses.** Moses is the denial's highest case, not its only target. Not even Moses, who could not see God's face and live, has seen God. What he was denied is the sight of God's face, not the law's standing as God's gift, which [the confession's third claim](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/19-grace-law-and-truth/) has set out.[^moses]

## The Son who alone makes him known

The declaration does not admit an exception to the denial. It names one who is not among the "anyone": the only Son, himself God, who is in the bosom of the Father. His competence to make God known is grounded in who he is, not in a vision granted to him. This is held with high confidence. Michaels, comparing 6:46, calls him the exception, and Carson reads 6:46's "except" alongside the verse. On their reading too the ground is who the Son is, himself God and in the Father's bosom, and the readings differ [on the word](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/15-only-son-made-him-known/), not on the ground.[^exception]

Barth finds the ground in the word θεός itself: "We are not told that he is the exception, that he sees and knows *theon*, but more simply and strongly that he is *theos*, that he is there where none of us can see."[^theos]

**Revelation by self-revelation.** The Son does not make God known as one who reports what he has seen from a distance. Barth draws this from the bosom of the Father, where the Son stands: "He who reveals stands over against him whom he reveals in such a way that to reveal him he has only to reveal himself." He gives at once the words of 14:9, "Whoever sees me, sees the Father".[^self] The knowledge of God the church confesses is knowledge given in the Son, who in making himself known makes the Father known.

**A telling complete.** The making known is complete. The aorist is constative: it takes in his whole earthly course, the death and the resurrection with it, as one act. What it names is God made known in the whole of that life, that death and that resurrection, and the telling is finished. This is held with high confidence. Complete is not exhausted. Morris, who finds in the verb "a full account of the Father", adds at once: "This does not mean that there is nothing more to be learned of him."[^complete]

## The Son who is God, in the bosom of the Father

**The reading.** The confession stands on the text this commentary follows, μονογενὴς θεός. That reading is held with high confidence, not the highest, because the editions divide: the Tyndale House edition prints *the only Son*. Its construal, *the only Son, himself God*, is held with high confidence, provisionally, as this commentary's reading. [The commentary on the text](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/01-the-text/) sets out the reading, and [the exegesis](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/15-only-son-made-him-known/) the construal. Neither is argued again here.

**Named God at the beginning and at the end.** On that reading the Prologue closes as it opened. The Word who [was God](/commentary/john/1-1-to-3/08-theos-en-ho-logos/) in the beginning is named God again at the end. This is held with high confidence, as a figure of the text this commentary follows, and not as evidence for that text.

**In the bosom, without temporal restriction.** The Son is in the Father's bosom without temporal restriction, and that includes his incarnate life: the incarnation did not interrupt it. Barth: "Having become totally flesh on earth, he is totally with God in heaven." He has just said that the Son's being in heaven is "Naturally not as *sarx* … but as Logos", and that the possibility of that distinction "must be left open here". That the incarnate life is included is not read off the participle alone. It is read from the Prologue's whole account of the Word made flesh. Nor does this commentary claim that the participle states the Son's being in heaven and on earth at once as the manner of his revealing. This is held with high confidence.[^abiding]

**The church's reception.** Gregory of Nyssa, defending Nicaea against Eunomius, refused the argument that being in the Father's bosom takes from the Son his being in the proper sense. If the only-begotten God were not, properly, because he is in the Father's bosom, then nothing that is in anything would be. The Son, Gregory writes, is in the bosom, not having come to be in it. His ground is the Son's being, not time. This is the church's articulation of what the phrase asserts, engaged at full weight and not fixed as the clause's own assertion.[^gregory]

**The intimacy.** The bosom of the Father names the exclusive intimacy of love and knowledge between the Son and the Father. That intimacy is the ground of the making known. It is the same relation that [verse 1 named](/commentary/john/1-1-to-3/07-pros-ton-theon/) as the Word's with God, named now as the Son's with the Father. This is held with high confidence, as [the exegesis](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/15-only-son-made-him-known/) holds it.

## The Baptist's witness, looked back to

The declaration completes what the Baptist said at verse 15. [His "he was before me"](/commentary/john/1-14-to-18/12-witness-who-still-speaks/) asserts the Word's being before the Baptist: priority in time, and so pre-existence, grounding the rank. This is held with very high confidence. Two of the church's early readers bear on it. Gregory of Nyssa cites the Baptist's πρῶτός μου ἦν beside the words of 8:58, *before Abraham came to be, I am*, in his refutation of Apollinarius. His argument there has not been read for this commentary. Origen counts the Baptist's first testimony, which for him runs from verse 15 to the end of verse 18, as the one that "presents the divinity of the Savior".[^witness] Both are recorded as pointers, and nothing is built on them here.

## What the confession hands on

The declaration, then, confesses this. No one has ever seen God in his being, and the theophanies did not show him so. The only Son, himself God, makes him known, not as one who saw what others could not, but as the one he is, in the bosom of the Father. He makes the Father known in making himself known, and the telling is complete. The Prologue that opened on the Word who was God closes on the Son named God. How firmly each is held:
- **God unseen**, without exception and not by the eye alone: very high for its sense and scope. **The theophanies**, a form, a manifestation, the glory: high. **The Word as the one seen in them:** recorded, not decided.
- **Not an exception, but one who is not among the "anyone"**, his competence grounded in who he is: high. **The making known, complete:** high.
- **The reading μονογενὴς θεός:** high, not the highest. **The only Son, himself God:** high, provisionally. **The Word named God at the beginning and at the end:** high, as a figure of the text.
- **In the bosom, without temporal restriction, the incarnate life included:** high. **The intimacy, the ground of the making known:** high. **Gregory against Eunomius:** the church's reception, engaged and not fixed as the clause's own.
- **The Baptist's "he was before me"**, pre-existence grounding the rank: very high.

The arc the whole Prologue has traced, from the Word who was in the beginning to the Son who has made God known, is taken up next.
