The fourth tread closes the staircase, and it answers the question [the third tread](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/08-cosmology-light-in-darkness/) left standing. The light shines within the darkness — and the darkness, at last, does something. Or rather: the darkness *fails* to do something, twice over. Verse 5b takes up the σκοτία one final time and makes it the grammatical subject of a verb, and on that verb — αὐτὸ οὐ κατέλαβεν — the whole movement comes to rest. Two things are fixed on this page: what the darkness failed to do, and how the tense of the failing frames it.

## The turn — the darkness becomes the one who acts

The clause begins where the last one ended. ἡ σκοτία is the same darkness of verse 5a, taken up by the catchword linkage that has built [the whole unit](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/02-gradatio-and-foyer/) — life takes up life, light takes up light, and now darkness takes up darkness — but it is taken up in a changed grammatical role. In verse 5a the σκοτία was the *sphere*: the dative object of a locative preposition, the field the light shines within. Here it is the *subject*: the party that meets the light and is made accountable for what it does with it.[^agent] The composition has been moving toward this all along. The unit began with what *was* in the Word and ends with what the darkness *did* — and did not do.

And the object of the verb is αὐτό, neuter, reaching back past the darkness to τὸ φῶς: [the light of men](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/07-anthropology-light-of-men/), the divine and personal light that has been the unit's subject since the second tread. The last word of the unit is about what happened to *that* light.

Between the two clauses stands one small word carrying a great deal of weight: the simple καί. Harris identifies it as adversative here — "the simple καί, which is sometimes adversative (1:5b, 10c, 11)" — and Abbott, from an independent philological tradition, reaches the same reading and translates it "and yet."[^adversative] That is where the tension of the unit is syntactically housed. Not in the preposition of verse 5a, which is locative and supplies no conflict, but here: *the light shines in the darkness — and yet the darkness did not κατέλαβεν it.* The whole verdict hangs on that "and yet."

## The verb — the darkness did not know the light

κατέλαβεν is bivalent, and the bivalence is not a difficulty to be resolved away. The lexicon itself is double: the standard grammatical analysis glosses the verb "seize, grasp, comprehend; also overtake, suppress," though not every philologist grants the two senses equal footing: Abbott holds that the verb's core is to *catch* or *take*, and that it "never means 'overcome' except so far as that may be implied in 'catching.'" Carson makes the point about the lexicon exactly: the standard lexical entry's "master" *preserves* the ambiguity rather than deciding it.[^bivalence] So the verb arrives carrying two senses, and the exegetical question is not which one to discard but how they stand to each other.

This commentary reads both, with the *comprehend* register foregrounded — and the foregrounding is where the case is strongest. What the darkness above all did not do was *know* the light.

The compositional argument is Ridderbos's, and it is the strongest of the several. Read as "the darkness has not *understood* it," verse 5b is the reading that best connects to what the Prologue itself goes on to say — to the witness who came so that all might believe (v. 7), to the world that "knew him not" (οὐκ ἔγνω, v. 10), to his own who "received him not" (οὐ παρέλαβον, v. 11). The verdict of 5b, on this reading, is the proleptic statement of the rejection the whole Gospel will narrate; the darkness is the field of the reader's own present, casting its shadow forward in unbelief.[^ridderbos] It is not an isolated remark about a cosmic contest. It is the first sounding of the Gospel's own theme of non-recognition.

Three further witnesses converge on the comprehend register, and — this matters for how much weight the reading can bear — each arrives by a different road. Cyril of Alexandria grounds it in the earliest Greek reception: "'Darkness' is what John calls the nature that lacks illumination, that is, the whole originate nature" — the creature that has no light of its own, in which the Son shines "even though the darkness has no idea of the light's existence." That is the same Cyril whose Word at the previous clause is not only "truly light" but "the giver of light to all whom he infuses with the light of understanding"; the light that gives light is the light the darkened creature does not recognize.[^cyril] Schnackenburg reaches it from the Jewish background and from the Prologue's own vocabulary: he reads καταλαμβάνω as "grasp," not "overcome," and equates 1:5b's οὐ κατέλαβεν directly with οὐκ ἔγνω of verse 10 and οὐ παρέλαβον of verse 11.[^schnackenburg] And Boyarin reaches it from the composition's sound. His datum is a chain of words built on the same root, running through the Prologue as a *Leitwort*: κατέλαβεν (v. 5), παρέλαβον (v. 11, "his own received him not"), ἔλαβον (v. 12, "as many as received him"), ἐλάβομεν (v. 16, "of his fullness we all received"). Heard within that chain, the darkness's failure at verse 5 is a failure of *reception* — and it is precisely that failure which makes the Baptist's witness necessary at verse 6.[^boyarin]

Bultmann supplies the reception-face of the same refusal. He reads κατέλαβεν as "grasp, make one's own," and he is explicit that he does not read it as "overcome" or "attack." The reading is of a piece with his darkness: if the σκοτία is a revolt against the light and not a substance — a mode of human existence rather than a cosmic stuff — then what it fails at is appropriation, not battle. That is not a fourth sense standing beside the others; it is what the comprehend register looks like from the side of the one who does not receive.[^bultmann]

Barth reaches the same verdict by a road none of these takes, and on the reading this page foregrounds he is the most emphatic voice the scholarly record affords. His route is dogmatic-exegetical: the darkness's failure to comprehend is a want of *receptivity*, and receptivity is precisely what the Prologue goes on to declare is given rather than native (vv. 12–13). "V. 5b confirms from the other side, from the human side, that the darkness has not comprehended the light. It had no power to appropriate it, to make it its own, to cease to be darkness and itself to become light. This is how we must translate and understand the statement."[^barth]

## The other half — nor could it put the light out

The overcome register is really present too, and it is not a concession made for the sake of tidiness. It is carried by John's own usage. The only other place in this Gospel where the verb meets light and darkness is 12:35, and there it plainly means *overtake*: περιπατεῖτε ὡς τὸ φῶς ἔχετε, ἵνα μὴ σκοτία ὑμᾶς καταλάβῃ — "walk while you have the light, so that the darkness does not overtake you."[^twelve] A reader who has the whole Gospel in hand cannot hear 1:5b without that sentence behind it.

The transfer from 12:35 to this verse is contested, however, and by a substantial voice. Barth states the 12:35 sense himself, marking that it is the very same verb, and then rules it "not possible here." He is more measured about the other form of the overcome reading, judging the conquest construal "too uncertain" and disruptive of the context while granting that the sense is "excellent … in itself" — the two refusals are not of one force, and the difference is worth keeping. And what he refuses is a *locus*, not the substance: he grants in the same volume that the darkness is "opposed and defeated" and that "the Word is mightier than the world," locating that at the ἐξουσία of verses 12–13 rather than at this verb. Bultmann, reading the verb as "grasp," blocks the same transfer from another direction. This commentary holds the transfer — under the guard set out below — and holds it knowing what stands against it.[^contested]

And the reading has a long and weighty line behind it: Dodd, who reads the clause on two levels at once — a world that "does not relapse into not-being, because light is stronger than darkness," and an opposition to Jesus that, "even when it seemed to triumph in the crucifixion, failed to conquer Him"; Lincoln, for whom the darkness simply cannot extinguish the light; Robertson, who prefers "overtook, overcame" over the Vulgate's *comprehenderunt* on the strength of the Greek; Westcott, who reads "overcame it not" and fixes it by John's own usage and the Greek Fathers against the Latin; Morris, for whom the darkness — "the evil environment" — was unable to overcome the light; and Brown, who translates "the darkness did not overcome it" while cataloguing the verb's several tendencies in his note.[^overcome] The patristic reception carries the same weight and is itself split along the verb's own fork: Gregory of Nazianzus's light, which "is chased by the darkness but is not overtaken by it"; Origen's darkness that goes on the offensive and fails, whether outrun or ambushed; Ambrose's man who thinks the dark will hide him and cannot escape the light shining in it — with Cyril alone on the comprehend side. The Fathers heard both senses from the first, and the division among them is not a confusion to be corrected but evidence that the verb was always carrying two things.[^fathers]

But — and this is the guard the previous tread set in place — the "overcome" that is affirmed here is *not* the clash of two cosmic principles on equal terms. Under the modified, non-ultimate dualism established at [verse 5a](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/08-cosmology-light-in-darkness/), the darkness has no independent standing over against God; it is the bounded condition of the creature. So the failure of 1:5b is not a battle narrowly won. It is the *failed mastery* of something that was never the light's equal — which is why the same sources who read "overcome" most strongly are, several of them, the very sources who set the guard. Dodd, who reads the overcome sense at full strength, is also the one who states outright that the dualism of this verse is not ultimate. Lincoln, who reads "overcome" rather than "comprehend," is also the one who places John within a Jewish contrast that was already modified, God on the side of light and ultimately in control. The guard and the reading come from the same hands.[^guard]

Here, too, is where the earlier page's unfinished business is settled. At verse 5a this commentary described the picture as *coexistence* — the light present within a darkness still dark — and noted that Lincoln, on that same page of his commentary, writes that "there can be no peaceful coexistence between such darkness and the light." That sentence is lodged in his gloss of *this* clause, on his way to the statement that the darkness "is not able to prevail," and it is at this clause that it is properly met. What Lincoln denies is a *truce* — a settlement between matched powers, each holding its ground. This commentary denies one too, and denies it here rather than at 5a: the darkness does not hold its ground, because it never had ground of its own to hold. What 5a claimed was a *presence*, not a parity; what 5b now adds is that the presence is unassailable. Read in that order, Lincoln's sentence is not a divergence from the reading but the negative form of it.[^truce]

So the two senses do not compete; they complete each other. The condition that will not *know* the light also cannot *extinguish* it, and both are the one failure of a bounded darkness before the light that made it. That is the reading a broad range of the commentary tradition has arrived at independently. Carson calls verse 5 "a masterpiece of planned ambiguity" and concludes that John, "subtle writer that he is, wants his readers to see in the Word both the light of creation and the light of the redemption the Word brings in his incarnation." Keener puts it as the verb's own metaphor: the darkness could neither grasp the light with the mind nor grasp it with the hand. Barrett names it a deliberate double sense — the darkness "neither understood nor quenched" the light. McHugh chooses his very translation to catch it, rendering the clause "the darkness has never become *master* of it," a phrase built to hold both. Koester reaches it from the symbol's behavior, Pagels from the Genesis-exegesis side, calling it "a well-known double entendre" that "can be translated either way." Six routes, one destination; and where six independent methods converge, the reading is about as secure as an exegetical judgment of this kind becomes.[^composite]

## The tense — one finished verdict against an unfinished shining

The tense is the composition's final stroke, and it is doing something the sense of the verb alone cannot do.

Against the layered present φαίνει of verse 5a — the light that *keeps on* shining, open-ended — οὐ κατέλαβεν stands in the aorist, and it is read here as *constative*. The constative aorist gathers a whole extended state of affairs into a single completed fact: in the phrase Waetjen borrows from Moule, "a line reduced to a point by perspective," viewing the prolonged struggle and the continuous victory as one thing. The punctiliar reading — one moment in which the darkness tried and failed — is foreclosed by it, and so is the gnomic. Carson points the same way, holding that the aorist here "merely establishes that the writer looks on the action holistically, not that the action took place at a specific time," and McHugh states the negative directly: the aorist at 1:5b is expressly *not* gnomic, not a maxim about how light and darkness behave in general. Morris, from a different angle, reads it as pointing to a single occasion and "true on more than one level" — a decisive summary rather than a timeless rule.[^constative]

The constative is exactly the instrument this verdict requires, because it is the one verb-form that can say, across the entire span of the darkness's opportunity, that it *neither* understood *nor* mastered the light. Not on one occasion. Not as a general principle. As a completed fact about the whole of it.

And the contrast of the two tenses is itself the point the unit has been composing toward. The light's shining is ongoing and unfinished; the darkness's response is bounded and done. Waetjen states the compositional priority plainly: verse 5a is "the prior and dominant reality," the imperfective present outranking the aorist. The unit's [full temporal architecture](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/03-macro-tense-run/) — the protological imperfect grounding the eternal frame, the marked present setting the unit's own time, and this constative aorist summarizing the darkness's failure against that ongoing present — comes to its resolution in this last verb.[^priority]

The tradition even preserves a trace that the decisive note was heard in this unit from the first. Gregory of Nyssa reads verse 5a not with the present φαίνει but with an aorist, ἔλαμψε — "shone" — a reading found in no New Testament manuscript and original to him. It does not disturb [the working text](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/01-the-text/), which remains φαίνει. But it is evidence of an early reception that heard the whole verse in a once-for-all register, and it sits naturally beside the constative that closes it.[^nyssa]

The soteriology tread — and with it the whole unit — thus lands on a note of quiet triumph, and it is the reason the passage matters where the introduction said it would. The light of the world shines within the darkness, and the darkness can do neither of the two things that would end its shining: it can neither understand the light and so possess it, nor master the light and so put it out. The fallen condition that does not recognize its Maker cannot, for all its not-knowing, overcome the light that goes on shining within it.

This is the seed of the summons the Gospel will press — "walk while you have the light, lest the darkness overtake you" (12:35) — and it is the first note of the victory the whole Gospel will narrate to its end. Before any of the drama of belief and rejection is shown, before the cross and before the empty tomb, the Prologue has already disclosed the outcome. The darkness did not overcome it. From that finished verdict the Gospel's whole story of light and dark unfolds.

[^guard]: The guard is the modified, non-ultimate dualism established at the previous clause and not re-argued here. Its consequence at this clause is specific: the "overcome" affirmed at 1:5b is the failed mastery of a bounded condition, not the defeat of a rival principle. That several of the strongest witnesses for the overcome reading are also the sources who set the guard is what makes the combination stable rather than strained — Dodd, *Interpretation*, p. 36, in the treatment of the Hermetic literature, where the sentence is stated outright — "In the Fourth Gospel the cosmological dualism of i. 5 is not ultimate, since everything came into being through the Logos" — and where he glosses οὐ κατέλαβεν "did not overpower it," citing 12:35, in the same paragraph; with p. 112 on the non-identity of Johannine Christianity with the Gnostic systems, where "the difference … emerges the more clearly because the terminology is so largely similar", and Lincoln, *Saint John*, p. 99 (John located within a developed Jewish contrast in which "God, on the side of light, is ultimately in control"). Neither reads a contest between equals, and neither has to be corrected in order to be used here.

[^truce]: Lincoln, *Saint John*, p. 99: "There can be no peaceful coexistence between such darkness and the light," in the sentences of his κατέλαβεν gloss, on his way to the statement that the darkness "is not able to prevail." The sentence was noted at the previous clause and is answered here, where it belongs: it denies a settlement between powers, which this commentary also denies, and it does not deny the light's presence within its field, which is what the previous clause claimed. The distinction is drawn explicitly rather than left for the reader, because the word *coexistence* is used on that page for the picture Lincoln uses the same word to reject, and a reader with Lincoln open will meet the collision before meeting the resolution.

[^composite]: Carson, *John*, pp. 119–120: 1:5 "a masterpiece of planned ambiguity" (p. 119), καταλαμβάνειν read both as overcome or extinguish and as understand or grasp, with his own conclusion at p. 120: "it is quite possible that John, subtle writer that he is, wants his readers to see in the Word both the light of creation and the light of the redemption the Word brings in his incarnation." The short form "John likely intends both" circulates as a quotation of Carson and is not his sentence; it is not used here. Craig S. Keener, *The Gospel of John: A Commentary* (Peabody: Hendrickson, 2003), p. 387: a deliberate double entendre, the darkness able neither to comprehend the light (grasp with the mind) nor to overcome it (grasp with the hand). C. K. Barrett, *The Gospel according to St John*, 2nd ed. (London: SPCK, 1978), pp. 158–159: a "deliberate double sense" — the darkness "neither understood nor quenched" the light. John F. McHugh, *A Critical and Exegetical Commentary on John 1–4* (International Critical Commentary; London: T&T Clark, 2009), pp. 17–20, rendering the clause "the darkness has never become *master* of it," a translation chosen to catch both the overpowered sense of the Greek commentators since Origen and the understood or embraced sense; on the ambiguous Latin *comprehenderunt* he notes that it "perhaps indicates that both meanings were thought to have been intended by the writer," a judgment he reports several modern scholars (notably Barrett) affirming. Craig R. Koester, *Symbolism in the Fourth Gospel*, 2nd ed. (Minneapolis: Fortress, 2003), pp. 143–147, reading the double sense out of the symbol's function and cashing the comprehension-sense through 1:10 — which is also independent support for the noetic foregrounding. Elaine Pagels, "Exegesis of Genesis 1 in the Gospels of Thomas and John," *Journal of Biblical Literature* 118 (1999), p. 490: "has not understood — or overcome — it," "a well-known double entendre" that "can be translated either way," framed as the Prologue's first negation. Harris, *John* (EGGNT), pp. 23–24, holds both senses "suitable" and probably jointly intended. The convergence is counted as multiple independent routes because the methods differ — verse-internal analysis, the verb's metaphorical structure, translational judgment, symbolic function, and the history of Genesis exegesis — rather than as one reading repeated.

[^constative]: Herman C. Waetjen, "Logos πρὸς τὸν θεόν and the Objectification of Truth in the Prologue of the Fourth Gospel," *Catholic Biblical Quarterly* 63 (2001): 265–286, at pp. 271–272: κατέλαβεν a constative aorist — in C. F. D. Moule's phrase, which Waetjen adopts, "a line reduced to a point by perspective" — viewing the prolonged struggle and the continuous victory as a single fact, and rejecting the punctiliar reading. Carson, *John*, p. 138, on the aorist: "That the tense is aorist is of little significance in itself, since that tense merely establishes that the writer looks on the action holistically, not that the action took place at a specific time, more than once, instantaneously or the like." The short form "not time-significant" circulates as a quotation of Carson at this point and is not his wording; his own sentence, which says more, is given instead — "holistically" being the aspectual claim the constative reading rests on. McHugh, *John 1–4*, pp. 17–20, expressly against a gnomic reading of the aorist here (contra Moulton–Howard–Turner III.73) — the direct primary-text support for the constative against the one alternative that would otherwise fit. Morris, *John*, p. 77, reading the aorist as pointing to "a single occasion" and "true on more than one level": his time-reference is a separate question, but the aspectual note is a decisive-summary reading and corroborates the constative. Robertson and Zerwick supply the second-aorist parse beneath all of it. Waetjen is engaged here on the aspect of the aorist only; his rendering of the verb's sense is given without argument and carries no weight in the lexical discussion above.

[^priority]: Waetjen, "Logos πρὸς τὸν θεόν," pp. 271–272: verse 5a is "the prior and dominant reality," the imperfective present outranking the constative aorist compositionally. The ranking is not merely a stylistic observation. It is what keeps the unit from closing on a defensive note: the last word about the darkness is a completed fact, while the light's shining is left grammatically open, and the composition puts the open thing above the closed one.

[^nyssa]: Gregory of Nyssa reads verse 5a with the singular aorist ἔλαμψε(ν), "shone," against φαίνει in all New Testament manuscripts — a reading original to him in the collation. Ronald E. Roades, *The Text of the Gospel of John in the Writings of Gregory of Nyssa* (MA thesis, Wheaton College, 1974), p. 18; superseding James A. Brooks, *The New Testament Text of Gregory of Nyssa* (New Testament in the Greek Fathers 2; Atlanta: SBL, 1991), p. 106, which omits the unit and treats the singular ἔλαμψε as influence from 2 Corinthians 4:6. The working text of this commentary remains φαίνει, as set out in the text-critical treatment of the unit; the datum bears on how the tenses of the verse were *heard* in the early reception, not on the text adopted, and it is recorded here because an early reader who took 5a in the aorist was hearing the whole verse in the once-for-all register that the constative of 5b supplies.
