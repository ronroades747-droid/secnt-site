The unit's shape is one thing; its time is another. The [staircase of terms](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/02-gradatio-and-foyer/) shows what the unit *is* — four treads standing in a fixed relation, life to light to darkness to the darkness's failure. It does not show what the unit *does* in time. That is carried by a second feature, running beneath the same four treads and just as deliberate: the sequence of the verbs. This section takes that sequence on its own, and only that. How the two features are held together — for they are neither alternatives nor duplicates — is the burden of the section [after this one](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/04-coordinate-articulation/). Nothing here re-argues the clause-level readings; those are settled, and are set out in the exegesis that follows.

## Three forms, and the break at verse 5

Read for its verbs alone, the unit runs:

> ἦν, ἦν (4) → φαίνει (5a) → κατέλαβεν (5b)

*in him* ***was*** *life, and the life* ***was*** *the light of men — and the light* ***shines*** *in the darkness — and the darkness* ***did not overcome*** *it.*

Imperfect, imperfect; then present; then aorist. The first thing to see is how hard the third of these breaks with what precedes it. By the time the reader reaches verse 5, the Prologue has said ἦν six times — three times in verse 1, once in verse 2, twice in verse 4 — and has not once used a present indicative. Between the imperfects stand the verbs of *becoming*: the aorist ἐγένετο, twice in verse 3, naming creation's coming-to-be, and the perfect ὃ γέγονεν, which — on the punctuation this commentary reads — stands at the seam and opens verse 4 as its subject, naming not the act of creation but its standing result. Being and becoming, then, and nothing else; no verb has yet stood in the present. φαίνει is the Prologue's first. Morris makes the narrower observation from the side of the Word's own predicates: the present tense is never used of the Word "with but one exception, 'shines' in verse 5."[^break]

<!-- diagram -->

That break is the datum on which everything in this section rests, and it is a compositional datum rather than an interpretive one: the forms are what they are, and the count is what it is. What the commentary adds to the count is the judgment that the shift is *composed* — a deliberate stroke, not an accident of predication forced on the evangelist by what he happened to be saying. That judgment is the reading of the strongest witnesses on the tenses. Ridderbos takes the imperfects of verses 1 through 4 as the "grand background" against which the present of verse 5 flashes out, refuses to end the first cycle of ideas at verse 4, and reads the imperfect as being seen "from the perspective of one who looks at the present in light of its origin" — a reading on which verses 1 through 5 are one continuous stretch and verse 5a its kerygmatic flash. Michaels marks the same shift more briefly and more sharply: at φαίνει "the writer returns to his own time, passing over the whole Old Testament period in silence."[^composed]

Barth — who will be read against below on what the run finally means — is among the clearest witnesses that the shift is the thing needing explanation. He raises it in exactly those terms: "the change from *ēn* to *phainei*, which is surprising at first glance." And he answers it not by explaining the shift away but by showing that the evangelist does this elsewhere: "we have already seen from v. 3b how flexible the tenses are for the author. We shall find further examples in the prologue when we come to v. 9 and v. 15a." He states the force of the Johannine present directly — "in John the present always has the ring of the special actuality of the event denoted by the verb in question."[^barth] That is a grammatical observation about a Prologue-wide pattern, made by a reader who will draw a different conclusion from it than this commentary draws; the observation is received here entire.

And the run is not only this unit's. It is the unit-level instance of a pattern the whole Prologue has been running — the alternation of ἦν with ἐγένετο and ὃ γέγονεν, of eternal being with creaturely becoming, which this commentary settled in its reading of [John 1:1–3](/commentary/john/1-1-to-3/13-creational-pivot/) and does not re-argue here. What verses 4 and 5 do is take that Prologue-wide pattern and complete it: the being-imperfect returns at verse 4a, after the aorists of verse 3, and is then broken — for the first time — by a present. The unit does not merely sit on the spine; it is the point at which the spine turns.

## What the run locates in time

Each of the three forms does a distinct temporal work, and the three works are not the same kind of thing.

**The imperfect grounds.** The ἦν of verse 4 is not a narrative past reporting something that once was and is no longer. It carries the incomplete, durative force of a standing state, and it names that state at the eternal-frame register — the life that *was* in the Word, before and above all created experience. Its force is treated where the clause is expounded, in the [ontology](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/06-ontology-life-in-him/) of verse 4a; what matters at the level of the run is its position. The imperfect is the backdrop. It supplies the deep ground against which the next form will register as a change.

**The present sets the unit's time.** φαίνει is the marked form, and its markedness is the unit's compositional engine. Grammatically it is durative — the light, in Robertson's phrase, "keeps on giving light" — and the commentary reads it as holding three frames of one shining together rather than choosing among them: the light that shone at creation, the light that came with Christ, and the light that shines now, in the reader's own darkness. Waetjen's formulation is the exact one the reading needs: φαίνει is "a continuous actuality, but not a timeless presence" — extended in history, not lifted out of it. McHugh reads the same present as shining "perpetually"; Westcott, a century before the aspect debate, put it that "it is of light's essence to invade darkness."[^present] The layered reading is not a refusal to decide. It is the decision — that the evangelist, having a set of tenses available for locating an event, chose the one form that would not locate it at a single point. What the light *is* in that shining, and what darkness it shines in, belong to the [cosmology](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/08-cosmology-light-in-darkness/) of verse 5a.

**The aorist summarizes.** κατέλαβεν is constative — not a snapshot of one moment but a whole extent of time viewed as a single fact, in Moule's image "a line reduced to a point by perspective." What it gathers into that point is the darkness's entire response across the whole reach of the shining: the prolonged struggle and the light's continuous victory, taken together as one completed thing. The sense of the verb — the darkness neither mastering nor comprehending the light — is the question of the [soteriology](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/09-soteriology-not-overcome/) of verse 5b.[^aorist]

Set the three side by side and the striking feature is the order. The natural expectation is that a narrative moves from the finished to the continuing; here the movement is the reverse. The unit's continuing verb comes second and its completed verb comes last, so that the shining is left standing in the present while the darkness's opposition is closed off in the past. The light *shines*; the darkness *did not* overcome. That inversion is the run's whole theological point in miniature, and it is why the aorist is the unit's last and most weighted word rather than an anticlimax after it.

## What the reading holds, and what it declines

Four narrower readings of this material are live in the literature, and the commentary's position is best shown by where it puts each of them rather than by a list of refutations.

That the aorist points to a *decisive occasion* — creation, the Fall, or Calvary — is a real reading with real support; Morris advances it and, in the same breath, relativizes it, holding that the verse is "true on more than one level," and Brown reads the same assurance as holding at both the first sin in the world and the night of Jesus' passion. The commentary receives the decisive occasion as a *foregrounded* frame, given full weight, but not as the whole reference: a constative aorist summarizes decisive moments without being reducible to any one of them.[^placed] That the reference is *distributed* — the whole arc, creation to now — is likewise received, as the reading's reach rather than its shape; taken alone it flattens a marked sequence into an undifferentiated "always." And that the present is the *evangelist's own vantage*, his contemporary proclamation, is received as the pole the present's markedness pushes forward — Ridderbos, Michaels, Bultmann and Menken are all reading something really there — without foreshortening the creational reach behind it.

The fourth reading is the one the commentary genuinely reads against, and it needs a distinction to state fairly. Carson notes that the aorist is "not time-significant," and he is right: Greek tense here is a matter of *aspect* — kind of action — before it is a matter of chronology, and the durative present and constative aorist are aspectual forms before they are temporal ones. That is the floor beneath everything above, and this commentary stands on it rather than against it; Carson's own reading of the unit as a deliberate ambiguity converges with the layered account rather than opposing it. But Barrett and Barth claim more than the aspectual floor. Barrett takes the present to name "an eternal property of the Light to shine, not a historical event"; Barth concludes that in these verses "obviously no one time is here marked off from another." Against that stronger denial the commentary's reading operates at full strength — and not only for the sake of verse 5a, since a shining lifted out of time takes the darkness's failure with it: if the light's shining is an eternal property rather than an event, then the darkness's not overcoming it is equally out of time, and the unit's last clause is read accordingly.[^against]

Barth's case is the finer of the two, and answering it requires saying exactly where the disagreement falls, because it does not fall on his grammar. His paragraph at verse 4 moves in three steps. The first is a grammatical observation: "the imperfect *ēn* includes a present here, as may be seen from the *phainei* that follows in the next verse" — the tenses reach into one another, and the imperfect is not sealed off in a closed past. That is received here entire; it is the very interpenetration a layered reading is built on. The third is a theological conclusion: "obviously no one time is here marked off from another." That is declined. And the step that carries him from the one to the other is the second: the past may stand in translation, he says, "only so long as by the past we do not understand a specific time but all past history." There the argument turns *reach* into indifference to *frames* — because the tenses extend beyond their own moment, no moment is distinguished at all. That inference is what the commentary declines, and declines it on Barth's own evidence: a writer who marks a shift as surprising, and who is watching the same flexibility at verses 3b, 9, and 15a, is a writer attending to *which* form stands *where*. The frames interpenetrate and are marked off. That is what a layered architecture is, and a deliberate sequence of three different forms is the evidence that the evangelist distinguished them.

Two strengths at two confidences, then, as with the unit's [standing shape](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/02-gradatio-and-foyer/). That the run is a composed feature of these two verses, and that it refers in time in the layered way described, is held with as much confidence as a reading of this kind admits — not because any single datum compels it but because independent lines converge on it: the grammarians on the aspect of the forms, the commentators who read the shift as deliberate, and the requirement of the unit's own clause-level readings, which do not cohere without it. The further claim — that this run is a component of a deliberately composed Prologue-wide pattern of tenses — is strong and load-bearing, but it reaches past this unit into material the commentary has not yet treated, and it is held provisionally until the rest of the Prologue is read. As with the Foyer, the distinction is not a hedge but a disclosure.

That the light which shone at creation, came with Christ, and shines now is *one* light, and its life one life, is the theological reach of this run; it is drawn out where the commentary gathers [the one life traced from creation to the Word made flesh](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/14-one-life-arc/), not here. What remains for the architecture is the question this section has deliberately left open. Two features have now been described, each on its own terms — a standing shape and a moving spine, on the same four treads. Neither is a version of the other, and neither was argued from the other's evidence. [How they are held together](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/04-coordinate-articulation/) is the next question, and the answer is not that one explains the other.
