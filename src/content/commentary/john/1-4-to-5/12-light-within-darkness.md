The second tread asked what the human creature must be that a divine light could be called *its* light, and answered that humanity is the creature [made to receive it](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/11-image-and-light-of-men/). The third tread turns to where that light is actually found. Not in a clear-eyed creature looking up, but shining in the dark — and so this tread yields a doctrine of the darkness quite as much as a doctrine of the light.

What the church has had to confess here is, at its centre, a *denial*. It is one of those places where the most important thing a doctrine does is rule something out.

## The denial the doctrine turns on

The denial is this: **the darkness of John's Gospel is not a second ultimate.**

That sounds like a small thing to insist on until one notices how naturally the opposite reading suggests itself. The verse sets two nouns against each other, light and darkness, with a verb between them; the Gospel will go on setting them against each other for twenty-one chapters; and the human mind, presented with two opposed powers, reaches almost immediately for the picture of a balanced contest — two eternal principles, a good and an evil, contending on equal terms, the outcome genuinely in doubt. That picture is very old, it is emotionally satisfying, and it is not what John is doing.

There are not two ultimates in this Gospel. There is the light through whom everything that exists came to be, and there is the darkness of the creature that has turned away from him. Those are not two of a kind. One of them made the other's existence possible.

Everything else on this page is an unpacking of that sentence.

## What John inherited, and what he did with it

John did not invent the light-and-darkness contrast, and it matters that he did not, because the shape of what he inherited already contains the denial.

Lincoln sets out the background: the contrast "is one that would also have been familiar from Jewish tradition, whether in the creation narrative of Gen. 1:3–5, in apocalyptic writings (cf. e.g. 1 Enoch 89.8) or, in more developed form, in the Qumran literature (cf. e.g. 1QM with its war between the sons of light and the sons of darkness)."[^lincoln] That is a real dualism, and its language is martial — a war, sons of light, sons of darkness. It would be easy to conclude that John has simply taken it over.

But Lincoln puts his finger on what the developed Jewish form had already done to it: "light and darkness came to represent the two sides of a modified dualism in which God, on the side of light, is ultimately in control and will triumph in the end. The Fourth Gospel, in which 'darkness' (σκοτία) occurs eight times, shares this perspective."[^lincoln]

*Modified* is the operative word, and the reason it is modified is worth stating plainly, because it is not a matter of degree. A dualism is modified when the two sides are not matched — and these two are not matched, because one of them is God's. The war language survives; the metaphysical symmetry does not. There is a conflict, and it is real, and it was never a contest between equals.

The same qualification runs back through the Old Testament material. McHugh notes that in the Hebrew scriptures "darkness is one of the constituent elements of the initial chaos (Gen 1:2) which is not annihilated, but brought under control by God and turned into a useful and subordinate part of his creation," and that "the realm of darkness too is subject to God's will, because he is its creator (Ps 139:11–12)."[^mchugh] Even at the opening of Genesis, where the darkness is most nearly a cosmic thing, it is a creature's darkness and it is God's to govern.

So John writes at the head of a dualistic-sounding tradition without writing a dualism. He is not a man importing a foreign framework; he is a man using an inherited one whose crucial qualification was already in place.

## Real — and not a nature

The denial can be pushed too far in the other direction, and the second thing to say is that the darkness is entirely real.

It is not an illusion, not a shortfall of information, not a stage of development the race will grow out of. [The exegesis found it to be the fallen human condition seen at once cosmically, morally, and noetically](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/08-cosmology-light-in-darkness/) — the created order under the absence of light, the darkness of sin, the ignorance of a world that does not know its Maker. Anyone who has watched a human being do the thing they know to be wrong, or a whole society agree not to see what is in front of it, has met this darkness and knows it is not nothing.

What it is *not* is a substance. Bultmann puts it in a sentence: "darkness is neither a substance nor the sheer power of fate; it is nothing other than the revolt against the light."[^bultmann] Notice what that formulation refuses. It refuses to make the dark a stuff — some dark material the world is partly made of, which would put it into the account of creation and so into the Word's own work. And it refuses to make it a fate — an impersonal force bearing down on people from outside, which would make the darkness something that happens *to* us rather than something we do. Revolt is neither. Revolt is a posture of a creature, and a creature that has taken a posture can take another one.

The Fathers reached that same point along a different road, and Origen states it as flatly as anyone ever has. Commenting on this very clause, he denies that people are dark by nature: "men are not such by nature, since Paul says, 'For we were once darkness, but now are light in the Lord.'"[^origen] The argument is worth following, because it is not a pious aside. Origen is writing against people who held that human beings come in fixed spiritual kinds — that some are constitutionally light and others constitutionally dark, and that nothing can be done about which one you are. His answer is the apostle's own biography. Paul *was* darkness. Paul is now light. Therefore darkness is not a nature, because a nature is precisely the thing that cannot be swapped. "If it is true that darkness has become light," he asks, "why cannot all darkness become light?"[^origen]

That is the non-ultimacy claim stated at the level of the individual person rather than the cosmos, and it is the more searching of the two. A darkness that is a nature is a sentence. A darkness that is a revolt is a condition — real, culpable, and reversible.

## Grounded, finally, in the third verse

None of the above would be decisive if it rested only on the temperament of the tradition. It rests on something the Prologue has already said.

Dodd locates the ground exactly, and it is upstream of our verse: "In the Fourth Gospel the cosmological dualism of i. 5 is not ultimate, since everything came into being through the Logos"; the emphatic negative of the third verse — that without him not one thing came to be — "would seem to imply that the evangelist is ruling out a view that something existent did come into being without the Logos, e.g. the primeval ocean of darkness."[^dodd]

This is the argument the whole doctrine hangs on, and it is beautifully economical. If nothing whatever came into being apart from the Word, then whatever the darkness is, it is not a thing that came into being apart from him. There is no room left in the sentence for a co-eternal dark. The Prologue closed that door two verses before it opened the subject, and it closed it not by denying the darkness but by making a universal claim about origins that has the denial as a consequence.

Our research concludes, with a high degree of confidence, that this is the right ordering of the argument: the non-ultimacy of the darkness in John is not a mood, an instinct, or a later theological tidying. It is an entailment of the doctrine of creation the Prologue states in verse three. Three independent lines of scholarship arrive at the same guard by different routes — one by clearing away the mythology, one by reading John against Greek religious speculation, one by placing him inside the Jewish contrast that had already been qualified — and their convergence is what carries the reading. No one of them is asked to hold it alone.

## Within — and still dark

Now the payoff, and it is the sharpest pastoral turn in the unit.

[The exegesis settled that the preposition is locative](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/08-cosmology-light-in-darkness/): the light shines *within* the darkness as the sphere it inhabits, not *at* it as a hostile object struck from a safe distance. Set that beside the denial we have just made and the doctrine comes into focus. The darkness is not an ultimate — and the light does not treat it as one. It does not besiege it. It does not stand off from it and shout across the gap. It enters it, and shines there, in the very condition of the people who do not know him.

And the dark stays dark. McHugh draws the consequence from John's own choice of preposition: "By writing not ἐπί but ἐν, he stresses that the darkness remains, undispelled by the light."[^mchugh] A different preposition would have said the light shone *on* or *over* the darkness and dispelled it. This one says the light is *in* it, and the dark is still there. The shining is not a single flash that ends the night; on McHugh's reading, "the light is perpetually shining, just as the darkness always remains."[^mchugh]

Westcott, writing a century and a half ago, saw that this is a paradox and refused to soften it: "The whole phrase is indeed a startling paradox. The light does not banish the darkness: the darkness does not overpower the light. Light and darkness coexist in the world side by side."[^westcott] Both halves of that are load-bearing. Take away the first and you have a triumphalism the verse does not support. Take away the second and you have a despair it explicitly denies.

Augustine put the same coexistence from the creature's side, and his image is the one that stays with people. Think of a blind man standing in full sun: "the sun is present to him, but he is absent from the sun."[^fathers] Nothing is wrong with the light. The light is fully there, undiminished, shining exactly as it shines on everyone else. The failure is all on the other side — and that is the picture of a world in which the Word is present and unrecognised.

## Neither optimism nor despair

Put the denial and the location together and the doctrine has a shape a person can actually live in. It rules out two errors, and they are the two that people actually make.

The first is a naïve optimism — the belief that because the light has come, the darkness is essentially over, and what remains is mopping up. The text will not carry it. The darkness *remains*. It is not a shadow retreating on a schedule; it is the standing condition of a world that has turned from its Maker, and it is still, at this hour, exactly as dark as it looks. A Christianity that cannot say that will keep being ambushed by the news.

The second is a dualist despair — the belief that the dark is simply how things are, a permanent counterweight, and that the light is a small brave candle in a very large night. The text will not carry that either. The darkness is not ultimate; it did not come into being apart from the Word; it is not a nature but a revolt; and the one thing the fifth verse will go on to say about it is that it did not win.

Between those two the verse holds a third thing, and it is harder than either because it refuses the consolation each of them offers. The dark is real *and* it is not final. Both at once, with no resolution offered here.

Isaac of Nineveh, writing to people undergoing precisely that, said the darkness they were passing through was not abandonment: "this darkness is brought about by divine providence for reasons that are known only to God," and God "allows it to emerge very soon from the darkness."[^fathers] That is pastoral counsel rather than exegesis, and it is offered as such — but it is the counsel that follows from the doctrine, and it is worth noticing that the Fathers who read this verse most closely did not use it to promise anyone that the dark would lift by Tuesday. They used it to say that the dark is not the last word about you.

## The register of the claim

This locus is theological illumination drawn from the clause; it is not part of what the clause was framed to establish. The exegetical weight at "the light shines in the darkness" rests on the force of the present tense, on what the darkness is, and on the sense of the preposition — [and that is where the argument was carried](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/08-cosmology-light-in-darkness/).[^settled] What this page adds is what the church has confessed on the strength of it.

The sourcing is worth naming plainly, because the convergence is doing real work and convergence can be faked by counting the same voice twice. Bultmann arrives at the guard by dismantling the mythological framework he thinks the Gospel's source inherited; Dodd arrives at it by testing John against Greek religious speculation and finding the decisive difference; Lincoln arrives at it by placing John inside a Jewish contrast already qualified from within. The first two exclude; the third positions. They do not agree about much else, and their independence is the point. Origen and the Fathers reach it from a fourth direction entirely — a pastoral argument about whether a person can change — and the patristic material here is read at that register: as reception, not as grammar.

One qualification the page owes. Origen's sentence comes to most readers through a modern anthology of patristic comment, and the anthology's editors gave it a heading of their own. It is cited above from his commentary itself, in the standard English translation, so that what stands inside the quotation marks is his.

## The shape of grace this tread sets before the reader

What the third tread teaches the church to confess is that the light is at home in the dark place without being conquered by it.

That is a strange sentence, and it should be. It is not what we would have written. We would have had the light arrive and the darkness flee, which is the shape of every story we like; or we would have had a long war with an uncertain end, which is the shape of every story we believe. John gives neither. He gives a light that goes and stands inside the condition of the people who cannot see it, and keeps shining there, and does not leave.

Whatever else that is, it is the shape of grace — grace before the word has been used, before the Word has been said to become flesh, three verses early and already unmistakable. The eternal light does not require the dark to clear before it will come. It comes into the dark. And it is precisely because the darkness is not ultimate that it can afford to.

Which leaves the question the verse has been building toward from its first word, and the only one still outstanding. The light shines in the dark. What, then, did the dark *do* with it?

The sentence runs "For darkness is neither a substance nor the sheer power of fate; it is nothing other than the revolt against the light," and Bultmann goes on to say that the Gospel's reading of the world's darkness "as the constant revolt and hostility against God" is what finds mythological expression in the figure of the devil. The sentence is Dodd's own assertion, made while contrasting John with the Hermetic *Poimandres*, where the primal light does stand over against a chaotic ocean of darkness: "In the Fourth Gospel the cosmological dualism of i. 5 is not ultimate, since everything came into being through the Logos, χωρὶς αὐτοῦ ἐγένετο οὐδὲ ἕν: the emphatic negative would seem to imply that the evangelist is ruling out a view that something existent did come into being without the Logos, e.g. the primeval ocean of darkness." [^origen]: Origen, *Commentary on the Gospel according to John, Books 1–10*, trans. Ronald E. Heine (Fathers of the Church 80; Washington: Catholic University of America Press, 1989), Book 2.134, p. 130: "But whether it is a question of the darkness of men or death, men are not such by nature, since Paul says, 'For we were once darkness, but now are light in the Lord,' and this is especially the case if we are now called saints and spiritual men." The Pauline text is Eph 5:8, which Origen quotes three times across 2.134–136. The argument runs on into 2.135 — "For if the spiritual man was once darkness, what is the earthly? And if it is true that darkness has become light, why cannot all darkness become light?" — and is directed against those "who think that there are spiritual natures," so that the denial is specifically a denial of fixed spiritual kinds. The sentence is often met in a modern anthology of patristic comment under an editorial heading that is not Origen's wording; it is given here from the commentary itself.

[^westcott]: B. F. Westcott, *The Gospel according to St. John* (London: John Murray, 1882), p. 5, in the notes on 1:5. The paradox sentences read "The whole phrase is indeed a startling paradox. The light does not banish the darkness: the darkness does not overpower the light. Light and darkness coexist in the world side by side." On the same page Westcott takes the verse to presuppose the Fall — "An acquaintance with the history of the Fall is evidently presupposed" — and reads the shining as continuous: "This action of the Light is not to be limited to any one point. It is continuous from the creation to the consummation of things, though there have been times when it has flashed forth with peculiar splendour." His phrase for the light's character, "it is of the essence of light to invade the realm of darkness," is read in this commentary within the locative frame rather than against it: it names the light's constant shining within its own field, not a directional force imported into the preposition.
