The exegesis of these two verses works clause by clause, but it does not work in a vacuum. It operates on a unit whose composition is deliberate and, in large part, already established — by the evangelist, and by the readings of the Prologue this commentary has already settled. That composition has two dimensions working at once. The first is *synchronic*: the shape of the unit as a standing whole — the internal staircase of terms, and the unit's placement within the frame the Prologue's opening throws across the whole. The second is *diachronic*: the temporal spine running through the unit, the patterned movement of its verbs from eternal being through present shining to completed act. This section takes the first; the [run of tenses](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/03-macro-tense-run/) follows, and how the two are held together — for they are not alternatives, and not duplicates — is the burden of the section [after that](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/04-coordinate-articulation/). None of this re-argues the clause-level readings; those are settled, and are set out in the exegesis that follows.

## The staircase of terms

The unit is built as a **staircase** — a *gradatio* by *anadiplosis*, in which each clause takes up the last significant term of the clause before it and climbs one step:

> ζωή (4a) → ἡ ζωή … τὸ φῶς (4b) → τὸ φῶς … τῇ σκοτίᾳ (5a) → ἡ σκοτία … οὐ κατέλαβεν (5b)

*in him was* ***life*** *— and the* ***life*** *was the* ***light*** *of men — and the* ***light*** *shines in the* ***darkness*** *— and the* ***darkness*** *did not overcome it.*

<!-- diagram -->

The figure is a textual datum, not an interpreter's overlay. The catchword linkages are on the page — ζωή taken up in 4b, φῶς in 5a, σκοτία in 5b — and they are there independently of how any individual clause is construed. Whatever a reader decides about the force of the imperfect or the sense of ζωή, the three ties remain, and the four clauses remain a rising sequence rather than a list.

What gives the reading its strength, beyond the plain evidence of the catchwords, is that the figure has been registered by readers working at methodologically distinct levels — no one of whom names it at the same register as another. Nässelqvist reads it colometrically, from the line-division of the ancient manuscripts, and finds the ζωή → φῶς → σκοτία chain a *gradatio* "clearly used," displayed line by line. Miller names an explicit verse-level "staircase construction A→B, B→C, C→D" running from the opening clauses of verse 1 across verses 4 and 5. Bultmann identifies the compositional mechanism as a couplet chain-locking by catchword. Brown names the Prologue's "staircase parallelism," a mark of "a far more carefully worked out poetic style." McHugh carries forward Bernard's older description of the same movement as climactic parallelism.[^gradatio] Five different ways of reading the text — colometric, verse-named, mechanism-named, style-characterizing, survey-reception — and all five find the same ascent. That spread is itself worth noting: the figure is robust precisely because it does not depend on any one method to see it.

The staircase rests, as every reading of this unit does, on the working text. It is the punctuation this commentary has adopted — the full stop after οὐδὲ ἕν, which leaves the relative clause ὃ γέγονεν to open verse 4 as the subject of what follows — that lets the *gradatio* begin cleanly, with ὃ γέγονεν ἐν αὐτῷ ζωὴ ἦν standing as a balanced line that closes on ζωή and passes it directly to καὶ ἡ ζωὴ ἦν τὸ φῶς. That the staircase begins cleanly only under this division was one of the two grounds on which [the text](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/01-the-text/) was settled, alongside the break of the oldest unpunctuated witnesses. What cannot be done is to run the argument the other way: the colometric case supports no reading but this one, and the figure is not offered here as a fresh argument for a decision already made.[^readinga]

Read for its content rather than its mechanism, the staircase carries a four-step movement, and that movement is the spine of everything the exegesis does with these verses: **ontology** — the eternal life that *was in* the Word ([1:4a](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/06-ontology-life-in-him/)); **anthropology** — that same life, turned toward humanity, *as* its light ([1:4b](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/07-anthropology-light-of-men/)); **cosmology** — that light *shining in* the world's darkness ([1:5a](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/08-cosmology-light-in-darkness/)); and **soteriology** — the darkness's failure to *overcome or comprehend* it ([1:5b](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/09-soteriology-not-overcome/)). The tread and the doctrine are not two things: each step of the figure is a step of the argument, which is why the exegesis takes the clauses in the evangelist's own order and does not rearrange them by topic.

One discrimination governs the whole treatment and is stated here so that it need not be restated at every step: the unit is **deliberate composition, not deliberate verse.** To say that the evangelist built a staircase is not to scan these lines as poetry, and it is not to reconstruct a hymn behind them. The figure stands whether the material is read as verse or, with the mainstream of recent interpreters, as elevated and rhythmical prose; what is claimed is deliberate work in the received text, not a recovered source.[^composition]

## The unit at the close of the foyer

The unit's synchronic reach does not stop at its own edges. The Prologue is read in this commentary under Carson's image of the *foyer* — the entrance hall through which the reader passes before stepping into the Gospel proper, where what is announced shapes how everything after it is seen. John 1:4–5 closes that entrance hall: the first movement of the Prologue ends here, and it ends by sounding, for the first time, the light-and-darkness theme on which the Gospel will run.[^foyer]

The foyer is framed. At one pole of the frame stands the life-and-light of verse 4; at the other, verse 14 — the Word made flesh, whose glory is beheld: ὁ λόγος σὰρξ ἐγένετο … ἐθεασάμεθα τὴν δόξαν αὐτοῦ, δόξαν ὡς μονογενοῦς. What the two poles share is not a resemblance of vocabulary but an identity of substance, and this commentary grounds it directly, with Dodd: the light and the glory are one divine self-disclosure. At verse 14 the flesh gives way to the beholding, and the δόξα beheld there is no longer visible radiance but glory apprehensible to the faculty of faith — for, as Dodd puts it at the level of the whole, "the Logos is the union of life and light."[^dodd] The light of verse 4, then, is not merely answered at verse 14; it is disclosed there, in the flesh, as glory. That is the substantive correspondence, and it is secure.

<!-- diagram -->

The further claim — that verse 4 and verse 14 are a *composed* pair, an inclusio the evangelist deliberately set to frame the Prologue — is a different kind of claim, and it is held here with high but provisional confidence. The distinction matters and is worth stating plainly, because it is the sort of thing commentaries usually leave implicit: what Dodd grounds is *what the two poles share*, not *that they were composed as a bracket*. The compositional pairing is not, as yet, attested by any source this commentary has engaged, and the commentary does not manufacture the difference away. Confirmation is reserved for the commentary's own engagement with verse 14, where that verse receives its full treatment and the frame can be closed from the other end; the movement expected there is upward, as the later work completes the Prologue's shape. Until then the reading stands as it is: strong, load-bearing for the unit's placement, and openly provisional in its compositional register.

The unit's synchronic architecture therefore has two strengths held at two confidences — the internal staircase, which is as secure as a compositional observation becomes, and the placement in the foyer's frame, which is strong but awaits its confirmation. Both are load-bearing in what follows, and the distinction between them is not a hedge but a disclosure: the reader is shown which of the unit's architectural claims rests on evidence complete in itself and which rests on work the commentary has not yet done. The [one life traced from creation to the Word made flesh](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/14-one-life-arc/) is the theological reach of that frame, and it is drawn out where the commentary gathers its theological implications, not here.

With the shape of the unit established, the architecture turns from what stands to what moves: beneath the same four treads runs a marked sequence of tenses, and it is [that run](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/03-macro-tense-run/) — imperfect, present, aorist — that gives the standing shape its time.
