The description of these two verses is finished; the exposition begins. Four clauses stand in a fixed order — an order the [architecture](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/05-architectural-implications/) has shown to be composed, not incidental — and the exegesis takes them as they were built: ontology, anthropology, cosmology, soteriology, each tread on its own evidence. The first tread is the deepest. Before the light, before the darkness, before any response to either, John says what *is*: that in the Word there was life, and that this life is the standing ground of everything that has come to be. Three things are fixed on this page, in order: how the clause is construed, what force its imperfect carries, and what its ζωή means.

## The construal — what has come to be, in him was life

The working text of the clause was settled in the [text-critical notes](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/01-the-text/): the sentence boundary falls after οὐδὲ ἕν, so that the relative clause ὃ γέγονεν opens verse 4 rather than closing verse 3, and the verb is the imperfect ἦν, not the present ἐστιν. Both decisions are carried here, not re-argued. Under that punctuation the clause construes naturally: ὃ γέγονεν — "what has come to be" — is the subject, ζωή the predicate nominative, ἦν the imperfect copula, and ἐν αὐτῷ the locative predication. *What has come to be — in him was life.*

The construal is entered across a pivot, and the pivot is doing theological work. The perfect γέγονεν does not name created reality in the act of its making — that was the aorist ἐγένετο of verse 3, creation as event. It names created reality in its *standing result*: all that has come to be, considered as it now stands. The perfect of resulting state is the grammarians' name for exactly this force, and it is what lets a clause about creation open a statement about the present standing of things.[^construal] And with the thought, the preposition has shifted. Verse 3 said creation came to be *through* him — δι' αὐτοῦ, the preposition of agency, the Word as the one through whom all things were made. Verse 4 says the life of what has come to be is *in* him — ἐν αὐτῷ, the preposition of location, the Word as the one in whom created reality has its life. The two prepositions are not interchangeable, and the movement between them is the clause's first teaching: creation was made through him; it lives in him.

## The force of the imperfect — an abiding life

The imperfect ἦν is not a narrative past, reporting something that once was and is no longer. It is the same imperfect of being that runs through the Prologue's opening — the sixfold ἦν of verses 1–4 — and its register is what Ridderbos calls the perspective of one who looks at the present in light of its origin, an origin antecedent to all human experience.[^ridderbos] Morris observes that in the Prologue the present tense is never used of the Word, with a single exception — the "shines" of verse 5, whose singularity the [run of tenses](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/03-macro-tense-run/) has already weighed.[^morris] Grammatically the imperfect carries incomplete action, duration, continuity: an abiding, standing state, not a completed fact.[^turner] The life that was in the Word is not an episode.

But the force must be held at its proper register, and the register here is *distinct* from verse 1. There, the imperfect predicated the Word's own eternal being: ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος. Here, it predicates the life that the created order has *in* him. The life of verse 4a is therefore coordinate with the eternal frame — it is not a created vitality, and it stands before and above all created experience — and yet it is distinct from the Word's own self-existent life. It is, in Ridderbos's formulation, the life he gives to the world, and not therefore the same as the life the Word shares in himself with the Father.[^gives] The grammatical caution is honored — Morris warns that the tense should not be pressed unduly[^caution] — but the direction of the clause is not in doubt: an abiding life, in him, on which what has come to be depends.

## The sense of ζωή — one divine life, under two relations

What, then, is this ζωή? The word must hold two senses at once — the divine, eternal life, and the life of the created order — and the whole question is how the two are held. This commentary's answer: not as an even balance, but as an ordered relation, and the order runs one way.

The lexical core is the divine life. ζωή here is, in Barrett's phrase, the essential energy of the Word;[^barrett] it is the *uncreated* life — McHugh, taking up Cyril of Alexandria's ἡ κατὰ φύσιν ζωή, the life that is such by nature, sets it against creatures that carry the germ of death in themselves.[^mchugh] In John's usage it is the divine and eternal life that the Gospel will progressively name ζωὴ αἰώνιος: the ζωή of 1:4 is the first word of a field that reaches its next landmark at 3:15 and runs to the Gospel's last page.[^brown] The creational sense — that this life is the life-principle of everything that has come to be — is real, and the construal requires it: the clause predicates life of *what has come to be*, across the resulting-state pivot. But it is a *derived* reach: what the one divine life amounts to *for* creation. The relation is directional and therefore asymmetric by construction. The divine life is the source; created life is the participation; the word *means* the divine life and *grounds* the created. That ordering is held with a firm and directional preference over the evenly balanced alternative — the reading, at home in Brown's Wisdom-hymn matrix, that gives the two senses coordinate weight.[^calibration] Brown himself, whose headline gloss is that Johannine ζωή is never natural life, supplies the derived reach in the same breath: just as the Word gave life and light to men in the creation, he writes, so Jesus the incarnate Word gives light and life to men in his ministry.[^reach]

This ordering is what turns the classic difficulty into the resolution. Schnackenburg pressed the objection harder than anyone: if ὃ γέγονεν — all created reality — is the subject of the clause, then no single ζωή can hold together the Word's divine life and a life-principle for all things; the term would be asked to mean two things at once. It is the strongest case for the rival punctuation, and it deserves a direct answer rather than a workaround.[^schnackenburg] The answer is not to divide the term but to order it. There is *one* divine, uncreated life in the Word, and it is the standing ground of created life; the two senses are one reality under two relations — source and participation — joined at the resulting-state pivot the construal already crossed. Read so, the clause does not strain: it says exactly what the ordered reading says it says. The resolution is offered as the answer Schnackenburg's own question calls for, not as a dismissal of it.

The ontological claim of the first tread is thus exact: the life that was *in* the Word is the divine life itself, and everything that has come to be has its life in him. It is the seed of a word the Gospel will make explicit — "as the Father has life in himself, so he has granted the Son also to have life in himself" (5:26) — and, under the Genesis frame that conditions this whole unit, it is the creation-life of Genesis 1 now named as held in the Word; that frame receives its own treatment where the unit engages it most directly, in the [cosmology of verse 5a](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/08-cosmology-light-in-darkness/).[^genesis] The doctrinal development of the claim — what the church confesses on the strength of a life held in the Word — belongs to the [theology of the unit](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/10-life-in-the-word/), and is not begun here.

From this ground the movement takes its second step, and the composition has already bound the two treads together: the staircase takes up ζωή at the head of the next clause. This same life, turned toward humanity, is its [light](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/07-anthropology-light-of-men/).
