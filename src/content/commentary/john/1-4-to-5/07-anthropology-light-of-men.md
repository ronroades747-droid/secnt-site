The second tread takes up the first. The subject of verse 4b is the life the reader has just watched the [first tread](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/06-ontology-life-in-him/) establish — ἡ ζωή, now named again with the article, "*the* life," the very life predicated of the Word in verse 4a — and it is carried one step forward: this life *was the light of men*. The [staircase](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/02-gradatio-and-foyer/) turns here, from what the life *is in the Word* to what it *is toward humanity*, and it turns by a copula that binds two articular definites to each other: ἡ ζωὴ ἦν τὸ φῶς. Three questions are fixed on this page, in order: what this φῶς is, how the life *is* the light, and whose light it is — the last in two steps, since "whose" asks both how far the light reaches and in what sense the light is humanity's own.

## The sense of φῶς — the divine, revelatory light the Word is

The τὸ φῶς predicated of the life is not a created illumination, and not a metaphor for reason or moral insight. It is the divine, revelatory light that the uncreated ζωή of verse 4a *is* when it is turned toward humanity — the life-light, the life made manifest. John marks it as unique by the article: τὸ φῶς τῶν ἀνθρώπων is *the* light, the sole source of light — in McHugh's phrase, the light *par excellence*, κατ' ἐξοχήν — "for all those who here on earth dwell in darkness and in the shadow of death," the hope of deathless life.[^mchugh] The Prologue itself corroborates the article's force when it names the same light τὸ φῶς τὸ ἀληθινόν, "the true light," at 1:9.

The referent of this light is the personal Logos — the one the reader has already met at 1:1 — and the sense is held at the Prologue's *disclosive* register: the light is the Word himself disclosed, and the Gospel's first-person "I am the light of the world" (8:12; 9:5) is the *explication* of what is disclosed here, not a premise read back into the Prologue. Two identifications are therefore excluded as the *sense* of the word, while allowed their proper reach as background. This φῶς is *not* the created light of Genesis 1 — the creation-light is frame, not referent, and the frame receives its own treatment where the unit engages it most directly, in the [cosmology of verse 5a](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/08-cosmology-light-in-darkness/).[^notnatural] And it is *not yet* the self-named light of 8:12 — the same light, disclosed here in the third person, self-named there in the first. Both exclusions guard the same thing: the word means the divine, revelatory light, and everything else it touches, it touches from that center.

## The predication — one divine reality under two relations

How does the life *become* the light? The copula ἡ ζωὴ ἦν τὸ φῶς operates at two levels at once, and this commentary reads it as a composite of both. At the level of *referent*, life and light converge on the one personal Logos: they are not two things but one reality, the Word who is both — the identification the tradition registers plainly, from the shared articles of the Greek to Origen's flat statement that the life and the light of men "are the same."[^referent] At the level of *composition*, the predication is directional: the divine life is the *source*, and the light of men is its revelatory *reach* toward humanity — "the gift that makes life possible" (Ridderbos), the light the divine life *became* as it was dispensed to the human race (Carson), the uncreated life that, being imperishable, brings the light that is the hope of deathless life (McHugh).[^directional] The two levels are not rivals to be averaged, and the movement's *order* is itself part of the meaning: Origen observed that John writes life first and light second because the order encodes a process, and Augustine compressed the same directionality into a soteriological exchange — the Word took death from what is ours to give life-as-light from what is his.[^order]

The composite is anchored from the divine side, and the anchoring is a firm and directional preference, not an even balance. It carries forward the source-and-participation ordering already locked for the [life](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/06-ontology-life-in-him/): the reality *is* the divine life, the Logos himself — that is the identification, at the referent — and that one life *is the light* by turning its imparted, revelatory face toward humanity — that is the directionality, at the "of men." One divine reality, then, under two relations: the Word's own uncreated life as source, and that same life *revealed-and-given* toward the human race as its light. This is the crux the tread turns on. The *in-him* life of verse 4a is, at the revelatory register, the *for-humanity* light of verse 4b — the life that is the standing ground of created existence is the light by which humanity is meant to see and live.

## The scope of "the men" — humanity as such, every human being

The genitive τῶν ἀνθρώπων is universal and categorical. It names the whole human race, every human being without distinction, and it names humanity *as a class*. The article is generic — "of men as a class … not of individuals only" (Westcott)[^westcott] — and the phrase "must refer to the whole human race," as McHugh puts it, pointing forward to the Prologue's own confirmation: at 1:9 the same light enlightens πάντα ἄνθρωπον, "every man," distributively, and at 8:12 it will be named at the cosmic register, the light τοῦ κόσμου.[^scope] The move from verse 3 to verse 4b is a deliberate narrowing within creation: from the πάντα of "all things came to be through him" to the τῶν ἀνθρώπων of "the light *of men*" — the aperture closes from the whole created order to the one creature to whom the life is *light*, the creature capable of response (Brown).[^narrowing]

Two narrowings are refused. The scope is *not* restricted to Israel or the covenant people. Whether the light's reception runs first through Israel is a real question — it is raised by the Prologue's own "he came to his own" (1:11), and the sources split on it, Harris reading "his own" as Israel, Schnackenburg leaning to mankind with Israel as representative — but it is a *reception* question, and it is held for this commentary's treatment of verse 11, without narrowing the scope fixed here.[^israel] And the scope is *not* restricted to the elect or to believers. The soteriologically restricted reading fails at every register — the generic article names a class, not a roll of recipients; 1:9's "every man" distributes the class exhaustively; and the Prologue's own progression requires the universal here, precisely so that the discriminations of 1:11–13 can land with their intended weight. It fails even at its own commonly cited source: Calvin, read directly, is decisively *not* a witness for a restricted light at this verse — his comment runs the other way, to a light "diffused over all mankind," reaching every human being.[^calvin]

## The genitive — the light *for* humanity and humanity's *own* light

How, finally, does the genitive relate the light to the men? Both ways at once. τῶν ἀνθρώπων is *objective* — the light directed *toward* humanity, the recipient toward whom the *par-excellence* light is turned — and *possessive* — humanity's *own* light, the constitutive good of the creature made in God's image.[^faces] The double-articular construction τὸ φῶς τῶν ἀνθρώπων foregrounds the two together, on the grammatical principle Wallace has made standard: where a genitive construction sustains more than one relation, the exegetical question is not which single relation the case abstractly permits but which the construction *foregrounds* — and here it foregrounds both.[^wallace]

The ordering between the two faces is again divine-anchored, coordinate with the directionality of the predication itself. The *objective* is foregrounded: the light is given and turned *toward* humanity, source to revelatory reach. The *possessive* is absorbed at the constitutive level: humanity is the creature constituted *to receive and bear* that given light. The light is humanity's "own," then, not as an autonomous possession it generates or controls but as the light *proper to the creature made to receive it*: one directional relation under two grammatical faces, not two competing relations. That humanity is so constituted — the image-of-God anthropology that the phrase "the light of men" carries in its genitive — is real theological weight, and Westcott, Calvin, and Harris each tie it explicitly to Genesis 1:27.[^imago] But it is drawn out as doctrine in the [theology of the unit](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/11-image-and-light-of-men/), rather than made load-bearing on the exegesis here, and its fuller Genesis grounding is engaged under the Genesis frame at the [cosmology](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/08-cosmology-light-in-darkness/).

The anthropology tread thus lands as one teachable claim: the divine life the Word himself has is, turned toward humanity, the light by which the whole human race — every human being, humanity as such — is meant to see and live, and humanity is the one creature made to receive that light *as* light. It is the seed of the Gospel's great self-predication, "I am the light of the world" (8:12): what the Prologue here discloses in the third person, the incarnate Word will there say in the first. And it sets up the turn the movement now takes — for the light of men does not remain an abstraction. It *shines*, and it shines [in the dark](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/08-cosmology-light-in-darkness/).

[^wallace]: Daniel B. Wallace, *Greek Grammar Beyond the Basics: An Exegetical Syntax of the New Testament* (Grand Rapids: Zondervan, 1996), pp. 119–21 ("The Plenary Genitive"). Wallace's named category is the subjective/objective pair — the genitive "both subjective and objective" — but the principle he grounds there is wider, and it is the principle this page applies to the objective and possessive faces of τῶν ἀνθρώπων: a decision between relations is not to be presupposed where both "fit in a given passage, and do not contradict but rather complement one another," lest — as Wallace quotes Zerwick — "we sacrifice to clarity of meaning part of the fulness of the meaning." The both-and reading, where the construction sustains it, is a resolution rather than a hedge. Wallace's treatment of the generic (categorical) article, pp. 227–31 — the article that "distinguishes one class from another," with the insertion of "as a class" after the noun as the key to identification — supplies the grammatical floor for the categorical scope.
