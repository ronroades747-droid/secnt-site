Two descriptions of these four clauses now stand, and the second was not argued from the first. One is a shape: the [staircase of terms](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/02-gradatio-and-foyer/), which shows what the unit *is* — four treads in a fixed relation, each taking up the last word of the one before. The other is a spine: the [run of tenses](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/03-macro-tense-run/), which shows what the unit *does* in time — imperfect, present, aorist, and the break at verse 5. This section asks what those two are to each other, and the answer is not that one explains the other, or that one is the real observation and the other its confirmation. Nothing here re-argues either description; both are established, each on its own evidence, in the two sections preceding.

## Two dimensions of one composition

The *gradatio* and the macro-tense run are **co-primary**. They are not two arguments for one conclusion — that would make them rivals for the same job, and would let the weaker be dropped once the stronger had done its work. Neither is prior to the other, and neither can be dropped. They are two dimensions of a single composition, and what makes them dimensions rather than duplicates is that they describe different things about the same object. The *gradatio* is the synchronic-rhetorical shape: the staircase of terms, standing all at once, the way the façade of a building stands. The run is the diachronic-temporal shape: the sequence of forms, unfolding, the way a building has a history.

And they trace the *same four treads*. The staircase moves life → light → darkness → the darkness's failure. The tenses, beneath those identical four clauses, move eternal ground → present shining → completed failure. Neither movement is a translation of the other into different vocabulary: one is a movement of terms, the other a movement of time, and they happen to be laid over the same four steps because they are the one composition seen along two axes.

<!-- diagram -->

Holding them co-primary — split by nothing but register — is what keeps them from borrowing each other's witnesses, and that discipline is not a formality. The figure is anchored by Nässelqvist and Miller; the tenses by Ridderbos and Waetjen. Two sets of readers, arrived at from two directions, with no one carried across. The boundary is not merely asserted, either: Waetjen, whose formulation on the aspect of the present the commentary leans on as hard as it leans on anything, expressly disclaims all staircase analysis in the same article. A witness who declines to testify on the other register is the cleanest guarantee available that the two registers are independent — the argument for the run cannot be quietly resting on the figure when the run's own anchor will not touch the figure.[^registers]

## What the double description buys

Because the two registers are independent, the unit's internal composition is **doubly attested** — described twice, by two sets of readers using two methods, and the two descriptions land on the same four treads without either having been fitted to the other. That is the ground on which the deliberate-composition reading is held with as much confidence as a compositional judgment of this kind admits: firmer than either description could carry alone, because the agreement is not the agreement of two people who consulted each other.

There is a second and independent argument for the same conclusion, and it runs from the other end — from the clause-level readings back to the architecture. Suppose the deliberate composition were removed and these four clauses taken as four true statements that happen to stand in a row. Then the readings the exegesis reaches, one clause at a time, stop cohering. The life of verse 4a is *the light* of verse 4b — not a second thing beside it, but the same divine life turned toward humanity — and that predication is intelligible only if the second clause is built on the first. The light that shines in verse 5a is the light of verse 4b carried across the clause boundary, and the darkness of verse 5b is the darkness of verse 5a held steady while the verb changes. The unit's temporal reading, in which the present sets the unit's time and the aorist closes against it, requires an author who arranged the forms in that order and no other.[^interlock] None of these readings was reached by consulting the architecture; each was reached at its own clause, on its own evidence. That they interlock is the finding, and what the interlock requires is exactly what the two descriptions independently report: deliberate progression. This is why the architecture is set out before the exegesis rather than derived from it — the reader should be able to see that the two lines of argument were run separately before being seen to meet.

## The wider frame, held more loosely

A second claim has been in play across these architecture sections, and it must be distinguished from the first or the confidence proper to each will be misread.

The claim just made is about the unit's *own internal composition* — that these four clauses were deliberately built. The further claim is about the unit's *placement within the Prologue's architecture*: the frame of the entrance hall in which this unit stands at one pole and verse 14 at the other, and the tense-spine of which this unit's run is one instance, taken together as a claim about the whole Prologue as a composed thing. That is a coordinate matter, and it is held here at strong but provisional confidence, for two principled reasons.

The first is that the verse 4 / verse 14 pair awaits the commentary's own treatment of verse 14. What is grounded now, with Dodd, is what the two poles *share* — the light of verse 4 and the glory of verse 14 are one divine self-disclosure — and not the further claim that the evangelist *composed* them as a bracket; the frame can only be closed from the other end.[^pair] The second is that the Prologue-architecture thesis reaches past this unit in both directions: it is not settled until the Prologue's frame is closed at 1:15–18, and its full statement belongs to the commentary's introduction to the Prologue rather than to any single unit within it. The movement expected on both counts is *upward*, as the later work completes the frame; this is disclosure of what has not yet been done, not doubt about what has.

Two early and independent witnesses do corroborate the wider claim, and they are worth having, though it should be said plainly what kind of evidence they are: they attest that the Prologue was *received* as a connected sequence, which is a different and weaker thing than attesting that it was composed as one. Gregory of Nyssa reads John 1:1, 1:4–5, 1:9, 1:14 and 1:18 as a single connected sequence — Word, life, light, only-begotten — quoting them together in two chains of adaptation; a Greek reader of the fourth century, that is, took these verses to be of a piece with their neighbours and used them so.[^nyssa] Boyarin makes the same point from the far end of the tradition and by another route entirely, tracing the verb of verse 5b through the Prologue's later clauses — κατέλαβεν, παρέλαβον, ἔλαβον, ἐλάβομεν — a chain of one root running from our unit through the reception drama to the confession of verse 16. The vocabulary of verses 4 and 5, on that showing, is woven into the Prologue's fabric rather than sitting in it as a foreign body.[^boyarin]

## The reconstructions, surveyed and not adopted

One family of readings would explain the unit's shape a different way, and because this commentary has now twice claimed deliberate composition, it owes that family an account rather than a silence.

The reconstructions propose that the material is elevated and rhythmical because it is not originally the evangelist's — that behind the Prologue stands a pre-Gospel hymn or source, recoverable in strophes, which the evangelist took up and glossed. The proposal is not eccentric; it is held, in one version or another, by a long and serious list of readers, and their schemes differ from one another as much as they differ from this commentary.[^survey] What is common to them, and what this commentary takes seriously, is the *observation* underneath: the material is elevated, it is rhythmic, and it groups. That observation is sound, and it is received here — at the level of the text as it stands.

What is declined is the *explanation*. The move from "this material is patterned" to "therefore it was composed elsewhere and imported" is the step this commentary does not take, and the reason is the one this section has been making: the patterning is doubly attested on two independent registers, it interlocks with readings reached clause by clause, and a source-hypothesis explains none of that better than deliberate authorship does. The same discrimination has already been applied twice in these pages to individual readers — Bultmann's chain-locking by catchword, and Boyarin's chain of one root through the Prologue, are both received as evidence of an integrated composition while the cultic-hymn and midrash-genre accounts that accompany them are set aside — and it is applied here to the family as a whole. A substantial counter-tradition reads as this commentary reads, finding deliberate work in the received text: Ridderbos, Michaels, Morris, Barrett, Carson, and Boyarin, arriving from positions otherwise quite unlike one another.[^finalform]

The question of the Prologue's literary kind is a real one and it is larger than this unit; its full treatment belongs to the commentary's introduction to the Prologue, where the whole can be seen at once. What governs here is the discrimination stated at the outset of the architecture and holding across all of it: this unit is **deliberate composition, not deliberate verse**. To find a staircase and a run of tenses in these four clauses is not to scan them as poetry, and it is not to reconstruct anything behind them.

Two dimensions, then, on the same four treads, separately anchored and jointly required — and a frame around them held at a lower confidence, openly. What has not yet been asked is what any of it *teaches*. The architecture is not merely formal: before a single clause is expounded, the shape of the unit is already carrying theology, and [what it carries](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/05-architectural-implications/) is the last of these architectural questions.
