The first tread asked whose the life is, and answered that it is held — that no creature owns the life by which it lives, and that [to be alive at all is to hold one's life in the Word](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/10-life-in-the-word/). The second tread asks a different question, and it is a question about us. If the life the Word has is, turned toward humanity, humanity's *light*, then what must the creature be that this light should be *its* light? The clause says τὸ φῶς τῶν ἀνθρώπων — the light *of men* — and the exegesis found that the genitive works [both ways at once](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/07-anthropology-light-of-men/): the light is turned toward humanity, and it is humanity's own. It is the second of those, the possessive, that the church has had to say something about. A light can be shone at anything. It is not every thing's *own*.

## The creature made to receive

What the possessive presupposes is a doctrine of the human creature, and Scripture supplies it at the head of the canon. The human being is made in God's image and likeness (Gen 1:26–27) — and among all that God makes, only of this creature is it said. That is the ground on which the light can be humanity's own without ceasing to be the Word's to give. The image is not a possession the creature generates; it is what the creature *is*, by the act that made it. And what the creature so made is *for* is disclosed in the same breath: it is made to stand before God and to know him.

The psalmist says the thing exactly, and says it as something already assumed rather than newly argued: "in your light we see light" (Ps 36:9). Notice the shape of that sentence. It does not say we possess a light and see by it. It says there is a light that is God's, and that seeing at all happens *in* it. The seeing is ours; the light is his; and the two are not in competition, because we were made to see this way. That is the whole logic of the possessive genitive in miniature, four centuries of Israel's worship before John writes it into a clause.

Westcott saw that the image is what makes the relation possible. Commenting on this very phrase he writes that "Man as made in the image of God stood in a special relation to the Word."[^westcott] The relation is *special* — not the relation of every creature to its Maker, which is real but different, but the relation of the creature that can be addressed. Calvin reaches the same place by another road. He will not let the light of verse 4 be an ordinary endowment: the life given to men, he says, "was not of an ordinary description, but was united to *the light* of understanding," and on that basis "He separates man from the rank of other creatures."[^calvin] Calvin is not talking about the image in so many words — his term is understanding, and it is worth keeping his word rather than improving it — but the separation he marks is the one the doctrine needs. Something about this creature is fitted to the light.

## "Their own" — and the sense in which they are not

Everything now depends on holding the possessive in the right register, because it will bear two very different readings and only one of them is Johannine.

The reading to refuse is the one that makes the light a human capability. On that reading humanity has a light of its own — reason, conscience, the spark of the divine — and the Word's coming confirms or perfects what was already burning. That reading has the advantage of taking "of men" seriously and the fatal disadvantage of making the Prologue's fifth verse unintelligible, since a light humanity generates is not a light the darkness could fail to overcome; it would simply be part of the darkness's own weather. And it cannot survive the previous tread: if [creaturely life is held rather than owned](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/10-life-in-the-word/), the light that life becomes cannot be owned either. The grammar of the two clauses is one grammar.

The reading the text will bear is that the light is humanity's own the way sight is an eye's own — not as something the eye manufactures, but as the good proper to a thing built for it. An eye in the dark is still an eye; what it lacks is not its nature but its object. So here. The light is *proper to* the human creature, which is to say the creature is not complete without it and is not merely capable of it but constituted for it. That is a claim about design, not about production. Humanity's own light, and never for a moment humanity's own possession.

Put the two together and the possessive stops being a rival to the objective and becomes its ground. The light is turned *toward* humanity — that is the movement — and it can be turned toward humanity as *light* rather than as mere illumination falling on an indifferent surface, because there is a creature there made to receive it as such. The objective relation is the one the verse foregrounds. The possessive is what makes the objective land.

## The whole person, and not one faculty

Here the Reformed dogmatic tradition has a caution to enter, and it is worth entering carefully, because the doctrine goes wrong most often at exactly this joint. The temptation is to locate the image in some one part of us — in reason, or in dominion, or in the will's freedom, or in a moral quality — and then to say that *that* is the point of contact where the light lands. Bavinck spends the opening of his treatment refusing every version of that move, and his own formulation is deliberately total: "the whole human person is the image of the whole Deity."[^bavinck] He will not divide it. "The whole being, therefore, and not *something in man* but *man himself*, is the image of God."

The consequence for our clause is direct. If the image were a faculty, the light would be addressed to a faculty, and the rest of the human being would be so much surrounding material. Because the image is the person, the light is addressed to the person — which is why the Gospel can go on to speak of walking in the light, and of loving darkness, and of deeds, and of coming to the light or refusing to, without ever changing the subject. The whole creature is what was made for this.

Bavinck's second point is the one that most directly serves the possessive. The orientation toward God is not an addition to human nature: "The service of God, the love for God, and fellowship with God are not superadded gifts but originally and integrally human."[^bavinck] Strike that clause and the possessive genitive collapses, because if fellowship with God were an extra fitted onto a nature complete without it, then the light would be an extra too, and "of men" would mean no more than "aimed at men." Bavinck's third statement says what such a nature is for: from the beginning human nature was so created "that it was amenable to and fit for the highest degree of conformity to God and for the most intimate indwelling of God."[^bavinck] *Fit for* is the whole doctrine of this page in two words.

None of this is a claim about what humanity achieves. Every one of those sentences is about what humanity was *made*, and all three verbs are passive or divine. The dignity is real and it is entirely received.

## The first guardrail — the image is as wide as the race

Two guardrails keep this locus from over-reaching, and both were set by the exegesis rather than imposed on it.

The first: naming the *imago* as the ground of the possessive does not narrow who the light is for. It would be easy to let it — to reason that if the light is proper to the image-bearer, and if the image is damaged or effaced in some, then the light is proper to fewer than all. The text forecloses that before it starts. The article at τῶν ἀνθρώπων is generic: Westcott's note is that the phrase is "of men as a class … not of individuals only,"[^westcott] and Harris's compact gloss on the genitive is simply "generic."[^harris] The class is the human race, and the *imago* is exactly coextensive with it. So the light is humanity's own *as such* — not the property of a subset who have kept themselves in a condition to deserve it.

This matters more than it may appear, because the restricted reading has usually arrived by way of this very locus, borrowing the image's dignity to draw a line inside the race. [The exegesis closed that door at every register](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/07-anthropology-light-of-men/), including at the source most often cited to hold it open.[^scope] The doctrine of the image is a doctrine of what human beings are, not of which ones qualify.

## The second guardrail — the image's ruin is not yet in view

The second guardrail is harder to keep, because the mind runs ahead. Say that humanity is the creature made to receive the light, and the next thought arrives unbidden: *then why does it not?* The Prologue is about to press exactly that. The very next clause reports that [the darkness did not apprehend the light](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/09-soteriology-not-overcome/), and a few verses on, the world that was made through him "knew him not," and his own "received him not."

But that tension is the Gospel's to unfold, and this verse does not resolve it. Two things in particular are not settled here and should not be smuggled in. First, what became of the image in the creature that refuses — whether it is lost, defaced, retained in ruins. Second, and more consequentially, *how* a humanity that will not receive the light comes to receive it after all: whether the turn is something the creature manages from its own remaining resources or something worked in it. Both belong to the passages on reception, and the Gospel takes them up deliberately and at length. To answer them here, from the constitution alone, would be to build the doctrine of grace out of the doctrine of creation — which is precisely the move the Prologue declines to make by putting these clauses in this order.

What the verse gives, then, is the fact of the constitution and not the story of its ruin. Humanity is the creature made for this light. That much is said. The rest is coming.

## The register of the claim

This locus is theological illumination drawn from the clause; it is not part of what the clause was made to establish. The exegetical weight at "the light of men" rests on the scope of the genitive and the way the life becomes the light — [and that is where the argument was carried](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/07-anthropology-light-of-men/). The *imago* grounding is what the tradition has confessed on the strength of it, and the page holds it at that register: firmly, because Genesis says what it says and the possessive needs a ground, but without letting it back-fill into the exegesis as though the clause could not be read without it. It can. It reads better with the doctrine in view, which is a different claim.

The sourcing is correspondingly modest and worth naming plainly. The systematic register here is Bavinck, engaged where he actually works — a doctrine of human nature, not a reading of John — and the connection between his anthropology and this clause is the commentary's, made in the open.[^bavinck] Harris makes the Genesis connection at the verse itself, and makes it with a hedge that is worth preserving rather than tidying away: the light-giving is, he writes, "presumably" a matter of the Logos endowing everyone "with the divine image (Gen 1:27) as part of their constitution."[^harris] Presumably. The tie is right and it is an inference, and the difference between those is the kind of difference this commentary tries to keep visible.

## The dignity the tragedy is written on

What the second tread teaches the church to confess, then, is that the human creature is the one made for this light — made to receive it as light, and not complete without it.

That confession does two things at once, and the Prologue needs both. It supplies the dignity: whatever any human being has made of themselves, they are a creature constituted for the knowledge of God, and the light that comes to them is not a foreign claim but the answer to what they are. And it supplies the tragedy: because only a creature made for the light can be said to have *refused* it. A stone in the dark is not in trouble. The Gospel's whole account of unbelief — the world that knew him not, the darkness that loved itself, the deeds done at night — is intelligible only as the story of a creature acting against its own constitution.

So the image is the ground of both the honour and the indictment, and the Prologue will lay both on it. It is also, though the verse does not say so yet, the ground of the hope: a creature made for the light is a creature the light can restore. But that lies ahead. What stands here is the making, and the fitness, and the light that is theirs because they were made for it.

The tread turns next to the place where that light is actually found — not in a creature's clear seeing, but shining in the dark.
