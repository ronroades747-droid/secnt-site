The third tread takes up the light and sets it in motion. The subject of verse 5a is τὸ φῶς — anaphoric to [the light of verse 4b](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/07-anthropology-light-of-men/), the same divine, revelatory, personal light, now no longer a predicate but the grammatical subject of a verb. And with that verb the composition turns a corner it has been building toward since its first word. Until now everything has been ἦν — *was*, the imperfect of eternal being. Here, and here alone in the Prologue's opening, the light *shines*: φαίνει, the Prologue's first present indicative. Three things are fixed on this page: what that present tense does, what the darkness is, and how the little preposition between them sets the one inside the other. And one frame stands over all three — the opening of Genesis, which this unit takes up for the third time in five verses.

## The frame — Genesis 1, taken up christologically

Before the clause is read word by word, the frame it is written in has to be named, because at this verse the frame comes closest to the surface. John 1:4–5 is the Prologue's third sustained engagement with Genesis 1:1–5 in the space of five verses: after ἐν ἀρχῇ at 1:1 and the creative word of 1:3, the vocabulary of the opening of Genesis returns here as a set — light, darkness, and shining, held in the same relation Genesis holds them in.[^triad]

Two disciplines govern how this commentary reads that return. The first concerns its *register*: the engagement is allusion, not quotation. John marks no citation, and the correspondence does not require lexical identity at every point — John's σκοτία answers the σκότος of Genesis without repeating it — because an allusion works by pattern, not by verbal match.[^allusion] The second concerns its *direction*, and it is the more consequential of the two. The echo does not run from a doctrine of creation forward into Christology, as though the Prologue were expounding Genesis and arriving at Jesus. It runs the other way. Ridderbos puts it exactly. The purpose of John 1:1–5, he writes, is not to expound Genesis 1 with an eye to the coming and work of Christ, "by way of a 'christological doctrine of creation,' but rather the reverse: to give to the Christ-event the fundamental 'setting' in which alone it can be understood." And a page later, the sentence that fixes it: "between Genesis 1 and John 1 lies the Christ-event." Dodd reaches the same conclusion from the critical side: "We start with faith in Jesus, which involves the recognition that the meaning which we find in Him is the meaning of the whole universe—that, in fact, that which is incarnate in Him is the Logos." And it is precisely there that John differs decisively from Philo.[^direction] Genesis is the frame the Christ-event is set in, not the premise from which it is deduced.

Read that way, the frame does not merely repeat; it *transposes*. The light of Genesis 1:3 is the created light of the first day, spoken into being; the light of John 1:4–5 is the uncreated light the Word himself is, and it is the light *of men*. And the darkness undergoes a change of role that is sharper still. In Genesis the σκότος of 1:2 is a bounded constituent of the world God is making, separated from the light by God's own act and given its place in the ordered day. In John it is no longer a constituent of the good creation but creation set *against* God — the dark of the fallen creature, the condition the light must shine into rather than the night God has appointed.[^transposition] That transposition is what makes the rest of this page necessary. Genesis separates light from darkness; John puts them in the same place and lets the light shine there.

## The shining — a marked present, holding three frames of one light

The grammar first, and it is settled. φαίνει is imperfective: it views the shining from inside its progress rather than as a completed event or a static timeless fact. Robertson names it the "linear present" — "the light keeps on giving light," durative and holding across the incarnational boundary — and cross-references the Johannine corpus's own confirmation at 1 John 2:8, ἡ σκοτία παράγεται καὶ τὸ φῶς τὸ ἀληθινὸν ἤδη φαίνει. Waetjen, reading the Prologue's temporal architecture at close range, calls it "a continuous actuality," and adds the qualification that keeps the reading honest: pointedly *not* "a timeless presence" available to intuitive intellect.[^floor] That is the floor. Perfective readings are foreclosed by it; the light's shining is ongoing, and the clause does not narrate a finished episode.

On that floor the present carries a *marked* compositional force. It is the Prologue's first present indicative, and it arrives in the middle of a long chain of imperfects — three times in 1:1, once in 1:2, twice in 1:4 — so that the shift is audible. It is the joint at which the [run of tenses](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/03-macro-tense-run/) breaks: the eternal-and-creational frame of 1:1–4 pivots here into the present, and the unit's time is set. Nothing in the clause requires the shift; the evangelist could have written ἔφαινεν and kept the chain unbroken. He did not, and the break is the point.

What the marked present then does is hold three frames of one shining together at once. It is *creational* — the light that the divine life is, illuminating the human race from the first of the Word's creative work: Robertson's durative reach across the pre-incarnate state, Waetjen's shining that runs "from the time of creation into the time of the Gospel."[^creational] It is *incarnational* — the personal Word shining in his self-disclosure to the world he made, which is the frame Ridderbos and Michaels read at the verse. Ridderbos argues it grammatically as well as compositionally: the "present tense 'shines' can hardly relate to a closed period in the past" — had a finished era been meant, the imperfect was to hand — and the light of this clause is "the light that came when Christ entered the world and that now shines." The Prologue's first movement arrives at 1:5 in the contemporary reality of that shining, and the correspondence of 1:5 with 1:10–11 confirms it: the shining of this clause is the shining that met rejection. Michaels marks the same shift more plainly still: at 1:5 "the Gospel writer returns to his own time."[^incarnational] And it is *ongoing-contemporary* — the same light shining now, in the hour of the Gospel's proclamation: the renewed present at 1:9, and the 1 John 2:8 parallel where the true light "already shines."[^contemporary]

These are not three readings among which the exegesis chooses. They are three aspects of one shining, and the reading is not a hedged middle between a "timeless" and a "historical" present but the one the unit's structure requires. The gnomic reading — φαίνει as a maxim about light in general, the sort of thing one says of the sun — is foreclosed at the outset, because the light that shines here is a person, not a principle. And the marked present must carry the eternal-and-creational reach *forward* into the present rather than abandon it, which only the layered force can do. Carson names the verse "a masterpiece of planned ambiguity," and the phrase is precise: not that the verse is unclear, but that it is *built* to be heard in more than one frame at once — the first-time reader hearing a creational shining, the reader who has been through the Gospel hearing the light and darkness that govern the rest of the book, and John licensing both.[^carson] Koester reaches the same conclusion by a different route, from the figure's incongruity: this light "cannot be comprehended as part of the natural cycle," and because the picture will not sit still inside that cycle "readers must search for its significance at another level."[^koester] The patristic reception converges from a third direction: Cyril's Word, who is not only "truly light" but "the giver of light to all whom he infuses with the light of understanding," makes the giving continuous and constitutive, and Augustine's blind man in the sunlight makes the shining independent of its reception — the light is not absent; the sightless man is absent from it.[^fathers]

## The darkness — the fallen human condition, real but not ultimate

Into what does the light shine? Into ἡ σκοτία — and the darkness of 1:5 is not a single register but a composite the Prologue's method holds together. It is the fallen human condition, seen at once *cosmologically* (the created order under the absence of light, the Genesis backdrop), *morally* (the darkness of sin — Robertson's "moral darkness," seeded here and made explicit at 3:19), and *noetically* (ignorance and unbelief, the realm that does not know its Maker). The three are the commentary's own composite, and they are held on Koester's principle rather than as a menu — he names the light's adversary on more than one register at once and treats the registers as overlapping rather than as alternatives; Zerwick registers the moral content at the grammarian's level, glossing σκοτία as darkness "with ethical content."[^registers] Of the three, the existential-noetic is foregrounded here. What the darkness most *is*, at this locus, is the condition of a humanity turned from God: Bultmann's "revolt against the light," not a substance — a mode of human existence rather than a cosmic stuff — and McHugh's "cultural climate, the godless atmosphere" — which, he is careful to say, the noun does not refer *directly* to unbelievers, nor to the spirit-powers of Ephesians 6:12.[^condition]

That last qualification carries more weight than it first appears to. What is being described is a *modified* dualism, not a metaphysical one: not an eternal counter-principle set over against God, not a Gnostic realm with its own standing, but the bounded and non-ultimate state of the creature. Origen states the boundary in a sentence: whether it is a question of the darkness of men or of death, "men are not such by nature."[^origen] And three independent witnesses guard it — two of them by exclusion, the third by placement. Bultmann demythologizes: the Gnostic cosmic-dualism frame is precisely what the evangelist takes up and dismantles. Dodd fixes the non-identity with Philo and the Redeemer-myth and states the limit outright — "the cosmological dualism of i. 5 is not ultimate." And Lincoln, who supplies the term the reading goes by, reads it out of the Jewish background: in the developed form of the light-and-darkness contrast — the apocalyptic writings, and the Qumran literature more developed still — the two "came to represent the two sides of a *modified* dualism in which God, on the side of light, is ultimately in control," and the Fourth Gospel "shares this perspective."[^guards] That third guard works the opposite way from the first two, and the difference is worth marking. Bultmann and Dodd guard the boundary by *excluding* — this is not the Gnostic myth, this is not Philo's cosmology. Lincoln guards it by *placing* — John belongs to a Jewish tradition in which the opposition was never between equals, because one side of it is God's. A reading is better secured by a witness who says where it belongs than by two who say only where it does not. McHugh converges from the Old Testament side, reading the darkness as a constituent of the chaos of Genesis 1:2 brought under God's control and subject to his will, with Psalm 139:11–12 and the Qumran Rule of the Community behind it; and the patristic reception converges again, Cyril and Augustine and Origen and Isaac of Nineveh all reading the darkness at this verse as the creature's condition rather than a cosmic substance.[^convergence]

So the darkness is granted everything it actually is and nothing it is not. It is real — a genuine condition, not an illusion or a mere privation of knowledge; the light shines *into* something. It is not ultimate — it has a beginning in the creature's turning and no independent standing over against God. And under the Genesis frame set out above, this is the darkness of Genesis 1 transposed: the primeval dark that God separated and appointed has become the dark of a race that has turned from him.

## The location — the light shines *within* the dark, and the dark remains dark

One word settles how the two stand to each other. The preposition ἐν is locative, not directional: the light shines *within* the darkness as the sphere it inhabits, not *at* it as a hostile object it strikes from outside.

This follows first from what the darkness has just been shown to be. A state and a sphere that the fallen creature inhabits is not the kind of thing one shines *at*; it is the kind of thing one shines *in*. And it is confirmed grammatically, from three directions. John himself has the directional construction and uses it elsewhere for exactly that purpose: "I have come as light *into* the world," ἐγὼ φῶς εἰς τὸν κόσμον ἐλήλυθα (12:46), εἰς with the accusative, the vector toward a target. Here he writes ἐν with the dative — the construction of position within a region, immersion in a medium, the sphere in which an action holds — and it is the same locative-incarnational construction the Prologue will use of the Word's own dwelling, ἐσκήνωσεν ἐν ἡμῖν, "he pitched his tent *among* us" (1:14).[^locative] McHugh presses the point on the choice of preposition itself: John writes ἐν and not ἐπί, and the choice "stresses that the darkness remains, undispelled by the light."[^mchughen] And Harris removes the last temptation to read a fight into the preposition by locating the adversative note where it actually falls: the "and yet" of these verses is carried by the simple καί of verse 5b, not by anything in verse 5a.[^adversative]

The picture of verse 5a, then, is neither triumph nor struggle but *coexistence*. The divine light shines, and goes on shining — "perpetually," in McHugh's word — within a darkness that is still dark. Westcott's formulation catches the dynamic without turning it into a campaign: "it is of the essence of light to invade the realm of darkness," and the invasion is the light's constant shining within its own field, a verse that presupposes the Fall and holds light and dark together in what he calls the paradox.[^coexistence] Augustine's blind man is the pastoral form of the same point: the sun has not withdrawn from the room.

One clarification, because a reader with the commentators open will want it. Lincoln, at this same verse, writes that "there can be no peaceful coexistence between such darkness and the light, but the darkness is not able to prevail or to extinguish the light of the Word."[^truce] That is not a contradiction of what is claimed here, and the difference is exact. Lincoln is denying a *truce* — a settlement between matched powers, each keeping to its own ground — and this commentary denies one too; the darkness has just been shown to be no one's equal. What is claimed here is not a truce but a *presence*: the light is in the dark place, and goes on shining there, and the dark place is not yet lifted. Coexistence in the locative and temporal sense — the same place, the same hour — not in the diplomatic one. Lincoln's own sentence, moreover, is lodged in his gloss of the *second* clause, where the question is what the darkness does about the light; and there this commentary will meet him.

And the adversative note — the darkness as resistant, the question whether it *masters* the light — is deliberately withheld here. It belongs to the verb of [the last tread](/commentary/john/1-4-to-5/09-soteriology-not-overcome/), and this page holds it there.

The cosmology tread thus lands as one teachable claim, and it is pastorally the sharpest of the four: the light of the world does not shine *at* the darkness from a safe distance but *within* it. The eternal Word enters the very condition of those who do not know him — the moral and noetic dark of a race turned from its Maker — and goes on shining there. It is the seed of the word the Gospel will make explicit: "the light has come into the world, and men loved the darkness rather than the light" (3:19). And it leaves the reader poised on the unit's final and decisive question. The light shines in the dark. What, then, did the dark *do* with it?

The sentence is often met in a modern anthology of patristic comment under an editorial heading that is not Origen's wording; it is given here from the commentary itself.

[^guards]: Bultmann, *John*, Introduction §3, pp. 7–9, on the Gnostic cosmic-dualism frame the evangelist takes up and demythologizes; Dodd, *Interpretation*, pp. 112–113 and 284–285 — the decisive non-identity of John's Logos and light-and-darkness with Philo's and with the Gnostic Redeemer-myth — and, at p. 36, the limit stated outright: "the cosmological dualism of i. 5 is not ultimate"; Andrew T. Lincoln, *The Gospel according to Saint John* (Black's New Testament Commentaries 4; London: Continuum, 2005), p. 99: the light-and-darkness contrast is one familiar from Jewish tradition — "the creation narrative of Gen. 1:3–5," the apocalyptic writings (1 Enoch 89.8), and "in more developed form, in the Qumran literature (cf. e.g. 1QM with its war between the sons of light and the sons of darkness)"; and "in the more developed form of the contrast, light and darkness came to represent the two sides of a modified dualism in which God, on the side of light, is ultimately in control and will triumph in the end. The Fourth Gospel … shares this perspective." Lincoln supplies both the term and the reason it is *modified*: the two sides are not matched, because one of them is God's. Three guards, reached along three different roads, and the convergence is what carries the reading: no one of them is asked to hold it alone.

[^convergence]: McHugh, *John 1–4*, pp. 16–20: the darkness read as a constituent of the chaos of Gen 1:2 brought under God's control and subject to his will, with Ps 139:11–12 and 1QS III behind it — a second Qumran anchor alongside Lincoln's, and one that places the non-ultimacy in the Old Testament's own frame rather than only in the New Testament's. The patristic convergence at 1:5a is at Elowsky, *ACCS John 1–10*, pp. 26–27: Cyril, Augustine, Origen, and Isaac of Nineveh — for whom the God-given dark is trial and never abandonment — all reading the darkness as the moral-anthropological condition the light addresses.

[^locative]: The lexical floor: ἐν with the dative marks position within a region, immersion in a medium, the sphere in which an action holds — not a vector toward a target, which in this Gospel is εἰς with the accusative, John's own choice at 12:46 (ἐγὼ φῶς εἰς τὸν κόσμον ἐλήλυθα). The construction at 1:5a is the same locative form the Prologue uses of the incarnation itself at 1:14, ἐσκήνωσεν ἐν ἡμῖν. The point is not that ἐν *cannot* stand in a context of conflict but that it does not itself supply one; the conflict, where it comes, is supplied by the verb of the next clause.

[^mchughen]: McHugh, *John 1–4*, p. 19: "By writing not ἐπί but ἐν, he stresses that the darkness remains, undispelled by the light" — the alternative he declines being ἐπί "with the accusative, genitive or dative," which would have implied "that the light shone into or over or upon the darkness, thus clearly affirming that the darkness was overwhelmed and dispelled." On the verb: "The light is perpetually shining, just as the darkness always remains," with 1 John 2:8 alongside. McHugh is the primary-text warrant for both halves of this section — the locative force of the preposition and the undispelled darkness that follows from it.

[^adversative]: Murray J. Harris, *John* (Exegetical Guide to the Greek New Testament; Nashville: B&H Academic, 2015), p. 8, with pp. 23–24: "the simple καί, which is sometimes adversative (1:5b, 10c, 11)." Harris locates the adversative force at verse 5b, which is independent grammatical corroboration that the "and yet" of the unit belongs to the last clause and not to the preposition of this one.

[^coexistence]: B. F. Westcott, *The Gospel according to St. John* (London: John Murray, 1882), pp. 4–5: φαίνει present and continuous — "it is of the essence of light to invade the realm of darkness" — the verse presupposing the Fall, and light and darkness held together in coexistence, which Westcott calls the paradox. The "invasion" is read here within the locative frame rather than against it: it names the light's constant shining within its own field, not a directional force imported into ἐν. Augustine's blind man in the sun (Elowsky, *ACCS John 1–10*, pp. 26–27) is the same coexistence stated from the creature's side.

[^truce]: Lincoln, *Saint John*, p. 99, in the sentences that follow his gloss of καταλαμβάνω: "Darkness represents the world in its alienation from the true source of life and remains as part of the narrative world of the Gospel. There can be no peaceful coexistence between such darkness and the light, but the darkness is not able to prevail or to extinguish the light of the Word." The denial is of a settlement between powers, and it is made on the way into the second clause — the clause that closes it being Lincoln's own statement of the outcome, engaged where that outcome is treated; the coexistence this page claims is the light's presence within its field, which Lincoln nowhere denies. The two are compatible, and the distinction is drawn here rather than left for a reader to draw.
